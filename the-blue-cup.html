Saaledes har jeg hørt det, on the Zumbro.

I. The Barn Door

Wanamingo, Goodhue County, Minnesota · June 1888

Everybody in the township knew Tollef, and nobody knew his other name. The Norwegians called him Spedalske-Tollef, Leper Tollef, and the Yankees in Kenyon called him the leper, and the children called him nothing at all, because when they saw him on the road they ran.

He was fifty-eight years old and lived at the county poor farm in a room by himself at the end of the woodshed, with his own bed and his own towel and his own tin plate and cup and spoon, which nobody else touched and which were washed separately in a pan of their own. The state Board of Health had sent the rules, and the poor farm kept them. It was not cruel. They brought his dinner to the door. In winter they kept his stove going. He had come over from Sunnfjord in 1866 with the sickness already in him and his name already in the leprosy register, where the King’s doctors had been writing down every leper in Norway since 1856, and he had worked as a hired man on half the farms between Kenyon and Zumbrota until his hands went. Now the fingers of his left hand were short and curled and the right was not much better, and he kept them wrapped in rags, and his face had gone thick and shiny across the brow and the nose, the way the faces of lepers go.

For twenty years nobody had known. He had been a good hired man, the best in the township at a grain cradle and later at shocking behind the binder, a quiet man who slept in the haymow and sent nothing home because there was nobody at home to send it to. He ate what was put in front of him and did not drink. Then in the harvest of 1884, at the Brekke place, he cut his hand on the knotter of the binder and did not feel it, and kept working, and Mrs. Brekke found the blood on the roller towel by the pump that night and went and looked at his hands while he slept. In the morning Brekke paid him to the day and drove him to Kenyon in the wagon without a word and set him down in front of the hardware store with his bundle. Mrs. Brekke burned the roller towel. By the end of the week it was all over the township, and by the end of the year he was at the poor farm.

He did not blame the Brekkes. He did not have the strength left over for it. He had known since he was twenty-six what was in his hands, and it had only been a matter of time.

That year the doctor from Bergen who had found the leprosy under his microscope came all the way to Red Wing to look at the Norwegians who had it. Tollef was not sent for. The county did not think him worth the train fare.

On a Sunday afternoon in June he walked six miles to Ole Kvale’s barn because the woman preacher was going to speak there.

She was called Gjertrud Hovland, and she was sixty, and a widow, and she had been preaching since she was a girl in Sunnfjord, where her father had been a lay preacher of Hauge’s people in the old days when the law said only the priest could speak about God to more than one family at a time, and the sheriff could take you for it. In America nobody took you for it. But the seminary men in Red Wing did not care to have a woman in the pulpit, and so she preached in barns and schoolhouses and kitchens, from Goodhue County out to the Dakota line, and the barns were full.

Ole Kvale’s barn was full. They had swept the threshing floor and set planks on nail kegs, and the women sat on the planks in their black Sunday dresses with the babies, and the men stood along the walls, and the boys climbed up into the mow and hung their legs over. The big doors were rolled open for the air. Tollef stood outside them, at the very edge, downwind, by the corner of the manure pile, where nobody would mind him and where he could just see her small black shape at the far end, standing on an upturned feed box in front of the grain bins.

She did not have a big voice. You had to be quiet to hear it. And the barn got quiet, and Tollef, outside the door with his rag hands folded in front of him, heard every word.

She read to them from the Revelation, the letter to the church in Smyrna.

I know thy works, and tribulation, and poverty, (but thou art rich) … Fear none of those things which thou shalt suffer: behold, the devil shall cast some of you into prison, that ye may be tried; and ye shall have tribulation ten days: be thou faithful unto death, and I will give thee a crown of life.

— Revelation 2:9–10

Then she talked about it for half an hour in the plain Sunnfjord way, about what it meant to be poor and what it meant to be rich, and most of the people in the barn went home and could not have told you afterward much of what she said. Tollef could not have told you either. He remembered only one part, where she stopped and looked down the length of the barn and said, He does not tell them to cheer up. He tells them he knows. I know thy poverty. He has looked at it, all of it, and he does not look away. And then he says, thou art rich. Today, in Smyrna, in the jail, with the ten days coming. Not some other day. A woman on the end of the nearest plank turned around then and saw Tollef in the door, and moved her baby to her other shoulder, away from him, without thinking, the way you move a baby away from a stove. Something happened to him in the middle of it, standing by the manure pile with the swallows going in and out over his head.

It was like the moment in March when the ice on the Zumbro lets go. All winter it has been one thing, white and shut and silent, and you have walked on it. Then one afternoon you hear it, a deep sound, and the whole river moves at once, and it is water again, and you understand it was water the whole time. Tollef stood there and understood it. Whatever it was he had been waiting for since Sunnfjord, it was not coming, because it had come. It had been there the whole time. He was not going to need anything else.

He stood very still so as not to lose it, and he did not lose it.

II. The Gentleman

The section road · the same afternoon

When the meeting was over he wanted more than anything to go and tell her. But there was no getting through. Everybody wanted to shake her hand, and they were packed in the doorway three deep, and Tollef was not a man who could push into a crowd. People made room for him, but it was the wrong kind of room. So he waited by the manure pile while they sang the last hymn and while they came out blinking into the light, and while they walked her to the Liens’ buggy, and while the buggy went off down the road toward the Lien place with a crowd walking behind it for the first quarter mile. Then the crowd turned back and went home to their chores, and the road was empty, and Tollef started down it after the buggy.

It was the middle of the afternoon and hot. The road ran straight along the section line between wheat on one side, still green, and corn on the other, knee-high. There were meadowlarks on the fence posts. A mile on, where the road dipped toward a creek, there was a gentleman standing in the air above the ditch.

He stood about as high as the top of a hay rack, with nothing under his boots. He wore a good black coat and a white shirt with a collar, and a gold watch chain across his vest, and he was clean in a way nobody in Goodhue County was ever clean in June, and his boots had no dust on them at all. Tollef stopped in the road and looked up at him.

Tollef, the gentleman said. He spoke a good Norwegian, the kind they speak in Christiania. You are a poor man. You are a leper. You live in a woodshed and eat off a tin plate and children run from you on the road. Nobody will sit in a pew with you. You have nothing.

Tollef did not say anything.

I can give you the Brekke place, said the gentleman. A hundred and sixty acres, the frame house, the barn, a team of Percherons. Money in the bank in Red Wing, as much as you want. More. A doctor from Chicago, a doctor from Berlin. A hired girl to cook for you who won’t mind. All you have to do is stand there in the road and say it out loud. Jesus is not the Christ. The Word is not the Word. The congregation is not the congregation. I have had enough of Jesus, and enough of the Word, and enough of the congregation. That’s all. Say it once, and I’ll go away, and it’s all yours.

Who are you? said Tollef.

I am the richest man you will ever meet.

Then Tollef said something nobody in the township would have believed he had in him. He told them about it later in the Liens’ kitchen, exactly as it happened, and Synneve Lien wrote it down that night in the back of her almanac, and that is why we have it.

Then you are a fool, Tollef said, and you have no shame, and you are not fit to talk to me. You stand up there and tell me I am poor. I am not poor. I am the richest man in this county. I have faith. I have kept my life as clean as a hired man can. I have a conscience, and I am afraid to do wrong. I know my catechism; I learned it for my confirmation at Førde, every word of Pontoppidan. What I had, I gave away, when I had it. And this afternoon, by Kvale’s manure pile, I got a little sense. That is seven things. A man who has seven of anything is not poor. Go and find somebody poor.

The gentleman looked at him for a moment from the air. Then he was not there anymore, and Tollef walked on down the road toward the creek.

III. The Cup

The Lien farm · late afternoon

Synneve Lien was in her kitchen baking lefse on the stove top for the preacher’s supper, and the preacher was sitting at the table shelling peas into a bowl, because she could not stand to sit with her hands empty, when a gentleman came to the screen door.

Synneve said afterward that she never heard a wagon. He stood on the step in a black coat and a gold watch chain, with his hat in his hand, and he did not come in, and he spoke past Synneve to Gjertrud Hovland as if they knew each other. He told her all of it. What he had offered and to whom and what the leper had said back. He told it the way a man tells about a horse that would not be sold.

Gjertrud went on shelling peas.

You could stand on air over that ditch till Judgment Day, she said. You could offer him a thousand dollars, or ten thousand, or every acre in Goodhue County with the barns on it. You will not get that man to say it. Not now.

The gentleman put his hat on and went down the step, and Synneve went to the door to see which way he went, and there was nobody in the yard but the dog, who was lying in the shade of the pump with his chin on his paws and had not barked.

Half an hour later Tollef came up the lane.

Synneve saw him from the window and her hand went to her mouth. She had nothing in the house for him. No plate of his own, no cup. She was a good woman and she was sorry for him, but she had four children asleep upstairs and the Board of Health had sent the rules, and she stood there by the stove not knowing what to do.

Gjertrud got up and went out on the step to meet him. She brought him in. She sat him in her own chair at the table and poured coffee into her own cup, the one she had been drinking from, the blue one with the chip, and put it into his rag hands and closed his fingers around it with her fingers. Then she pulled a kitchen chair close and sat down knee to knee with him and said, Now tell me.

He told her. He did not have good words for it, and he said that. He told her about the river ice going out. He said that for thirty-two years, since the day in Sunnfjord when the doctor wrote his name in the book, he had been waiting for something, he did not know what, a cure or a judgment or just the end, and he had carried the waiting around with him everywhere like a sack of seed wheat on his back, to America, to the Brekke place, to the woodshed. He said that in Kvale’s barn door he had found out the sack was empty and had been empty all along, and he had set it down, and he did not think he would pick it up again. Then he told her about the gentleman in the air. She listened to all of it without saying anything, nodding a little, the way you listen to somebody tell you a thing you already know but are glad to hear.

When he was done she said, Yes. That is it. You have it. And she put her hand on his head, on his thin hair, the way her father used to put his hand on people’s heads in Sunnfjord, and held it there a while.

He stayed until the sun was low. Before he went he told her one more thing, quietly, so Synneve at the stove would not hear, but Synneve heard. Then he thanked Synneve for the coffee, though it was not her coffee, and put on his hat and went down the lane toward the road, and Gjertrud stood on the step and watched him go.

IV. The Heifer

The Kvale lane · evening

He took the shortcut home along the lane between Kvale’s pasture and the creek. The sun was going down behind him red and the whole sky was the color of the inside of a shell. There were mosquitoes coming up off the water. He was not in a hurry.

Ole Kvale had a red heifer, a yearling, with a white star on her forehead, that he had bought that spring from a man in Holden who was glad to sell her and did not say why. She had been in the pasture all day. Nobody ever found out how she got through the fence. There was a rail down afterward, at the corner by the creek, but Ole swore it had been sound in the morning.

The Kvale boy found Tollef in the lane at five the next morning when he went out to bring in the cows. He was lying on his back in the grass at the edge of the lane with his hat a little way off, and the ground around him was cut up with hoof marks, and his chest was broken in, the way a cow will do when she goes down on her knees on a man. His face was not hurt. The boy said it looked like he was asleep in church.

The heifer was standing in the creek a little way down, up to her knees in the water, drinking.

•••

The old women in the township had a story about that heifer, which they told later, after it was over, and only to each other.

They said that long ago in Sunnfjord, in the old days, four farmers’ sons went up to the summer pastures where a dairymaid was alone with the cows, and stayed the night with her, and in the morning they talked it over among themselves and decided to take back the silver they had given her and her own silver brooch besides, and leave no one to tell. She heard them. As they were killing her she swore she would come back for them, every one, if she had to come back as a beast to do it, and she has been coming back ever since. That is why she has a cow’s shape, the old women said, the way the huldra in the old stories hides a cow’s tail under her skirts. They said she had found two of the four already, one in Norway and one somewhere in the south, a long time ago. They said Tollef was the third. They said there was one more.

They did not say how they knew. Ole Kvale sold the heifer that fall to a cattle buyer from St. Paul, and nobody in the township ever saw her again.

V. The Bitter Fruit

The poor farm burying ground · three days later

They buried him at the poor farm, in the corner of the field where the county put its own, because the churches did not want a leper in their churchyards, and no pastor came. Gjertrud Hovland came. She had stayed on in the township when she heard. A good many people came with her who would not have crossed the road to Tollef while he lived, and stood around the grave in the wind with their hats in their hands, and some of them were ashamed and some were only curious.

When the box was down Gjertrud said a few words and they sang a verse of a hymn. Then nobody went home. They stood around her in the stubble and asked her things, the way people in the barn had wanted to ask.

Where is he now? said Ole Kvale, who was ashamed. That was my heifer. Where did he go?

He went home, said Gjertrud. He was rich. You heard what he told the gentleman. Whatever was waiting for him, he was ready for it.

Then why was he a leper? said somebody else, a hard old farmer from Holden who did not hold with women preachers. If he was such a man. Why did God give him that?

Gjertrud stood a while before she answered. The wind went over the stubble.

He told me something, she said, on Sunday, at the end, at the door. He told me that when he was a boy of eight in Sunnfjord, in 1838, the sheriff came to take a lay preacher before the bailiff, for preaching without the priest. They led him down the road past the farms with his hands tied, and all the boys ran along beside, and Tollef ran up and spat on him. On his coat. His mother saw it and said God would remember that. Tollef said he thought about it every day of his life, and when the sickness came he knew why. He believed that. He wanted me to know it before he went.

Nobody said anything.

I was there, said Gjertrud Hovland. It was my father. I was nine years old and I was running along behind the sheriff’s cart, and I saw a boy spit on my father’s coat, and I saw my father look down at him and say, God bless you, boy. I did not know until Sunday who it was.

Then it is true, said the farmer from Holden. It was a judgment.

I don’t keep God’s books, said Gjertrud. I know a boy did a hard thing, and a man carried something for fifty years, and on Sunday he set it down. That’s what I know. She looked at the farmer from Holden, and at all of them, the ones who had run from Tollef on the road and the ones who had sent their children running. And I know what Solomon says about the rest of us.

Therefore shall they eat of the fruit of their own way, and be filled with their own devices. For the turning away of the simple shall slay them, and the prosperity of fools shall destroy them.

— Proverbs 1:31–32

Then she put on her bonnet and went back to the Liens’ buggy, and the people stood around the grave a while longer in the wind, not looking at one another, and went home.

•••

That evening the poor farm did what the Board of Health said to do. They carried Tollef’s bed and his blanket and his towel out behind the woodshed and burned them, and his tin plate and his tin cup and his spoon they threw into the fire too, and scrubbed the room with lye.

At the Lien place, Synneve took the blue cup with the chip down from the shelf to break it and bury the pieces, because she had four children and she did not know what else to do. Gjertrud took it out of her hands. She washed it at the pump. Then she filled it with coffee from the pot on the back of the stove and sat down at the kitchen table and drank it, all of it, slowly, while Synneve watched, and set it down empty on the oilcloth between them.

Background

The verse

Fools of little understanding have themselves for their greatest enemies, for they do evil deeds which must bear bitter fruits.Dhammapada 66, tr. F. Max Müller (1881)

In the commentary, the leper Suppabuddha sits at the outer edge of the crowd in Räjagaha, hears the Buddha teach, and is transformed. Too humble to push through the crowd, he waits until it has gone. On the road, Sakka, lord of the gods, appears in the air and tests him, promising him limitless wealth if he will renounce the Buddha, the Dhamma and the Sangha. Suppabuddha calls him a shameless fool. He is not poor, he says, because he has the seven treasures: faith, virtue, conscience, concern, learning, giving and wisdom. Sakka reports this to the Buddha, who says that no amount of money could make the leper say it. Suppabuddha visits the Buddha, is warmly received, and on his way home is killed by a young cow. The cow is a vengeful spirit, once a courtesan murdered by four young men, who has sworn to kill all four. When the monks ask about his fate, the Buddha tells them he has been reborn among the gods, and that he was a leper because in a past life he spat on a holy man. Goodhue County, 1888

In the mid-nineteenth century Norway had more leprosy than any other country in Europe, most of it in poor fishing and farming communities on the west coast. In 1856 it set up the world’s first national leprosy register, and isolation laws followed in 1877 and 1885. In 1873 the Bergen physician Gerhard Armauer Hansen identified the bacterium that causes the disease. Some Norwegians who had it emigrated to the Upper Midwest, and before 1890 about fifty cases were reported in Minnesota. Most patients lived with their families or on county poor farms, in rooms of their own, with their own bedding and dishes. In 1888 Hansen himself came to Minnesota and spent several weeks in Red Wing, the Goodhue County seat, studying the immigrant cases (MNopedia).

Many of those immigrants brought the revival of Hans Nielsen Hauge with them. Hauge was a lay preacher who was jailed fourteen times under the Conventicle Act, which forbade religious meetings without a parish priest until Norway repealed it in 1842. Women preached in his movement from its earliest years. In America his followers formed the Hauge Synod, a low-church body that opened its seminary in Red Wing in 1879. The old women’s cow-spirit borrows from the huldra of Scandinavian folklore, a beautiful forest woman who hides a cow’s tail under her skirts.
