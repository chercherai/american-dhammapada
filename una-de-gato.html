Uña de Gato

— Isaias 35:1

Así lo oí contar, at the ford of El Paso del Norte.

I. The Grandmother

San José del Parral, Nueva Vizcaya · spring 1672

Doña Leonor de Arteaga was the widow of a silver miner, and she had seven children, and the Church had taken six. Her eldest, Diego, who should have had the mines, gave his share to the poor in the plaza of Parral on a single morning and walked north to New Mexico in the gray habit of Saint Francis, and in New Mexico they called him Fray Diego de San Antonio and had forgotten he had ever had another name. Her three daughters were in the convent of Jesús María in Mexico City behind a grille. Her second son was a Jesuit in Durango, and her third was a Franciscan somewhere in Sonora that she could not find on a map.

That left Rodrigo, who was seven.

If this one goes, she told her confessor, the mines go to the Crown and my husband's name goes into the ground with him. I will tie him to the house while he is still small enough to tie.

The Church allowed a betrothal from the age of seven. Doña Leonor found a girl of nine from a family with a hacienda of sheep on the Florido, and arranged the promise, and on the day of the espousals she dressed her son in black velvet with a lace collar and a little sword and a gold chain across his chest, and put him in the coach with six mules, and took him with half of Parral behind them to the house of the bride.

The kin of both houses stood in the sala. They dipped their fingers in a silver basin of water and blessed the children. Then the bride's aunts turned her by the shoulders toward a chair by the window and said to her, laughing, Look, there is your great-grandmother. May you live as long as she has.

Rodrigo looked where they were looking. Which one is her great-grandmother?

Why, there, young sir, in the chair. Doña Mencía.

She was said to be a hundred and four. She had come up from Zacatecas in an oxcart when there was nothing at Santa Bárbara but the first mines. She had two teeth. Her hair was thin and white as raw cotton, and her face was all folds, and her hands lay in her lap spotted like a quail's egg, and her back was bent like a beam under a load of roof.

Will Juana look like that?

The aunts laughed again. If God gives her the years.

Rodrigo looked at the girl in her stiff yellow silk, who was picking at the sugar on a little cake, and then at the old woman, and then at the girl again. So that is what Diego saw, he thought. That is why he went. And he decided, standing there in his velvet with the gold chain on his chest, that he would go that same day.

II. The Wagon Camp

The road outside Parral · the same day

On the way home in the coach he said he needed to make water. The coachman stopped the mules, and he got down and went behind a mesquite and came back. A little farther on he said it again, and got down, and came back. The third time his mother's cousin sighed and said that the boy had eaten too much at the espousals, and nobody watched him anymore. The fourth time he said, Go on ahead, I'll walk behind, and they went on ahead.

Outside Parral, where the road came down from the hills, a train of wagons was camped for the night on its way north to New Mexico, with friars going up to the missions and a few soldiers for escort, and the mules and oxen grazing around them on what grass there was. Rodrigo walked into the firelight in his velvet and his lace and his gold chain, and knelt to the first friar he saw, and asked to be given the habit.

There were thirty of them in that camp, more or less. They looked at him.

Child, you are dressed like a viceroy's son. We don't know whose son you are. How can we give you the habit?

Don't you know me? I'm Diego de Arteaga's brother.

Who is Diego de Arteaga?

Rodrigo thought. You call him Fray Diego de San Antonio.

The friars looked at each other, and then all of them began to talk at once. Fray Diego, before he went north the last time, had said it to every friar of the Custody he met. If my little brother ever comes to you asking for the habit, give it to him that same hour. Don't send to my mother to ask. She will say no. I am his father and his mother now.

So they took off the gold chain and the little sword and the lace, and sent them back to Parral by a muleteer with a letter, and the friar who mended their sandals cut down a gray habit to the boy's size by the fire, and they tied the cord around him three times because there was so little of him. A letter went north to Santa Fe the next morning with a soldier riding ahead of the train.

When it came, Fray Diego took it to the old friar, Fray Gil de la Concepción, who had been born in New Mexico, at San Gabriel, in the first years of the colony, and had never once seen Spain. Fray Diego asked leave to ride south and meet the train.

Stay here for now, Fray Gil said.

A week later Fray Diego asked again.

Stay here for now, Diego. We will go together, later.

Meanwhile the train went north, slowly, and the boy walked beside the wagons in his cut-down habit. At El Paso del Norte, where the road forded the river, he heard that two of his mother's men had come up behind the train asking for him. So he went to the oldest friar in the train and asked him for something to pray that would take him all the way, to the end, and the old man gave it to him, a few words to say and a way to sit. When the train went on north the boy stayed behind at the bottom of the Jornada del Muerto, where the river turned away into the mountains and the road went straight out across the dry plain, and walked off the road a league toward the river into the uña de gato, the catclaw, where nobody lived and nobody would come after him, and sat down.

III. The Catclaw

The bench above the river, south end of the Jornada · summer 1672

The catclaw grew on a gravel bench above the river in a thicket a man could not walk through standing. Every branch was set with small hooked thorns that took your sleeve and would not let it go, so that you had to stop, and back up, and unhook yourself one thorn at a time. Wait a minute, the thorns said to anyone who came in. The soldiers had worse names for it.

It was a drought year, in a run of them. Up the river the pueblos had little in their granaries, and a friar had written to Mexico that Spaniards and Indians alike were eating hides and the straps of the carts. The Apaches came down on the horse herds when they were hungry, which was always. The boy lived in the catclaw on what the bench gave him, mesquite beans and the water of the river, and said his few words, and sat.

In July the rains came up out of the south in the afternoons, black clouds with the lightning walking under them, and the catclaw put out its flowers, little pale yellow spikes like cattails that smelled of honey from a hundred yards off. Before the rains were over, before the cottonwoods along the river had begun to turn, the boy had come to the end of what the old friar gave him. He did not tell anybody. There was nobody to tell.

IV. The Dead Man's Road

Fray Cristóbal, north end of the Jornada · October 1672

In October, when the rains were done, Fray Diego asked a third time, and Fray Gil said, Now we will go. They went down the river from Santa Fe with a good many friars and lay brothers, and soldiers, and settlers going south, and herds, and the wagons that were going back down to Mexico empty, more than five hundred souls all told by the time they gathered at Fray Cristóbal, where the road leaves the river.

Fray Lucas, who was young and kept the old friar's papers and walked at his elbow, stood with him where the road forked.

Father, there are two ways to where the boy is. One is the river way, around through the mountains. It is long, but there is water every day, and there are people. The other is the Jornada, straight across. It is ninety miles and half as long, and there is no water on it, and the Apaches watch it, and since they found the German's bones out there two years ago the soldiers say it is not a place for Christians at night. Which shall we take?

Is Fray Silvestre with us?

Yes, Father.

If Silvestre is with us, take the Jornada.

Fray Silvestre was a lay brother from Zacatecas, a big soft-voiced man who looked after the mules, and he had never in his life gone without anything. It was a thing everyone in the Custody knew. If a ranchero killed a sheep, a leg of it found its way to Silvestre. If a governor's wife had chocolate sent up from Mexico, a cake of it ended in Silvestre's sleeve. In the hungry years Indians in the pueblos who had nothing gave him something anyway, a handful of piñons, a twist of chile, and went away looking pleased. He gave all of it away again as fast as it came. He could not help any of it, and he was a little embarrassed by it, as a man is by a wart.

They took the Jornada. What happened on it is told in the Custody to this day, and the soldiers who were there told it the same way as the friars, which was unusual.

At every league along the dead man's road, where there was no water and never had been, they found a ramada of fresh brush standing by the trail, with its shade swept, and under it big clay jars of water, sweating and cold, and baskets of tortillas still warm in their cloths, and dried meat and piñons and squash, and nobody there. No tracks went to them or away. The soldiers looked for tracks. In the mornings, before light, the friars at the head of the column heard voices ahead of them in the dark asking, in a Spanish that was a little strange, the way a song is strange, Where is Brother Silvestre? Which one is Brother Silvestre? And Silvestre, red to the ears, would go out ahead with his lantern and come back with his arms full, and bring all of it to Fray Gil, and Fray Gil had it shared out among the column, friars and soldiers and settlers and the Manso boys who drove the herds, and there was more than enough. They went the ninety miles of the Jornada in short stages, a league a day and no more, and nobody was thirsty once. They said afterward that the whole Custody had crossed the dead man's road on the luck of one lay brother.

Down at the bottom of it, in the catclaw, the boy knew they were coming.

V. The Convento

The bench above the river · October and November 1672

When they came off the Jornada at the south end and turned toward the river, the catclaw bench was a convento.

That is how it is told. There was a church of white stone with a bell in its wall, and a cloister around a garden with a fountain running in it, and a cell for Fray Gil with a bed and a writing table and a window toward the river that smelled of something like incense, and five hundred cells for the rest of them, every one with its door and its cot, and long covered walks for the hot hours and the night, and a refectory with a table that ran the length of it. The boy stood at the gate in his cut-down habit with the cord wrapped around him three times, and knelt to Fray Gil, and then to his brother. Fray Diego picked him up off the ground and held him and could not say anything at all.

They stayed a month. Every morning there were voices on the road asking for Brother Silvestre, and every morning Silvestre came back with his arms full.

There were two old friars in the company, Fray Hernando and Fray Blas, who had been forty years at missions where the church was mud and the friars ate what the pueblo ate, and they did not like what they saw. They said it to each other in the covered walk, in low voices.

When does a child pray, with all this building to see to?

And the old man comes ninety miles through the Jornada to stay a month in it. Because the child is Diego de Arteaga's brother. Because the family has silver. That is all this is.

Fray Gil, walking in the garden early on the first morning, saw them talking and knew what they were saying. On the day the company left, when he had gone out of the gate a little way, he stopped on the road and stood there with his eyes closed for about as long as it takes to say a Credo.

Fray Hernando put his hand to his belt. My breviary. I left it on the cot. Fray Blas looked at his feet and found he had come out in his old worn sandals and left the good ones under the bed, and his water gourd too. The two of them turned back.

There was no convento. There was the gravel bench above the river and the catclaw on it, gray and hooked, so thick a man could not walk through it standing. They went in after their things anyway, because the breviary had been Fray Hernando's since his ordination, and the catclaw took their sleeves and their hoods and the skin of their hands, and the thorns went into their feet through the old sandals, and they went in and out among the bushes all morning and could not find any place that looked like a place where anything had been. At last they found the breviary and the good sandals and the gourd hanging together on a branch of catclaw at the height of a man's head, hooked there neatly by their straps, as if somebody had hung them up for them to see. They unhooked them one thorn at a time and walked back up to the road bleeding, and caught up with the company by evening.

Fray Gil stayed on the river another month after that, at the friars' camp by the ford, and then went home up the river to Santa Fe.

VI. The Widow's Table

Santa Fe · winter 1673

Doña Beatriz de Anaya was the richest widow in New Mexico, as riches went there, and she gave to the friars more than anyone. Her husband's tribute came in from three pueblos in corn and blankets, in good years and in bad, and she knew where it came from, and she gave as if she knew. Any friar passing through Santa Fe who came to her door was fed in her kitchen, and there was chocolate for him if there was chocolate in the town.

Fray Hernando and Fray Blas came the first morning after they got back, with their feet still bound in rags, and sat in her kitchen and drank atole.

Fathers, you went with Fray Gil to see Fray Diego's little brother?

We did, señora.

They tell me it is a beautiful place where the child lives.

Fray Hernando put his cup down. Beautiful? Señora, it is a thicket of uña de gato on a gravel bank. You cannot walk in it. It is fit for hermits and javelinas, and not many javelinas. And he showed her his hands.

That same morning, a little later, two young friars came to her door who had also been with the company. One of them was Fray Lucas. She sat them down and gave them atole and asked them the same.

Señora, I can't tell you what it was like, Fray Lucas said. There are no words for it in Spanish. It was like the cloister of San Francisco in Mexico, only finer. It was like what the Apocalypse says about the city. I slept a month in a cell there and never wanted for anything.

Doña Beatriz sat a while with her hands in her lap after they had gone. The old ones said one thing and the young ones said another, and she did not think any of the four of them was lying. She thought she could see how it might be. She did not say it. She said, I will ask Fray Gil, and waited.

He came to her table on the feast of the Epiphany with Fray Diego and a dozen others. When she had served them and they had eaten, she knelt by his chair and asked him plainly. Father, some of the ones who went with you say the boy lives in a thorn thicket, and some say he lives in a palace. Which is it?

Fray Gil was very old by then, and he did not talk much, and when he did talk it was often in Latin, which he had learned before he learned to read Spanish, from the friars at Santo Domingo. He said a line of it to her.

Laetabitur deserta et invia et exultabit solitudo et florebit quasi lilium.

Then, because she was waiting, he said it again in her own language, and said he did not mean only the boy's bench. He meant a pueblo or a plaza or the open desert, the bottom of a river or the top of a mesa. Wherever people like that boy lived, he said, the ground was glad of it.

The land that was desolate and impassable shall be glad, and the wilderness shall rejoice, and shall flourish like the lily.

— Isaias 35:1

Doña Beatriz nodded as though he had told her where a thing she had lost was. Fray Hernando, who was at the table too, with his feet in clean bandages, looked at his hands for a long time.

VII. The Honey

The convento at Santa Fe · Lent 1673

That winter, at the convento in Santa Fe, the friars talked at recreation about Fray Silvestre. Why was it that a lay brother who could barely read was never once in want, when governors went hungry? And why, Silvestre's own mother had told everyone in Zacatecas, had she labored seven days to bear him, with the child lying crosswise in her, so that the women gave her up twice?

Fray Gil, who heard most things, asked what they were talking about, and they told him. He said there was a tale the old people told in the Rio Abajo, which they had from the first friars, who had it from Spain, the way such tales go. He told it to them the way the friars told exempla in a sermon, and they listened the way friars listen to a sermon at recreation, half smiling.

Once, he said, a king and the council of his town fell to rivalry over which of them could give the greater feast to a holy man who had come to them with his followers. The king gave a feast, and the town gave a greater one, and the king a greater, back and forth, six times. The seventh time the town swore there would be nothing lacking from its table, and they looked it over, and saw there was cooked honey but no fresh honey in the comb. So they sent men out of all four gates with a thousand coins each to find some.

On the road a countryman was walking into town with a branch over his shoulder with a comb of wild honey on it, which he had cut from a tree by the road, to give to his village alcalde. One of the town's men stopped him and asked if the honey was for sale. No, said the countryman. Take a coin for it anyway, the man said. The countryman thought: this comb is not worth a copper, and he is offering a coin for it, so he must have a good many coins. He said, not for one. Two, then, said the man. Not for two. And so up, and up, until the man offered him the whole thousand, and he took it, and then stood in the road with the purse in one hand and the honey in the other and said: Are you mad? This is not worth a copper. What do you want it for?

The man told him. It was for the holy man's table, and there was no fresh honey in all that feast. The countryman put the purse back in his hands. If it is for that, he said, I won't sell it for any price. Let me give it, and have the good of the giving. The town agreed. They strained the comb into a great silver basin, and the countryman went home and came back with a pot of clabbered milk of his own and stirred it in, and they set it before the holy man and his followers, and there were many hundreds of them, and every one ate as much as he wanted, and there was some left.

A friar asked how one comb fed so many.

Don't ask that, said Fray Gil. There are four things a man can go mad thinking about, and how God feeds people is one of them.

That same countryman, he went on, a long time later, was made a captain, and was sent to take a town. He sat down in front of its four great gates for seven years and seven months, and the town did not fall, because the people went in and out by the little gates in the walls for wood and water. His mother heard of it and sent to him. My son is a fool, she said. Tell him to shut the little gates. He did, and the town could not get out for wood or water, and on the seventh day it gave itself up. And for the honey, said Fray Gil, he was never in want, in this world or the next. And for the little gates, he was a long time getting out of where he went, and a long time coming in, wherever he came in.

Was that Brother Silvestre, Father? one of the young friars asked, grinning.

Fray Gil smiled a little and did not answer, and somebody laughed, and the bell rang for Compline.

Another evening that Lent they talked about the boy. Think of the merit, they said, a child who could raise a convento with five hundred cells out of a thorn bush for the old man to sleep in. Think what he must have laid up in heaven by now, at eight years old.

Fray Gil came in from the cold with his hands in his sleeves and heard it.

That child is not keeping any account, he said. Not of the good and not of the other. He let both go, down there in the uña de gato. If you asked him, he would tell you what the servants say in Saint Luke.

So you also, when you shall have done all these things that are commanded you, say: We are unprofitable servants; we have done that which we ought to do.

— Luke 17:10

Down on the bench above the river, in March, before anything else on the desert had leaves, the catclaw put out new shoots, red-brown and soft, with the thorns on them still green and bending, not yet hooked. A little gray habit hung on one branch to dry, with its cord coiled under it on the gravel, three turns.

In the commentary, Sāriputta and five of his brothers and sisters have all become monastics, and their mother marries the youngest, Revata, at seven to keep him at home. At the wedding the kin bless the bride to live as long as her grandmother, a woman of a hundred and twenty, toothless and bent; Revata sees what his brother saw, slips away from the carriage on the pretext of relieving himself, and asks some forest monks for ordination. They do not know who “Upatissa” is until he gives his brother's monastic name, and they ordain him on his brother's standing instruction. He goes off to an acacia forest and becomes an arahant within the rains. The Buddha, after twice telling Sāriputta to wait, sets out with five hundred monks by the short road through haunted country because the elder Sīvali, the disciple to whom gifts always come, is with them, and spirits feed the whole company at every league. Revata turns the thorn forest into a monastery by psychic power. Two old monks who grumble about favoritism go back for forgotten things and find only acacia thorns, with their belongings hanging on a branch. When they and two young monks give the lay patron Visākhā opposite reports, the Buddha speaks verse 98. The commentary then tells Sīvali's past: a countryman who would not sell his honeycomb for a thousand coins but gave it to a Buddha's feast, and who in a later life, on his mother's advice, starved a besieged city by shutting its little gates. Nueva Vizcaya and New Mexico, 1672

New Mexico's Franciscan missions depended on supply caravans up the Camino Real from Mexico City, which by royal order left every three years and took about six months each way. The road passed through the silver country of Nueva Vizcaya, where Parral had boomed since the strike of 1631. North of El Paso del Norte the road left the Rio Grande and crossed some ninety miles of almost waterless plain, a route Juan de Oñate chose because it was shorter and easier for ox carts and livestock than the river. It came to be called the Jornada del Muerto after the bones of a German were found along it in 1670. Under canon law a betrothal could be contracted once both children were older than seven.

In the 1670s drought and famine struck the province, Spaniards and Pueblo people alike were reduced to eating hides, and Apache raids increased. The missions rested on Pueblo tribute and labor, and the friars' campaign against Pueblo religion grew harsher; in August 1680 the Pueblos rose together and drove the Spanish out of New Mexico for twelve years (Pueblo Revolt). The catclaw acacia, common in southern New Mexico and Chihuahua, is also called wait-a-minute bush, because a person caught in its hooked prickles has to stop and free himself.
