Walking the American WayCommentary 24.11 · Aputtaka

Laurel Hell

Thou fool, this night thy soul shall be required of thee: then whose shall those things be, which thou hast provided?
— Luke 12:20

Here’s how I heard tell of it, at the mouth of Bearpen.

I. The Jars

Bearpen Fork of the North Fork of the Kentucky River · August 1899

Jehu Carnes was found dead on the fourth of July, sitting at his own table with his spoon in his hand and a bowl of mush in front of him gone hard as plaster. He was seventy-nine years old. He had lived alone at the head of Bearpen for fifteen years, since his wife died, and before that he had lived there with her and no children for twenty-seven. He had no brother living and no sister and no nephew and no niece, and when the circuit clerk went through the books at the county seat to see who his heirs might be, there was not one, not a cousin, not anybody, back to the Revolution.

They buried him on the point above the house beside his wife. The preacher, an Old Regular Baptist from over on Carr Fork who had known him sixty years, stood at the head of the grave with his hat in his hand and lined out a hymn, and the few who came sang it back to him the long slow way, and afterward the preacher opened his Bible to the twelfth of Luke and read one verse and closed it again.

But God said unto him, Thou fool, this night thy soul shall be required of thee: then whose shall those things be, which thou hast provided?

— Luke 12:20

Nobody on Bearpen thought that was unkind. They knew what he meant. Everybody on the creek had always known Jehu Carnes was rich. They just did not know how rich, and they had never seen a dollar of it.

In August the escheater came up the creek. He was a lawyer from the county seat named Cyrus Webb, a careful heavy man in a linen duster, and the Auditor of Public Accounts in Frankfort had appointed him to look after property in that county that fell to the Commonwealth when a man died and left no one to have it. He had a paper from the circuit court and two deputies and a wagon, and he had three men from the creek hired at a dollar a day with mattocks and spades.

It took them seven days.

They found the first jar under the hearthstone, a half-gallon fruit jar with a zinc lid, packed so full of gold that the coins did not rattle when they shook it. Twenty-dollar pieces, double eagles, with the eagle on the back. They found two more under the puncheons of the floor. They found one in the springhouse wall behind a loose rock, and one in the smokehouse in the ash pit, and one buried in the hog lot so deep the man with the mattock nearly quit. They found three in a hollow sycamore down by the creek, stuffed up inside it with rags, and one under each of the four bee gums, and the bees came out and stung everybody. They found greenbacks rolled in oilcloth in the rafters, gone soft and brown, and silver dollars in a lard can in the corn crib, and a leather poke of gold under the shuck mattress he died sleeping on.

Every evening Mr. Webb counted it at the dead man's table by a coal-oil lamp, with a deputy at the door with a shotgun, and wrote it in a ledger. On the seventh evening he added the columns twice and sat back and looked at the number for a long time. It came to forty-one thousand, three hundred and sixty dollars. There was not that much money, he thought, in every bank between there and Beattyville put together.

They hauled it down the creek in the wagon with the deputies walking on either side, and the whole creek came out on the porches to watch it go by.

II. How He Lived

The head of Bearpen

Mr. Webb had been asking about him all week while the men dug, and everybody on the creek had something to tell.

He had owned the best land on Bearpen, nine hundred acres of it, bottom and hillside and the high woods, and the biggest poplar timber anybody had ever seen. For forty years he had cut it and snaked it down to the creek with oxen, and every spring, when the snows melted and the log tide came, he had sent it down the North Fork in rafts, to Beattyville and on down the river to the sawmills at Frankfort, and come home with money. In the nineties a man from Pittsburgh had come through buying up the coal under people's land for next to nothing, and Jehu Carnes was the only one on the creek who made him pay ten times what the others got, and in gold. The tides had always come high for him. Other men's rafts broke up on the shoals and his came through. Other men's sons drowned breaking jams and his hired hands came home. Whatever he touched made money. He had never seemed to know what to do with it except dig a hole.

He lived in the same two-room log house he had built in 1846. He wore tow linen, the coarse stuff spun from flax waste that people had stopped wearing after the war, and in winter a coat made of a feed sack with holes cut for his arms. He ate mush made of cornmeal sweepings, the bran and grit from the bottom of the mill bin that the miller gave away for hogs, and he drank clabber gone sour, and that was all he ate, morning and night, for as long as anybody could remember. He drove a sled behind a blind mule because a wagon cost money. When it rained he held a leaf over his head off the umbrella tree that grew by his springhouse, a leaf as long as your arm, and walked to the store at the mouth of the creek that way, with the water running off the end of it.

The widow Hensley, who had kept house for him two days a week the last years, told Mr. Webb about the chicken. Once, the first year, feeling sorry for him, she had fried a chicken and made biscuits and gravy and brought it to his table on her own good platter, the blue one. He had looked at the platter a long time. Then he stood up and said, So that's what folks eat, is it. You come in my own house to make sport of me. And he had run her out the door and down the path to the creek, throwing rocks and clods of dirt and the biscuits after her, and stood in his door when she was gone and said, to nobody, Mush is what a man ought to eat.

The storekeeper told the same thing about a coat. A drummer from Cincinnati had come through with good wool coats and Jehu had stood and looked at one for most of an hour and then thrown it in the road and walked off under his leaf. The miller told it about a buggy. It was always the same. He would look at the good thing for a long time, the way a hungry dog looks at a man with a stick, and then he would drive it off.

Mr. Webb wrote it all down. He had been a lawyer for twenty-two years and seen a great many kinds of men, and he did not understand this one. At noon on the eighth day, with the money gone down to the bank at the county seat and nothing left to do on Bearpen but sign his name, he walked down to the schoolhouse at the mouth of the creek to see Miss Orpha Day.

III. Noon

The Bearpen school · the dinner hour

The Bearpen school was a log house with one door and one window and a stick-and-mud chimney, and a stove that smoked, and split-log benches with peg legs, and forty-three scholars in it from six years old to nineteen, sitting by size. The common school ran in the summer and fall in that country, from July until the roads got too bad to walk, and in the middle of the day the children took their dinner buckets out under the beech trees and ate cold cornbread and beans and played Antny-over across the roof.

Miss Orpha Day had taught it for forty years. She was sixty years old and had never married, and she had been to every meeting on the creek in her life and never once gone up to be saved, which the preachers held against her and the mothers did not. She was a thin straight woman with grey hair pulled back hard and spectacles she had bought in Lexington the one time she went there. Everybody on Bearpen under the age of fifty had learned their letters from her, and some of their mothers had too. People went to her about things they did not take to the preacher or the doctor or the law. It was said on the creek that Miss Orpha knew things. Nobody knew how.

She was sitting on the doorsill with her own dinner bucket when Mr. Webb came down the path in his linen duster, mopping his neck, and she moved over and let him sit on the step below her.

Well, Mr. Webb, she said. What are you doing out in the heat of the day?

He told her. He told her about the jars and the bee gums and the sycamore, and the forty-one thousand dollars, and the mush and the clabber and the tow shirt and the leaf, and the widow Hensley running down the path with biscuits hitting her in the back.

I can't make it out, he said. A man with that kind of money, eating hog feed and carrying a leaf for an umbrella. He could have had anything. Why would a man do that?

Miss Orpha set her dinner bucket down on the sill beside her and looked out at the children under the beeches for a while.

I'll tell you, she said. It goes back a ways.

IV. The Walking Man

Bearpen · 1857

When Miss Orpha was a girl there was a man who walked the mountains. Everybody from the Big Sandy to the Cumberland knew him, and nobody knew where he came from. People called him Old Tagg. He did not preach and he did not peddle and he did not beg. He walked the ridges with a stick, winter and summer, in a long coat, and slept in barns and corn cribs where people let him, and if somebody set food in front of him he ate it and said Thank ye and walked on. Those were nearly the only words anybody ever heard him say. Children followed him a mile down the road just to be near him, and he let them. Dogs that bit everybody else lay down in the road when he passed. Old people said a house he had eaten in did well. Some said he had been a preacher once and lost his voice and found something better. Some said he was the Wandering Jew. Miss Orpha said, sitting on the sill, that she had seen him twice as a girl and had never in her life seen anybody look so much as if he had come to the end of something.

In the fall of 1857 Old Tagg came up Bearpen and stopped at the Carnes place at dinnertime.

Jehu was thirty-seven then and newly married to Ruth Fields from over on Rockhouse, who was a good woman, everybody said so, a churchgoing woman who gave to anybody. Jehu was already close. He saw the old man standing at the gate and got up from the table and said over his shoulder to his wife, Give the old man something, and went out to the field.

Ruth Carnes stood in her kitchen and thought she had not heard that word in that man's mouth since the day she married him. Give. And she thought, Today I am going to give, the way I have wanted to. She brought Old Tagg into the house and sat him at the table and set before him the best of everything she had. She cut the ham that was meant for Christmas. She made biscuits with the white flour that came from the store. She opened the honey and the preserves and poured him sweet milk, and she fried a chicken, and she wrapped what he could not eat in a cloth for him to carry.

Jehu met him on the path going down, with the bundle. Did you get anything, old man? And he took the cloth and opened it and looked. There was the ham, and the white biscuits, and the chicken.

Miss Orpha said it was not that he took it back. He did not take it back. He tied the cloth up again and handed it to the old man, and Old Tagg said Thank ye and went on down the creek. But Jehu stood in the path and said, out loud, so that the boy who was hoeing for him that summer heard it and told it for the rest of his life: That ought to have gone to my hands. A man that eats like that will work for you. That old man will eat it and lay down in somebody's barn and sleep. It's throwed away.

Now listen to me, Miss Orpha said to Mr. Webb. That dinner made him rich. Old folks always said a house Old Tagg ate in did well, and that house did better than any on the creek. The tides came high for him from that year on. You've seen what it came to. But what he said in the path, that it was throwed away, that went into him too. From that day he never could stand to see a good thing used. Not by anybody. Not by himself. He'd look at a chicken on a platter and see it throwed away. He had the money and he had the begrudging, both, and they came in the same door the same day.

V. Sang

The high woods above Bearpen · September 1859

Jehu had a younger brother named Elisha who owned the other half of the Carnes land, the lower half, the wide bottom at the forks and the best of the poplar. In the spring of 1859 Elisha drowned on the North Fork breaking a log jam at the head of a tide, and his wife had died two winters before of the lung fever, and he left one child, a boy named Davy, six years old.

The boy came to live with his uncle Jehu and aunt Ruth, and the circuit court made Jehu his guardian, and the land went into Jehu's keeping until the boy was grown.

Miss Orpha knew Davy Carnes. It was her first school, the summer she was twenty, in a smaller house than this one, and he was the youngest scholar in it, a towheaded boy who could already say the alphabet backwards and was proud of it. She said he was a talker. He went everywhere holding his uncle's finger, because his uncle was the biggest thing he had left, and as they went he talked. That's my daddy's mule. That's my daddy's bottom. Them's my daddy's poplars, Uncle Jehu, ain't they. When I'm big I'm going to raft them to Frankfort.

Jehu heard it every day. And he thought, Miss Orpha said: He talks like that now. When he's grown, what will there be left of mine?

In September, when the sang berries turn red and the whole creek goes up into the high woods to dig ginseng to sell to the buyers for the China trade, Jehu took Davy sang hunting. They went up past the last cleared ground into the coves under the ridge where the big laurel grows, the thickets so dense and so tangled and so high over your head that a man can get into them and never find his way out. Hunters had died in them. The mountain people called those places laurel hells. Jehu took the boy to the edge of one, and there, under a laurel bush, he took him by the neck and killed him. Miss Orpha said it the way she said everything, plainly, looking out at the beeches. He broke him the way you would snap a radish. Then he threw the body as far as he could into the laurel, and walked home, and told Ruth the boy had wandered off from him digging sang and he could not find him.

The whole creek hunted for Davy Carnes for three weeks. Miss Orpha hunted. She let her school out and took the big scholars up into the coves with her and they hollered his name until they had no voices, and fired guns, and men went into the edges of the laurel on their bellies and came out torn and bleeding and did not find him. Jehu hunted harder than anybody. After three weeks they stopped. Children got lost in the laurel. It had happened before.

The lower half of the land went to Jehu. And Ruth Carnes, who had wanted children more than anything in the world, carried four babies in the twenty-five years after that and lost every one of them before its time, and buried them in a row on the point above the house, and died at forty-eight. Nobody ever told her anything. Miss Orpha said she thought Ruth knew anyway.

VI. Where He Is

The Bearpen school · noon

Mr. Webb had not said a word.

So that's your answer, Miss Orpha said. The dinner Ruth gave got him his money. What he said in the path kept him from ever touching it. Every time he looked at a biscuit he saw it throwed away. And the boy took his children from him, and his name, and that's why it's going to Frankfort with you in a wagon. It's not the first time, either. You go back in your books, Mr. Webb, you'll find more than one on these creeks died with jars in the ground and nobody to dig them.

And now? Mr. Webb said, and did not know why he asked it.

Miss Orpha took off her spectacles and wiped them on her apron.

The good's all used up, she said. He used it up on gold. He never laid by any more. There's nothing holding him anywhere now. He's in the laurel, Mr. Webb. Further in than any man ever crawled. He's been in there since the fourth of July, and he can't find his way out, and nobody's hollering his name.

Mr. Webb sat on the step for a long while. Under the beeches the children were playing Antny-over, throwing a yarn ball over the roof of the schoolhouse and yelling, and a boy of six or seven came running around the corner after it and stopped dead at the sight of the man in the linen duster and ran back.

Forty-one thousand dollars, Mr. Webb said at last. And he never ate a decent meal. And he lived a mile up the road from this. He looked at the schoolhouse behind him, at the stick chimney and the one window with paper over a broken pane and the smoke-black ceiling and the forty-three dinner buckets in a row along the wall. He never gave you a dollar, did he. Not a stove. Not a book.

Not a penny, Miss Orpha said.

Then she reached behind her for the Bible she read from every morning, because in those days the Bible was the first reader in every school on the creek, churched or unchurched, and she found the place without looking for it and read it to him on the step.

But they that will be rich fall into temptation and a snare, and into many foolish and hurtful lusts, which drown men in destruction and perdition. For the love of money is the root of all evil: which while some coveted after, they have erred from the faith, and pierced themselves through with many sorrows.

— 1 Timothy 6:9–10

Drown men, Miss Orpha said, and closed the book. That's the boy. Pierced themselves through. That's him.

Then she stood up and rang the hand bell for the afternoon, and the children came in from the beeches, and Mr. Webb sat on the step and listened to forty-three voices through the wall saying the multiplication table in a single long chant, the way they learned it then, sixes and sevens and eights, rising and falling, and found that he was crying, and did not know when he had started.

VII. The Laurel

The high woods · September 1899

In October a wagon came up Bearpen with an iron stove in it, and window glass, and a crate of new readers, and a slate for every child, and a bell for the roof. There was no note with it. Nobody on the creek had to ask who sent it. Some said Cyrus Webb had spent every cent of his fee on the Carnes estate to buy it, and some said more than that.

But before that, in the last week of September, when the sang berries were red, he came back up the creek with four men and axes and asked Miss Orpha to show them the way.

She took them up past the last cleared ground into the cove under the ridge. She had not been there in forty years. She went to it as straight as if she had been there the day before. The men cut into the laurel with their axes, and it was slow, the trunks twisted together like a rope and hard as iron, and they cut for most of a day, a tunnel a man could stand up in, with the cut laurel piled behind them.

At the end of the afternoon one of the men stopped cutting and called back.

He was lying where he had been thrown, very small, gone mostly back into the leaves after forty years, under the laurel, with one shoe still on. Miss Orpha knelt by him in the leaf mold. Mr. Webb said afterward that she did not cry. She said, Well, Davy, the way you would say it to a scholar who has come in late, and gathered him up into a clean meal sack, and one of the men carried him out.

They buried him on the point above the old Carnes house, though not in the row. They buried him across the point on the other side, under a big poplar that had somehow been missed in all those years of cutting, one of his daddy's poplars, where you could see the whole of Bearpen down to the forks. Miss Orpha read over him. All her scholars came, and stood in rows by size.

Coming down, the men stopped to look back. The tunnel they had cut into the laurel hell went in from the edge of the cove, dark and straight, the ends of the cut trunks showing white. Inside it the laurel had already started to close.

Background

The verse

Pleasures destroy the foolish, if they look not for the other shore; the foolish by his thirst for pleasures destroys himself, as if he were his own enemy.Dhammapada 355, tr. F. Max Müller (1881)

In the commentary, a rich man of Sāvatthī called Aputtaka, “Childless,” dies without an heir, and King Pasenadi of Kosala spends seven days carrying his wealth to the palace. At noon the king comes to the Buddha and describes how the man lived: he drove off servants who brought him fine food, clothes and carriages with sticks and clods of earth, ate broken-rice porridge with sour gruel, wore hemp, rode a broken-down chariot and held a parasol of leaves over his head. The Buddha explains. In a past life the man told his wife to give alms to a silent solitary sage, and she gave the best food in the house; meeting the sage on the road, he looked in the bowl and regretted it, saying his servants should have had it. He also killed his brother's only son in the forest for his inheritance. The gift made him rich; the regret kept him from enjoying anything; the murder left him childless through seven lives and sent him to hell, where, his merit spent, he is again. When the king laments that the man never used his wealth or gave it to a teacher living nearby, the Buddha speaks this verse. Read the full commentary story.

Eastern Kentucky, 1899

In the 1890s the coal under the mountains of eastern Kentucky was being bought up, often cheaply and before any railroad reached it. The most famous buyer, John C. C. Mayo, was a schoolteacher from Paintsville who began buying mineral rights with his teaching salary, formed a land company in 1888, and later devised the broad form deed, which severed what lay under the ground from the surface and let the mineral owner take it by almost any means. Timber came out first. Logs were hauled to the creeks and floated down the forks of the Kentucky River on the spring floods, the “log tide,” gathered into rafts at Beattyville and taken down to the sawmills at Frankfort, a trip of a week or more. In late summer families dug wild ginseng, “sang,” for export to China; in recent decades most of the country's wild ginseng exports have come from the southern Appalachian states, especially Kentucky. The rhododendron and mountain laurel thickets of those coves are so dense that they are called laurel hells, because it is nearly impossible to get through one.

When a person died with no heirs at all, the property passed to the state by escheat, an old common-law rule that once sent a dead tenant's land back to the Crown. Kentucky's statutes of the period provided for escheaters, appointed by the state Auditor, who sued in the name of the Commonwealth in the circuit court of the county where the property lay. Jehu Carnes is buried by an Old Regular Baptist preacher, who lines out the hymn the way that church still does, reading each line for the congregation to sing back. Bearpen Fork, its school and everyone on it are invented.
