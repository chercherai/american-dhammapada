Skill and Sport

And do not seek to take the life
Which you can never give.
— “Don’t Kill the Birds,” McGuffey’s Third Eclectic Reader

The way I heard it told, on the north side of the Minidoka project.

I. The Bar

Norland, Minidoka County, Idaho · Saturday, September 12, 1914

Orrin Sayles and Walt Brandt had stood up together in May in the basement of the Methodist church, each with his right hand raised and three fingers straight, and said the Oath and the twelve points of the Law in front of Mr. Harbison and eleven other boys. They were thirteen. After that night you did not see the one without the other.

On the second Saturday in September they walked the three miles down to the river to swim. A Scout had to swim fifty yards to make First Class, and below the old ferry the Snake had a slack place behind a gravel bar where a boy could pace it off. They swam it four times apiece. Then they lay on the bar to dry, with the stones warm under them, and talked about nothing, the way you can with one person.

The desert came down to the water on both banks. Seven years before there had been nothing on the north side but sage and jackrabbits and wind. Then the government dam went in above, and the canal, and people came from Iowa and Nebraska and Utah and took eighty acres apiece, and now when you stood on the bluff you could see the exact line where the laterals stopped, green on this side of it and gray on that. Above the dam the lake was a bird reservation by order of the President, and nobody could fire a gun there. The geese knew it. They traded up and down the river all that month.

Two of them came up the channel now, low, one a little behind the other.

Orrin sat up. He had the best arm on the project. He pitched for the Norland nine against grown men from the south side, and at the rabbit drives he never carried a club. He picked a pebble off the bar.

I’m going to hit that near one in the eye.

You can’t, Walt said.

I’ll put it in the eye on this side and out the eye on the other.

You can’t do that either.

Watch.

The first stone went behind the near goose. It heard the stone go by and turned its head to look back. Orrin had the second one in his hand already, a round one the size of a walnut, and that was the one. It went in at the far eye and came out at the near. The goose cried once and fell, turning over, and struck the gravel a yard from their feet. The other went on up the river, and they could hear it a long time.

Told you, Orrin said.

Walt did not say anything.

Three older boys of the troop were on the bank above with a pair of signal flags. Emmett Lund, who was fifteen and had the Beaver Patrol, came down onto the bar. He looked at the goose and then at Orrin.

Number six, he said. Say it.

A Scout is kind.

All of it.

Orrin said all of it. He is a friend to animals. He will not kill nor hurt any living creature needlessly, but will strive to save and protect all harmless life.

Pick it up, Emmett said. We’re going to Mr. Harbison. You too, Walt. You told him he couldn’t.

II. The Prescription Case

Harbison’s Drug, on the square

Lyle Harbison had come to the project in 1908 from a ditch farm in Colorado by way of a pharmacy course in Denver, and he kept the only drugstore in Norland. He was forty-four and unmarried. He had started the troop two years back because he was asked, and because nobody else would, and the Methodists lent the basement.

On Saturdays the farm families were in town and the fountain was three deep. The five boys came in the alley door. Orrin had the goose rolled in his shirt.

Mr. Harbison was behind the prescription case counting tablets onto a tile with a spatula, by fives. He looked up and saw who it was and what the shirt had in it. He looked down again and finished the count, funneled the tablets into a bottle, wrote the label, and pasted it on square. Then he wiped his hands and came around.

He unrolled the shirt on the back counter. He turned the head with one finger, this way and that way.

Orrin. Is this yours?

Yes, sir.

With a stone.

Two. The first one was to make him turn his head.

Mr. Harbison stood a while. Out front the fountain hissed and a child was asking for strawberry.

“And do not seek to take the life which you can never give,” he said. I had that out of the Third Reader when I was nine years old. I thought it was about sparrows.

He folded the shirt back over the bird.

You took an oath in May. Nobody made you. You walked three miles in the dark to take it. And I have known people who never took one, who never heard of a Scout in their lives, plain farmers with a family to feed, who were more careful than that over a good deal less. You weren’t careful over this at all.

It was Walt who asked. What people?

III. The Upper Ditch

Colorado · about 1880, as he told it

When he was a boy, Mr. Harbison said, there were two ditches out of the same river. His father farmed under the Upper. The one below was called the Fairview. They had the same river and near enough the same decree, and on the Upper the water got to the last man on the last lateral every August, dry years too, and on the Fairview it quit halfway down and the bottom forty farms burned up.

The Fairview people studied on it and decided it was the horse. The Upper had a ditch rider named Dunham who rode a gelding black as stove polish, and it was said along the river that water followed that horse. So one July the Fairview board drove up and hired him off the Upper for the season. They paid forty dollars, in gold. Their own rider rode the black horse up and down the Fairview until September, and the bottom forty burned up the same as before.

The next spring they came back with a tablet and a pencil. They said, Write down for us how you people run your ditch. Whatever the rule is, we will have it lettered on a board in gilt and hang it in our headgate house.

The Upper people did not know they had a rule. So the Fairview men went around and asked them one at a time.

The president of the ditch said he was not the man to ask. On the Fourth of July he had fired his rifle into the air, once for each quarter of the Union, and he had never found where the fourth ball came down. For all he knew it came down on something living in the reservoir. He could not say he had kept any rule.

His mother said she had given her good shawl to one daughter-in-law and a sack of dried apples to the other, and had felt mean about it ever since. Dunham the ditch rider said that he had once driven a stake for a measuring box into a crawdad hole and never knew whether the crawdad was at home. The man who hauled for the company said he had touched up his team with the whip to beat a storm in, and they were willing horses and had not needed it. The storekeeper, who was treasurer, had pulled a head of wheat in a field worked on shares, to see was it ripe, before the landlord’s third was measured out, and that head was a third the landlord’s. The man who kept time on the water turns said it had come on to rain once while he was counting out a turn, and he ran for the shed and lost his count, and it might be he gave one neighbor a minute of another neighbor’s water. The tender at the headgate had cursed a man who came for the key after dark with a woman on the wagon seat, and she turned out to be the man’s sister.

There was a widow at the end of the last lateral who took in sewing. A stranger had paid her five dollars ahead on a suit of clothes and never come back to be measured. She had turned away other work for three years on account of that five dollars, and gone short, and she was not sure but that somewhere in the third year she had begun to wish him ill.

Every one of them said the same thing, Mr. Harbison said. Don’t put my name to it. I can’t say I’ve kept it.

The Fairview men said there was nothing in any of that. A crawdad. A minute of water. The Upper people said maybe not. The Fairview men wrote it all down regardless, and what it came to, when they boiled it, was five lines. Don’t kill what you have no need to. Don’t take what isn’t yours. Leave another man’s household be. Say what is so. Come to the headgate sober. They had it lettered on a board in gilt and hung it up, and what is more they kept it, and the next August there was water at the bottom of the Fairview.

It never was the horse, he said. A ditch is nine miles of people. The water gets to the end if nobody along the way takes a little that isn’t his. And people who will lie awake over a crawdad don’t reach down at night and lift a headgate.

IV. Two. One.

The back counter

Those were farmers, Mr. Harbison said. Nobody had sworn them to anything. And you stood up with your hand in the air.

He looked at Orrin’s hands, which were hanging at his sides.

I have watched you pitch. Those hands will do whatever you tell them, and that is not common. It is a thing to be careful with, the way I am careful with what is in the blue bottles. Your hands. Where your feet take you. And your mouth, Orrin. This didn’t start with a stone. It started with “Watch.”

He went to the shelf over the desk where he kept the Pharmacopoeia and the Dispensatory, and brought down a thick book in a brown binding, the first of two.

This man could throw. I know what is said about him on Sundays, and this is not that part. This is his son writing.

He once killed a hare sitting in the flower-garden at Shrewsbury by throwing a marble at it, and, as a man, he once killed a cross-beak with a stone. He was so unhappy at having uselessly killed the cross-beak that he did not mention it for years, and then explained that he should never have thrown at it if he had not felt sure that his old skill had gone from him.

— Francis Darwin, The Life and Letters of Charles Darwin (1887), vol. I, ch. III

He was a good shot besides. He went around the world on a ship, shooting birds for his collection wherever it put in, and somewhere on the way he handed the gun to his servant and did not ask for it back. He says why himself.

I discovered, though unconsciously and insensibly, that the pleasure of observing and reasoning was a much higher one than that of skill and sport.

— Charles Darwin, Autobiography, in Life and Letters, vol. I, ch. II

He could sit by a pond any number of hours watching a float, by himself, and want nothing. I don’t expect you to believe on a Saturday afternoon that looking is better than hitting. You could find out.

He shut the book.

Now. It is killed, and it is September, and Mrs. Aiken on the A-2 lateral has five at the table and no man. The two of you will pluck it and draw it and carry it out to her. When she asks where you came by a goose, tell her.

She asked. They told her.

•••

Orrin Sayles pitched for Norland until the war and for a sugar-factory team after it, and nobody ever again saw him throw at a living thing. Some days he would pick a stone up off the road and carry it a while, and you would see him set it down. He did not speak of the goose. Walt Brandt, who knew, did not speak of it either.

But Orrin went down to the bar by himself most evenings that fall and sat until dark, and in the back of his Handbook, on the blank leaves they give you for notes, there is a list in pencil.

Sept. 12. Canada geese going up the river. 2. 1.

Sept. 13. 1, same place. Calling.

Sept. 14. 1.

It goes on that way down the page, a date and a number, with the ones giving out toward the end of the month and the numbers getting larger. The last line of that year says, Oct. 9. Counted to 300 and quit. They were still coming.

Background

The verse

He who controls his hand, he who controls his feet, he who controls his speech, he who is well controlled, he who delights inwardly, who is collected, who is solitary and content, him they call Bhikshu.Dhammapada 362, tr. F. Max Müller (1881)

In the commentary, two young monks who were ordained together and go everywhere together bathe in the river Aciravatī and stand drying in the sun. Two geese fly over. One monk says he can put a stone through a goose’s eye, the near one and then the far; his friend says he cannot; the first pebble makes the bird turn its head and the second goes in at one eye and out at the other. Other monks bring the pair to the Buddha, who asks if it is true, and then tells them that householders long ago had scruples over far smaller things. His story is the birth story of the Kuru rule: a kingdom without rain borrows a neighbor king’s lucky black elephant, gets no rain, and sends to have the neighbor’s five precepts written on a gold plate; the king, his mother, his surveyor, his charioteer, his treasurer, his grain-measurer, his gatekeeper, a courtesan and others each protest that they cannot be sure they have kept them, on account of some trifle; the precepts are copied and kept, and the rain comes. Read the full commentary story and the birth story of the Kurus.

The Minidoka project, 1914

The Minidoka Project was one of the first works of the federal Reclamation Service. It was approved in 1904; Minidoka Dam on the Snake River was finished in 1906 and the north-side canal in 1907, and settlers took up farm units of eighty acres in what had been sagebrush. The lake behind the dam was set aside in 1909 as a bird reservation, now the Minidoka National Wildlife Refuge. Norland, its drugstore and its people are invented; Rupert, the project town nearby, is real.

The Boy Scouts of America was four years old in 1914. The sixth point of its Scout Law is quoted here as printed in the first Handbook (1911), which also required a First Class Scout to swim fifty yards. The year before, the Weeks–McLean Act had put migratory birds under federal protection for the first time, and on September 1, 1914, eleven days before this story, Martha, the last passenger pigeon, died in the Cincinnati Zoo. “Don’t Kill the Birds” is Lesson LV of the 1879 McGuffey’s Third Eclectic Reader. Darwin’s recollections, and his son’s account of the cross-beak, are in the first volume of the Life and Letters (1887).
