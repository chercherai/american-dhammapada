Walking the American WayCommentary 20.11 · Kisā Gotamī

Dry Mustard

They did eat and drink, they bought and sold, they planted and built.
— Luke 17:28

Now I heard it like this, in the carports of Stonybrook.

I. The Jar

Montrose Street, South Philadelphia · 1936

Her grandmother told it every October, when the mustard greens came in at the Ninth Street market, and she told it the same way every time, sitting on the top step with a colander of beans in her lap.

There was a girl below Washington Avenue so thin the whole neighborhood called her Sicca, the stick. Some people said she had once picked coal out of a rich man’s yard and it had turned to gold in her hands, but Nonna Gallo did not vouch for that part. She had been there for the other part. In the fall of the influenza Sicca’s little boy died in her lap, and Sicca would not have it. She put him on her hip in his blue blanket and walked the neighborhood asking for medicine, and a nun at the school on Ellsworth Street, where they had made a hospital, told her the medicine was mustard seed. A pinch of it, from a house where nobody had ever died.

She came to me, Nonna Gallo said. Your grandfather was four days dead. I had the black crepe on the door and she walked right past it. I went and got the jar. I had the jar in my hand, Rosalia, like this, and then she asked me. Has anybody died in this house.

Rosalie was seven. She sat one step down and asked what she asked every year.

Did she find one?

Where would she find one? On Montrose Street? In my mother’s house in Caltanissetta there were more dead than chairs.

So what happened to her?

She’s a sister now. You’ve seen her, at the clinic, the thin one. Sister Antonia. She took the boy’s name. Nonna Gallo snapped a bean in half and looked at the two ends. That first sister was a hard one, to send a girl out like that. This one never sends anybody. Fifteen years in that clinic, and she never sent one.

Rosalie thought about it on the step, and for a good while after. She did not say what she thought, because her grandmother would have laughed. She thought the trouble was the houses. Every house she knew was old. Three people had died in the front bedroom on Montrose Street alone, and the room knew it; you could tell by the way the door hung. Sicca had looked in the wrong place. Somewhere there had to be a house so new that nothing had happened in it yet, and that was where you would go, if you ever needed to.

II. Sparrow Lane

Levittown, Pennsylvania · 1952–1955

The house on Sparrow Lane was nine weeks old when Eddie Marino carried her over the sill of it. It smelled of paint and cut pine. It had three bedrooms on one floor, a kitchen with a General Electric range and a Bendix washer already standing in it, a carport, and a picture window that looked out on a square of raked dirt where Mr. Levitt’s men had sown grass and staked one sapling the thickness of her thumb. There was no cellar. The heat ran in copper pipes through the floor itself, so that in January the boy would lie down flat on the kitchen tiles like a cat.

Eddie had been in the Navy, and a veteran could have the house for a hundred dollars down. He worked the open hearth at the new Fairless Works on the river, four to midnight most weeks, and came home with his eyebrows singed and slept until ten.

The year before, all of it had been spinach and broccoli. There were seventeen thousand houses going up on those fields, forty a day when the weather held, and there were six kinds, and on Sparrow Lane they were all one kind. The streets curved so that nobody could drive fast. Every street in Stonybrook began with an S. You could not put up a fence, and you could not hang wash on a Sunday, and you had to cut your grass once a week from April to November, and Rosalie did not mind any of it. Mr. Levitt sold to anybody who was white. That was in the papers too, and nobody on Sparrow Lane said much about it either way, which she would think about later, when she had started thinking about who was on the street and who was not.

The first night they had no bed yet. They put the mattress on the floor of the big bedroom and lay looking at a ceiling without one crack in it, and she thought, nobody has ever died in this room. Nobody had been sick in it, or born in it, or had a fight in it. She said so to Eddie, and he laughed and said give it time, and she hit him with the pillow.

Michael was thirteen months old that summer. He took his first steps without a hand to hold across the kitchen floor of that house, and he slept in the small bedroom she had papered with sailboats.

Her sisters came up on the train on Sundays to see the house. Her mother-in-law came, in her black coat, and walked through the rooms touching things. They opened the oven. They ran the water. All on one floor, her mother-in-law said, like a hospital, and she meant it kindly. They stood at the picture window and watched Mickey run on the lawn, and they told her she had everything, and she agreed with them. She had the house and the washer and a 1950 Plymouth and a husband who handed over his envelope on Fridays, and she had the boy, who was brown as a nut by July and had his father’s ears. From the time she got up she was thinking about the grass and the slipcovers and the payment and his shoes. She thought about them the way you run a hand along a shelf to be sure everything is still on it.

She had never slept as she slept on Sparrow Lane. On Montrose Street there had been the trolley, and the Lombardos through the wall, and her grandmother coughing. Here there was a sprinkler somewhere, and then nothing at all, and then morning. The whole street slept that way, four hundred houses with the lamps out.

III. The Glass

August 1955

That was the spring of Davy Crockett, and Mickey had the cap. Every boy on Sparrow Lane had the cap, a ring of fur with a striped tail down the back, a dollar and change at the five-and-ten, and he wore his to the table and to the bathtub and to bed. It was also the spring of the Salk shots. They lined the first and second graders up in the school gymnasium before the term let out, and the mothers on Sparrow Lane stood in the hall and cried, because it was over; another summer or two and nobody would have to keep a child out of the pool again. Mickey was four. The four-year-olds were to have theirs next.

The first storm came through on a Friday in the middle of August and rained itself out by Saturday noon. The lane was full of puddles. She let him out in them in his cap and his bathing trunks, and stood at the picture window with a cup of coffee and watched him stamp.

On Sunday he had a headache and would not eat. On Monday morning he could not bring his chin down to his chest, and when she gave him milk it came back out through his nose. The doctor came to the house and looked at him for less than a minute and went to the telephone in the hall.

They took him to the contagious hospital in the city, and there they took him through a door. She was not allowed through the door. There was a corridor with a window in it, and through the window she could see a row of tanks, long steel ones, each with a head at the end of it on a little shelf, and a mirror over each head. The fourth head was his. They had shaved a patch above his ear. The nurses wore masks, and they were not unkind, and they said no eleven times.

The cap could not go in to him either. Anything that went in would have to be burned after. So she kept it. She stood at the glass for four days with her pocketbook on her arm and the cap inside the pocketbook, and Eddie stood beside her when he was not at the mill, and the tanks breathed. You could hear them through the wall, all together, like a ward of sleepers.

The second storm came up the coast on Thursday. It rained all that night on the hospital roof. At ten minutes to six on Friday morning a doctor came down the corridor with his mask pulled under his chin and said it to the two of them, and held out a clipboard, and Eddie signed where the doctor’s finger was.

Rosalie did not sign anything.

She had not seen it. She had seen a tank through a pane of glass, with a small head at the end of it, and then a doctor’s mouth moving. Anybody could say anything in a corridor. She had sat up with her grandmother. She had kissed her grandmother’s forehead in the box in the front room on Montrose Street, with the mirror covered, and she knew what dead was. It was a thing in old houses. It was not a thing that came to a house with the paint still soft in the closets, and took a boy who had been stamping in a puddle on Saturday.

At home Eddie picked up the telephone to call Di Renzo, the undertaker on Dickinson Street who had buried all the Gallos, and she put her finger down on the cradle and held it there.

Ro.

Not yet.

He looked at her finger. He had been awake four days. Monday, he said. I can give you till Monday.

IV. The Pocketbook

Ellsworth Street, South Philadelphia · Saturday morning

She took the Plymouth and drove to the city with both hands on the wheel. The radio said the Delaware was out of its banks from Easton down and still rising, and that a creek in the Poconos had come up over a summer camp in the dark, and they did not have the count yet. She turned it off.

The clinic was where it had always been, in the ground floor of the old school, with the same green paint to the height of a child’s shoulder. There were mothers on the bench with babies. Sister Antonia was at the scale. She was fifty-nine years old and looked like a clothes prop in a veil, and she weighed a baby and wrote the number on a card and gave the baby back before she looked up.

You’re a Gallo, she said. From across from the coal yard.

Rosalie. I’m a Marino now. We’re up in Levittown.

The Sister took her into the room behind, where there was a low table and some small chairs left from when it was a school. She sat down on one with her knees up and waited.

Rosalie stayed on her feet. She told it fast. The puddles, the milk, the glass, the tanks, the doctor with his mask down. She said they were saying her son was dead, and that she had not been let in to see for herself, and that she knew what the medicine was, because her grandmother had held the jar.

She did, said Sister Antonia. She had it in her hand.

So tell me to go and get it.

I never tell anybody that.

I know you don’t. I know why. You think there isn’t any such house. Rosalie heard her own voice going up, and let it go. There wasn’t, down here. There is now. I live in one. There are four hundred of them on my side of the parkway and the oldest is three years old. You can still smell the paint. Nobody has ever died in one of those houses, Sister. Three years ago it was spinach.

Sister Antonia looked at her for a while. Then she looked at the pocketbook, which Rosalie had on her arm with her other hand flat over the clasp.

What have you got in the bag?

Nothing.

It looks heavy.

Rosalie went out past the mothers on the bench. The Sister did not get up and did not call her back, and for the rest of her life that was the nearest she came to sending anyone.

V. The Doors

Stonybrook · Saturday afternoon

She did not go into her own house. She left the Plymouth in the carport and took the tin measuring cup from the nail by the kitchen door, since a woman at your door with a cup in her hand needs no explaining, and went next door.

Peg Novak came to the screen with a dish towel on her shoulder. Her face changed when she saw who it was. Everybody on the lane knew; the ambulance had seen to that.

Peg, have you got any dry mustard? Just a little. A spoonful.

Honey. Sure I do. Come in. Sit down, for God’s sake.

I have to ask you something first. Has anybody died in your house?

Peg laughed, once, out of fright. In this house? It’s the same as yours. They finished it the week before we came. She went and got the mustard, a red and yellow can, and stood in the kitchen doorway turning it over in her hand. She and Stan had come down from Nanticoke when the collieries started closing. My father, she said. A roof fall, in thirty-eight. They brought him home on a door. My sister’s little girl, with the diphtheria. I don’t know what made me think of them. She put two spoonfuls in the cup. That isn’t here, though. That’s all up home.

At the Lerners’ there was a little brass case on the doorpost and a card table in the living room with a jigsaw puzzle half done. Ruth Lerner was from the Bronx and kept her mustard in a crock. Three years old, this house, the same as yours. Who would have died in it? Then she was quiet for a moment. Sol’s family were from a town near Lublin. He has a list. It’s in the drawer with the insurance papers, forty-one names, and he takes it out once a year. I don’t know if you would say they died in this house. She filled the cup to the half mark, far more than a spoonful. I would say they live here.

Maureen Pruitt had come over from Coventry in 1946 on a ship full of brides, and her mustard was in a yellow tin with a bull’s head on it, the real kind, that took the roof off your mouth. My mum and my gran. The fourteenth of November, 1940. One night, the both of them, and I was at the pictures. She said it lightly, the way she said everything, and her hand shook, and mustard went on the step.

Betty Kovach was from Donora, out past Pittsburgh. Her husband had followed the steel east. The smog, she said. October of forty-eight. It sat down on the town for five days, you couldn’t see the porch rail from the door. My pop was one of the twenty. He had the asthma already. She looked past Rosalie at the lane, the wet lawns, the sky washed clean by the storm. That’s why I like it here. You can see.

The Hallorans had a photograph on top of the television set, a boy in a Marine uniform with his cap too big for him. Korea, the first winter. Mrs. Halloran did not say anything about it. She saw Rosalie look, and she went and got the mustard.

At the house on the bend a girl of twenty came to the door with a baby on her shoulder and said no, nobody, they were all fine, thank God. She filled the cup from a jar. Rosalie was at the foot of the walk when the girl called after her. There was one before Linda. A boy. He lived two days. She shifted the baby. We never had a picture taken. I never told anybody on this street that. I don’t know why I’m telling you.

A man in his undershirt, on the night turn, got up out of his sleep to answer the bell. Eleven men, he said, in a wood in Germany, in one afternoon. He did not have any mustard. He stood on his step in his bare feet and said all eleven names anyhow, and then he said he was sorry for her trouble and went back to bed.

She went on. Sparrow Lane, Spindle Lane, around the long curve and back. Nobody refused her. They filled the cup until it would hold no more and then they pressed the cans and jars on her, so that she was carrying them in the crook of her arm against the pocketbook. Not one of them had a death in the house, and every one of them stopped in the doorway and told her who they had brought with them.

VI. The Lamps

Sparrow Lane · evening

By seven the light had gone long and yellow. Somebody’s sprinkler was going on a lawn that could not have needed it, after two storms, ticking around and around. She stood in the middle of Sparrow Lane where it bent, with the cup in one hand and four cans of mustard in her arm, and the lamps began to come on.

Every house had one. It was the same lamp, or near enough, on the same table, in the middle of the same window, and they came on one after another down the curve of the lane as the women went to them. She had looked at those lamps for three years and seen a new street full of young people.

She saw the street now. There was a miner laid on a door in the Novaks’ living room. There were forty-one people from a town near Lublin at the Lerners’ card table. An old man from Donora sat on the Kovaches’ davenport trying to get his breath, and two women from Coventry stood in a kitchen, and a boy who had lived two days was in the house on the bend, and eleven soldiers were in the house where the man had gone back to sleep. They had come up in the moving vans with the lamps and the bedsteads. They had come over on the ships. In every one of those windows the dead outnumbered the living, three to one, ten to one, and it was so in the new streets going up beyond the parkway and in the old ones on the far side of the river, in the Bronx and Nanticoke and Coventry, and on Montrose Street, and in Caltanissetta where there were more of them than chairs. It was so on Lombard Street, where her grandmother used to buy greens from a colored woman with a tub garden, and where nobody had been handed a hundred-dollar house. The fields under her feet had been somebody’s before they were spinach. She had thought there was a kind of house it could not find. There had never been any such house. There had only been three years, and a street that slept well.

The pocketbook was pulling at her shoulder. It had been pulling since Monday, and she had not let herself feel it.

She went up her own walk. Eddie was at the kitchen table in the dark with his hands around a cup of coffee gone cold. She set the cans down on the drainboard one at a time, and the measuring cup, heaped and yellow, and then she picked up the telephone and held it out to him.

Call Di Renzo, she said. Tell him Michael Edward Marino. Tell him he was four in June. I want to go down with you and see him. I want the blue suit, and I want it open.

Eddie took the telephone out of her hand as if it might break, and dialed.

VII. The Small Chairs

Ellsworth Street · Tuesday

They buried him from St. Paul’s on Christian Street, where she had been baptized, and took him out to Holy Cross, where the Gallos had a plot. Half of Sparrow Lane drove down for it in a line of cars with their headlights on. Beyond the Gallo stone there was a long piece of ground with grass on it and no stones at all, and she saw Sister Antonia, who had come out on the trolley, stand at the edge of that ground for a minute by herself before she came over to the grave.

Afterward Rosalie left Eddie with her sisters and walked to Ellsworth Street in her black dress. The clinic was closed for the afternoon. The Sister was in the back room washing bottles, and she dried her hands and sat down on one of the small chairs, and this time Rosalie sat too, knees up, across the low table.

Did you get the seed?

No, Sister. I got mustard. I have nine cans of mustard on my drainboard. She found she could almost laugh, and then could not. The houses are three years old, and in every one of them the dead are more than the living. They brought them along. I never knew it. I lived on that street three years and I never knew who was in those houses.

You thought it was only you, said Sister Antonia. Everybody thinks so. I thought so for a whole day, from Montrose Street to Race Street, with him on my hip. She said the next part quietly, looking at the table, the way you would read someone the hours off a door.

Fear not the sentence of death. Remember what things have been before thee, and what shall come after thee: this sentence is from the Lord upon all flesh.

— Ecclesiasticus 41:5

We had everything, Rosalie said. Everybody told me so. I counted it every morning.

I know you did.

They did eat and drink, they married wives, and were given in marriage, until the day that Noe entered into the ark: and the flood came and destroyed them all. Likewise as it came to pass, in the days of Lot: they did eat and drink, they bought and sold, they planted and built.

— Luke 17:27–28

That’s hard, Sister. They weren’t doing anything wrong. Planting and building.

Where does it say they did wrong? It says they were busy. It says the water came while they were counting. The Sister folded the dish towel in four. You’re awake now. It costs what it costs. Stay awake.

Rosalie opened the pocketbook. She had not opened it in eight days, not for a handkerchief, not at the grave. She took out the cap and laid it on the low table between them, with the tail straight. It was smaller than she remembered. It smelled of his head.

Sister Antonia did not touch it. She looked at it for as long as Rosalie did.

You can take that home again, she said. Hang it where it hung. There’s no rule against it. It will weigh what a cap weighs.

•••

The river went down that week and left its mud in the towns upstream. On Sparrow Lane the women came across the lawns, one or two at a time. They brought a ham and a tray of stuffed cabbage and a noodle pudding and a trifle in a glass bowl, and they stayed. Ruth Lerner brought the list out of the drawer one afternoon and read Rosalie all forty-one names at the kitchen table. The girl from the house on the bend told her the boy’s name, which was Thomas. Rosalie wrote them down. By October she had a page for every house she had knocked at, and the women on Sparrow Lane had started coming to her kitchen when there was something they could not say in their own.

She kept the nine cans in a row on the sill over the sink. The cap went back on its hook by the carport door.

She did not sleep as she had. Most nights she got up around two, when the furnace was off and the house was still, and went and sat in the chair by the picture window. The lane lay out in front of her in its long curve under the streetlights, every lawn cut, every window dark, four hundred houses breathing together. She sat with all of them, the ones in the beds and the ones who had come along in the vans. Then she reached over and turned on the lamp.

Background

The verse

Death comes and carries off that man, praised for his children and flocks, his mind distracted, as a flood carries off a sleeping village.Dhammapada 287, tr. F. Max Müller (1881)

The commentary tells the story of Kisā Gotamī twice. The long telling belongs to verse 114. This second, short one returns to the moment she comes back empty-handed. Her child had died, she would not believe it, and she went about looking for a cure until someone sent her to the Buddha, who asked for a pinch of mustard seed from a house where no one had died. “In the entire village the dead are more in number than the living,” she reports. He answers that she was wrong to imagine she alone had lost a child, that death sweeps all beings away like a torrent with their longings unfulfilled, and he speaks verse 287. Read the full commentary story, or about Kisā Gotamī. The series told the long version as The Mustard Seed; the girl called Sicca in that story is the Sister in this one.

Levittown, Pennsylvania, 1955

Levittown was built by Levitt & Sons between 1952 and 1958 on 5,750 acres of Bucks County spinach and broccoli farms, beside the new Fairless Works of U.S. Steel. The first family moved into a house on Stonybrook Drive on June 23, 1952. When building stopped there were more than 17,000 houses in six models, set on concrete slabs with radiant heat in the floor, along curving streets whose names share the first letter of their neighborhood. Veterans could buy with almost nothing down. Buyers came from Philadelphia row-house neighborhoods, from the declining anthracite towns and from the steel valleys of western Pennsylvania. Early rules forbade fences and Sunday wash lines. Levitt refused to sell to Black families, and when William and Daisy Myers bought a house in Levittown in 1957 they met weeks of mob harassment. The Encyclopedia of Greater Philadelphia has more.

The results of the Salk polio vaccine trial were announced on April 12, 1955, and the first shots went to young schoolchildren that spring, so the summer of 1955 was among the last in which American parents feared the disease as they had for decades. In August two storms crossed the region five days apart. The second, Hurricane Diane, caused the worst floods then on record in eastern Pennsylvania; Brodhead Creek in the Poconos overran a summer camp and killed 37 people, most of them children. The neighbors’ dead are drawn from real events: the Donora smog of October 1948, which killed twenty people in a steel town near Pittsburgh, and the bombing of Coventry on the night of November 14, 1940. The Marinos, their neighbors, Sparrow Lane and the clinic on Ellsworth Street are fictional.
