I. The Steer

Marathon County, Wisconsin · spring 1998

Walt Brandt milked ninety Holsteins in a tie-stall barn on County Road N, west of Marathon City, in the same barn where his father had milked forty, and he was the one farmer in the township the Hmong families called when somebody died.

It started by accident. A young man named Tou Xiong drove out one April morning with a cousin and a borrowed stock trailer, looking for a steer. The farms nearer town had told him no, or named a price meant to send him home. Walt had a Holstein steer he had kept back for the freezer. He sold it for what it would have brought at the sale barn, and helped them load it, and asked what it was for.

My uncle’s wife, said Tou. For the funeral.

The uncle had come along. He stood by the trailer in a gray nylon windbreaker with his hands folded behind his back, a small old man with a face as brown and creased as a harness, and before they loaded the steer he walked around it twice, slowly. He ran his palm down its back. He looked at its feet and its eyes and the inside of its lip. It was how Walt’s father used to go over a cow at an auction, and Walt had never seen anybody from town look at an animal like that.

He kept cattle, Tou said. In Laos, before. Cows, buffalo, horses. He was a teacher in the school too, but everybody had cattle.

The old man’s name was Neng Chue Xiong. He said almost nothing that day, in either language. But when the steer was in the trailer he came across the yard and took Walt’s hand in both of his and held it, and looked at him the way he had looked at the steer, all the way down, without hurry. Walt was forty-nine that spring. Nobody had looked at him like that in a long time, and he did not know what to do with his face.

After that it was understood. When a family in Wausau needed an animal for a funeral, somebody called the Brandt farm. Walt sold them dry cows and old cows and steers at sale-barn price, and let them take as long as they wanted choosing, and learned not to ask more than they told him. He learned that a funeral could go on three days and nights at the funeral home, that there was a long bamboo reed pipe played at it, and that whenever a family wanted something done right, they sent for the old man.

And every time Walt went into town, to the implement dealer or the bank or the vet, he drove the long way around, over the river to the east side, where the Xiongs lived in a narrow frame house with a garden that took up the whole back yard, and he left something on the porch. A gallon of milk out of the bulk tank, still cold from the stainless. A pound of butter. Two pounds of cheese curds from the cheese plant down the road, so fresh they squeaked against your teeth. A pint of cream with a paper towel rubber-banded over the top.

He would knock, and Neng Chue Xiong would come to the door and thank him with both hands, and Walt would say what he always said.

You ought to come out and see the place. Stay a few days. My folks’ old house is sitting empty.

Not yet, the old man said. Thank you. Not yet.

Carol said to leave the poor man alone. Their son Danny, who was in high school then, said the old guy probably thought his dad was nuts. Walt kept on doing it. Seven years, the milk and the butter and the curds, and the old man kept on saying not yet, and neither of them seemed to mind.

II. Signs

November 2004 – November 2005

Then the thing happened up north in Sawyer County.

On the second morning of the deer season in 2004, on private land near Birchwood, a party of hunters found a Hmong hunter from St. Paul sitting in one of their stands and told him to get off the property. Nobody afterward agreed about everything that was said, or who raised a rifle first. When it was over, six of the hunters were dead and two were wounded, and the man from St. Paul was arrested a few hours later, when he came back out of the woods to his camp.

Walt heard it on the radio in the milkhouse. By the end of the week there was nobody in Marathon County, white or Hmong, who was not talking about it, and nobody who was talking about anything else.

That winter you heard things at the co-op you had not heard said out loud before. Somebody’s brother-in-law knew a fellow up north who had found the Hmong camp and done his business in their cooking pots. Somebody had a bumper sticker. A man Walt had sat next to in church for twenty years said, standing at the feed counter, that they ought to send every one of them back where they came from, and then looked at Walt as if waiting for him to say something, and Walt paid for his mineral and went out to the truck and did not say anything, and drove home with his face hot.

In the spring Tou called and asked, carefully, whether he and his brother Kou might hunt Walt’s back forty in November. Nobody else would let them on. Walt said yes. He walked the line with them in May when the ground was soft, showed them the corner posts and where his land quit and the Hessel place began, the safe directions to shoot, the road, the house, the barn. Then he sat at the kitchen table and wrote out the permission on a pad from the feed mill and signed it and dated it, and Carol witnessed it, so nobody could ever say.

That was the year Danny was in Iraq.

He had joined the Army out of high school, against everybody’s advice, and now he drove a fuel truck in convoys up and down the roads north of Baghdad, roads where men buried artillery shells in the gravel shoulder and set them off with the button of a garage-door opener. Walt knew this because he had made himself learn it. He watched the news in the kitchen at noon with the sound turned low and the paper open on the table to the list of the dead, and he lay awake at night doing arithmetic. He hated the men by the roadsides. He hated them the way he had never hated anything, with his jaw, in the dark, three and four in the morning, faceless men he could see perfectly. It sat in his chest all day like a stone in a cow’s gut.

Danny’s blaze-orange coat hung on its hook in the mudroom, where he had left it after the last season they hunted together. Walt had told Carol he would not hunt this year without him. He walked past the coat four times a day.

In September the trial was held up in Hayward and the man from St. Paul was convicted of all six killings. In November, the week the judge gave him life, the radio stations ran announcements from the DNR about trespass and safety in English and Hmong, one after the other, and up near Birchwood the township put up No Trespassing signs printed in three languages.

The day after the sentencing, Walt drove the long way into town and left curds on the porch on the east side and knocked. He said what he always said.

Neng Chue Xiong looked at him a long time in the doorway.

Yes, he said. Saturday. I will come.

III. The Bur Oak

The Brandt farm · the week before the opener

He did not come up to the house. Walt found him on Saturday morning when the milk truck pulled out, sitting under the big bur oak at the end of the lane, on a folding canvas chair he must have brought with him, with a plastic grocery bag at his feet and his hands folded on his knees. Tou had dropped him there at daybreak on his way to the Saturday shift at the paper mill. The oak still had its leaves, brown and stiff, the way bur oaks hold them into winter, and they rattled over his head in the wind.

Mr. Xiong. Why didn’t you come up?

I am up, said the old man, and smiled for the first time Walt had ever seen.

Carol aired out the old house and made up the bed with the good quilt. And for seven days Walt Brandt fed him.

Every morning after milking he carried down a gallon of milk drawn warm from the tank before the cooler took it, a dish of cream, butter Carol had worked in the kitchen with the paddle that had been her mother’s, curds, cottage cheese, a wedge of brick cheese from the plant down the road. In the evenings Tou and Kou came out after work to look over the back forty before the opener, and the boy Pheng, Tou’s son, seventeen, who drove his great-uncle wherever he needed to go, and they all ate together in his parents’ old kitchen, Hmong food out of plastic tubs from the east side and Walt’s milk and butter, and Walt noticed that no one ever went away hungry from that table and nothing was ever left over.

The old man came up to the barn at milking. He did not ask to; he simply came and stood in the alley in his windbreaker with his hands behind his back and watched. The cows, which did not like strangers, did not seem to notice him. On Wednesday night a heifer had trouble calving, and while Walt was going for the chains the old man went into the pen and put one hand flat on her flank and talked to her low in Hmong, and by the time Walt got back the calf was out on the straw and the heifer was licking it and the old man was wiping his hands on a feed sack.

They talked, slowly, in the evenings, after the nephews went home. Walt did most of it. The old man asked short questions and waited for long answers. He asked about Walt’s father and the forty cows. He asked about the milk price, which was better than it had been, and the bank. He asked about Danny.

Some of what the old man said about himself, Walt pieced together later. That he had taught children to read in a village in the mountains of Laos until the war came up the valley. That he had carried a rifle for the Americans after that, for twelve years, and that most of the boys he had taught to read had carried rifles too, and that most of them were dead. That in the spring of 1975 he had walked to the Mekong with his family and crossed it at night, and had lived in a camp in Thailand called Ban Vinai for seven years, and had come to Wausau in the winter of 1982 to a church basement full of donated coats, and had worked eleven summers on his knees under the black shade cloth in the ginseng gardens west of town.

On Thursday Walt asked him why he had never come before.

The old man thought about it. At first you brought milk so I would come, he said. That is a good reason. Then after some years you brought milk because you bring milk. That is a better reason. Now I came.

Thursday night Walt told him about the men by the roadsides, and the arithmetic at three in the morning, and the man at the feed counter, and the stone. He had not told Carol all of it. He had not told the pastor any of it. The old man listened with his eyes on the floor. When it was done he said nothing at all for a long time, and then he said only, Yes. I know that one. And he went to bed.

Walt went out and did the late check on the heifer, and found himself singing to the cows under his breath the way his father used to, the old hymn from church, the one his father had liked best, A mighty fortress is our God, a bulwark never failing, all four verses, the words coming back to him one after another out of the dark of the barn like cows coming in to their stalls.

On Friday evening, the seventh day, he was in the milkhouse washing up when the compressor on the bulk tank shut off. It had been running so long that he had stopped hearing it. When it quit, the whole room rang with the quiet, and Walt stood there with the hose in his hand and understood that the stone was gone. He could not have said where it went. Danny was still in Iraq. The roads were still the roads. The men by them were still men, some of them no older than Danny, with mothers who read lists. He stood in the clean wet milkhouse and breathed, and it went all the way down.

He went out to tell the old man and found him sitting in the folding chair under the bur oak in the dark, with his collar up, looking at the barn lights.

Something happened, Walt said.

Yes, said Neng Chue Xiong. Good. Tomorrow I go home.

IV. Stop Here

Saturday · the opener

They walked down the lane together a little after six in the morning, in the gray before sunrise, with the frost white on the hay stubble and the oak rattling at the bottom of the hill. Walt had already milked. He carried the old man’s grocery bag and the folded canvas chair. Down at the road Pheng’s little Honda sat with its parking lights on and its exhaust going up straight in the cold. Tou and Kou had been in the back forty since five, sitting against two trees on the far fence line in their orange coats, waiting for light.

All over the county trucks were parked on the shoulders. All over the state, more than half a million people sat in the woods with rifles across their knees, watching the dark go gray. In a few minutes it would be legal to shoot.

Halfway down the lane, the old man stopped and turned and took his bag and his chair.

Stop here, he said. Go back to your cows.

Walt had meant to walk him to the car. He had meant, honestly, to stand by the car window and say something, though he did not know what. But he stopped. He did what he was told, the way he had done everything for a week. The old man took Walt’s hand in both of his and held it, and then went on down the lane with the bag and the chair, small and gray, and Walt turned around and started back.

He cut across the hayfield toward the lights of the barn, the shortest way, the way he had walked ten thousand times. He was wearing his brown canvas chore coat and his seed cap. Danny’s orange coat was hanging on its hook in the mudroom.

The first shots of the season began, the way they always did, all at once, a flat popping from every direction like the first corn in a pan, from the county forest and the Hessel place and the river bottom and the farms across the road.

Pheng, standing by the open door of the Honda, saw Walt go down in the stubble. He said afterward that he did not hear one shot louder than the others. Walt simply knelt, as if to look at something on the ground, and then lay down on his side.

The old man dropped the bag and the chair and went back up the hill faster than Pheng had ever seen him move. By the time Pheng got there, with the cell phone at his ear, Neng Chue Xiong was kneeling in the frost with Walt’s head on his knee, and his hand flat on Walt’s chest the way he had laid it on the heifer, and he was talking to him low in Hmong, and Walt was looking up past him at the brown leaves of the oak, and then he was not looking at anything.

Across the field, by the barn, the cows were bawling to be let out.

V. The Wardens

Saturday to Sunday night

Two deputies came, and the ambulance, which was no use, and before nine o’clock three wardens from the DNR, who walked the field in a line with their heads down. They found where Walt had fallen, and they stood there a long time looking west, toward the fence line and the woods, and then north, and then west again.

They took Tou’s rifle and Kou’s rifle, gently, with paper tags on the trigger guards, and sat the brothers at Carol’s kitchen table in their orange coats for four hours, one at a time, with a deputy in the doorway. They asked Pheng to tell it again and again. They asked the old man, through Pheng, where he had been standing, and he showed them.

By noon it was all over the county. By the time the bars in Marathon City filled up on Saturday night there was nobody in them who did not know that Walt Brandt had been shot dead on his own land on opening morning, and that Walt Brandt had let Hmong hunt his back forty, and that two Hmong had been in his back forty with rifles when it happened. Nobody needed to say the rest. A rock came through the front window of a Hmong grocery on the east side on Sunday morning. On Sunday afternoon somebody at the gas station on Highway 29 told Carol’s sister how sorry he was, and said they would hang for it this time, by God, and she said thank you.

On Sunday night the Xiong family sat on the living room floor of the house on the east side, twenty of them, more, with the curtains drawn and the porch light off. Tou had been crying, and was angry that he had been crying, and he said it to his uncle in Hmong in front of everyone.

If you had not gone out there, he would be alive. He gave us his land. He gave you his milk for seven years. And you went out and sat under his tree, and he walked you down his road, and now he is dead, and they will say it was us. They are already saying it. If you had stayed home, none of this.

The room was quiet. The old man sat on a kitchen chair with his hands folded on his knees.

If I stayed home, he said, if he stayed home, if he went north, if he went south, if he went east to the river or west to the woods, it was going to find him that morning. I did not bring it. You did not bring it. Nobody here can stop the thing that comes to a man.

He looked around the room at all of them, the brothers in their hunting clothes, the women, the children on the stairs.

Listen. A man with a gun can do that to a man. An enemy can do that to his enemy. One who hates, to the one he hates. You know this. All of us know this. But a mind that is pointed the wrong way will do worse to the man who carries it. Worse than any gun. Now everybody out there is pointing his mind. Do not point yours back.

Some of them, years later, said that was the night they stopped being afraid in the place where they had been afraid since Birchwood. They could not explain it better than that. Tou went out on the porch and turned the light back on.

VI. The Hymn

Marathon City · the Wednesday before Thanksgiving

Danny Brandt got home Tuesday night, thirty hours out of Baghdad by way of Kuwait, on emergency leave, in a uniform that still had the dust of the convoy route in its seams. He hugged his mother in the doorway for a long time. He did not cry. He walked out to the hayfield in the dark by himself and stood on the place, and came back in, and took his father’s deer rifle out of the gun cabinet in the den and cleaned it at the kitchen table, all of it, every part, and put it back together and did not put it away.

The funeral was at the Methodist church on Wednesday morning. The church was full and the basement was full and people stood along the walls. Carol had chosen the hymns. The last one was Walt’s father’s, and Walt’s.

The old man came, with Tou and Pheng, in white shirts and dark jackets, and sat in the last pew on the left. Danny saw heads turn. He saw a woman he had known all his life get up from that pew with her husband and move to the other side. He saw the man from the feed counter look at the three of them and look away. The old man sat with his hands folded on his knees and looked at the casket.

They stood for the last hymn. The organ was loud. Danny did not sing. He held the hymnal open and read the words in front of him as if they were orders.

Let goods and kindred go,
This mortal life also;
The body they may kill:
God’s truth abideth still,
His kingdom is forever.

— “A Mighty Fortress Is Our God,” Martin Luther, tr. F. H. Hedge (1853), st. 4

At the lunch in the basement, over ham sandwiches and bars and the big percolator, his uncle Gary put a hand on his shoulder and said low that the state crime lab had the bullet, and the rifles, and it was a matter of days, and everybody knew whose rifles were in that field. You let the law do it, Dan. Danny nodded.

On Thursday his mother cooked Thanksgiving dinner for eleven people because she did not know what else to do, and Danny ate, and helped with the dishes, and after dark he put on his own orange coat for the warmth of it and put his father’s rifle in the truck and drove into town.

VII. The Ancient Foe

The east side · Thanksgiving night

He parked across the street from the narrow frame house with the engine running for the heat and the rifle across his knees. He did not know what he had come to do. He had driven a fuel truck past a hundred houses like this one on the other side of the world with a rifle across his knees, and he had always known what he had come to do, and now he did not, and that frightened him more than anything had frightened him over there.

After twenty minutes the porch light came on and the old man came down the steps in a parka and house slippers and crossed the street, and opened the passenger door of the truck, and got in, and shut the door. He looked at the rifle. Then he looked out the windshield.

They sat a while.

Your father told me you drive the fuel, Neng Chue Xiong said. On the roads with the bombs.

He told you that.

Every night. Seven nights. You were the only thing he talked about that he was afraid of. The old man was quiet. On Thursday night he sang a song to the cows. The one they sang in your church.

Danny’s hands tightened on the rifle.

There is a part of the song, the old man said. Your father sang it for me twice, so I could learn it. And he said it, slowly, in his careful English, looking out the windshield at his own lit porch.

For still our ancient foe
Doth seek to work us woe;
His craft and power are great;
And armed with cruel hate,
On earth is not his equal.

— “A Mighty Fortress Is Our God,” tr. F. H. Hedge (1853), st. 1

Your father said this one is the devil. I think so. But I do not think he is out there. The old man put two fingers on his own chest, on the parka. Thirty years he lived in here. In me. I hated the ones who came up the valley. I hated them in the camp, in the church basement, on my knees in the ginseng. Every night. It did nothing to them. Nothing. They never knew. It ate me. The man in the woods killed your father’s body. Your song says it. The body they may kill. But this one, and he touched his chest again, can do worse than that, to you. He is armed with cruel hate. On earth is not his equal. He is sitting in this truck.

Danny did not say anything for a long time. Then he said, Who did it?

I don’t know.

Was it your nephews?

No.

How do you know?

The old man thought about it honestly. I know them, he said. But I did not see. It is not the question. He opened the door and the cold came in. Go home to your mother. Your cows are waiting.

Danny drove home with the rifle on the floor of the truck. In the mudroom he unloaded it and put the shells in his coat pocket and stood the rifle in the corner and hung up the orange coat on its hook, beside his father’s brown one.

•••

On Friday morning the warden called. A boy of fifteen, Kyle Hessel, from the next farm west, had come into the sheriff’s office the night before with his grandfather. At first light on opening morning he had shot at a buck running along the fence line between the two farms, and missed it, he thought, and never saw where the bullet went. He had heard on Saturday night what happened to Mr. Brandt. He had not eaten in five days. The crime lab matched the bullet to his rifle by noon. Tou and Kou got theirs back that afternoon, with the paper tags still on.

Nobody asked the old man what Walt Brandt had done, in this life or some other one, to come to such an end in his own hayfield. So he never said.

Danny drove over to the Hessel place on Saturday. The boy was sitting on the back steps in his barn coat with his arms around his knees, and when he saw who it was he stood up as if to be hit. Danny sat down on the step below him. They looked at the field for a while, where the fence line ran down to the woods. Then Danny told him the things his father had told him when he was twelve: that you keep the safety on until you see the whole animal. That you know what is behind it. That a bullet does not stop where you stop looking.

The boy cried, finally. Danny sat with him until he was done.

VIII. Opening Day

The Brandt farm · November 2006

Danny’s enlistment ran out in the summer, and he came home to the barn. That fall a family on the east side needed a cow for a grandmother’s funeral, and they called the Brandt farm, and Danny walked them through the dry cows with a flashlight and let them take as long as they wanted.

On the Saturday before Thanksgiving, after milking, he went out the mudroom door a little before six in his orange coat. Tou and Kou and Pheng were waiting by the machine shed in theirs, stamping in the cold, and they went out together down the lane in the gray toward the back forty.

The old man was sitting at the bottom of the hill under the bur oak in his folding chair, with a thermos of Carol’s coffee and a blanket on his knees and an orange stocking cap that Danny had bought him at the farm store pulled down over his ears. The leaves rattled over his head. He watched them go.

Four orange shapes, crossing the frost toward the woods, bright as a fire in the stubble, so that anybody sitting out there with a rifle across his knees could see them coming a long way off.

Background

The verse

Whatever a hater may do to a hater, or an enemy to an enemy, a wrongly-directed mind will do us greater mischief.Dhammapada 42, tr. F. Max Müller (1881)

In the commentary, Nanda is a wealthy herdsman who tends the cattle of the great lay patron Anāthapiṇḍika. For a long time he keeps inviting the Buddha to his home, and the Buddha waits until Nanda is ready. At last the Buddha sits down under a tree near Nanda’s place, and Nanda feeds the monks with the products of the cow for seven days. On the seventh day he hears a teaching and enters the stream. He carries the Buddha’s bowl a long way down the road, until the Buddha tells him to stop and turn back. On the way home a hunter’s arrow kills him. The monks say that if the Buddha had never come, Nanda would still be alive. The Buddha answers that wherever Nanda had gone, he could not have escaped that death, and speaks the verse about the wrongly directed mind. No one asks what Nanda had done in a past life, so the Buddha says nothing about it. Read the full commentary story, or about Anāthapiṇḍika, whose herds Nanda kept.

Central Wisconsin, 2005

Hmong families began arriving in Wausau as refugees in the late 1970s, most of them families of men who had fought for the CIA against the Pathet Lao and North Vietnamese in the Laotian Civil War. After 1975 tens of thousands crossed the Mekong into Thailand. Many spent years in camps such as Ban Vinai before churches and agencies resettled them in towns like Wausau, where by 2002 Hmong residents made up about an eighth of the city. Many of the first generation worked in Marathon County’s ginseng gardens, under black shade cloth. More on the Hmong in Wisconsin.

On November 21, 2004, the second day of the gun deer season, a confrontation over a tree stand on private land near Birchwood left six white hunters dead and two wounded. The shooter, Chai Soua Vang, a Hmong hunter from St. Paul, was convicted in September 2005 and sentenced that November to life without parole; the hunters and Vang gave sharply different accounts of who fired first. The killings left deep tension between white and Hmong hunters. When the 2005 season opened, Hmong hunters reported harassment, the DNR aired safety and trespass announcements in Hmong, Spanish and English, and the township near the shooting posted signs in all three languages. Wisconsin’s nine-day gun deer season opens on the Saturday before Thanksgiving.
