The Widow’s Priests

Be of the disciples of Aharon; loving peace, and pursuing peace.
— Pirqe Aboth i. 13

Assim mo contaram, on Mill Street.

I. The Basin

Mill Street, New York · the weeks of the Omer, 1706

Dona Branca Lopes lived in one room over a cooper’s shed at the low end of Mill Street, three doors from the house where the nation rented an upper floor for its synagogue. She was past seventy. She had been born at Trancoso in Portugal, in a house where her mother lit the Friday lamp inside a clay jar so that no light showed at the shutters. In that town there was no synagogue, no scroll, no reader and no priest, and a neighbor who saw clean linen on a Friday could send a family to the Holy Office.

She had come out by Bayonne and Amsterdam. A nephew in the Curaçao trade brought her to New York, and the yellow fever of 1702 took him and left her. Now she taught the children of the nation their letters, for whatever the parents sent up the stair: a loaf, a string of candle ends, half a cord of wood in the fall.

There were about a hundred Jews in the town that year, most of them Portuguese like herself, and a few Germans the Portuguese called tudescos. Her pupils were seven. The eldest was a girl of eleven, Sarah Nunes. The youngest was Leyb, five years old, whose father Hirsch was a tudesco from Fürth and made tallow candles in a shed behind the slip.

Inside her door, on a stool, stood a pewter basin and a cup. A child coming in poured water three times over each hand and dried them on the cloth. Then the child sat down on the bench and kept still. Then, when she nodded, the child said yesterday’s portion through without the book, three letters for the small ones and a line of the prayers for the big ones. When all seven had done it she looked along the bench and said the same thing every day.

Bom. Now my priests may begin.

Meus cohanim, she called them. One at a time it was meu cohen, the girls the same as the boys. She called nobody that who had not washed and sat and said the portion. There was a fourth rule under the three. If a child came up the stair with a quarrel still in him, having hit a brother or called the candlemaker’s boy a name in the street, she seemed to know it by the way he held the cup, and she sent him back down.

A priest goes up with clean hands. Go and get yours clean. The water is only to remind you.

II. The Cohen

The thirty-third day

Senhor Daniel Cohen Faro shipped flour and barrel staves to Curaçao and brought back cocoa. He had a brick house on Dock Street and a pew, such as it was, nearest the ark. He was a Cohen. His father had been one and his father’s father, and the two spread hands were cut on his grandfather’s stone at Ouderkerk. On the festivals he took off his shoes and went up before the ark with his prayer shawl over his head and blessed the congregation, and when a firstborn son was redeemed the five pieces of silver were laid in his palm. He was not a proud man about most things. About this he was careful, which he held to be different.

He passed under her window every day on his way to the waterside, and every day he heard it. Now my priests may begin. One afternoon he climbed the stair and looked in at the door. He saw a bench of children. One was a girl, and one was the tudesco’s boy, whose feet did not reach the floor.

He thought about it for a week, because he was a fair man. A widow may call children what she likes. But she was learned in her way, and the nation deferred to her on the fast days and on how a thing had been done in the old country. In six years she had never called him anything but Senhor Daniel. If the word was going so freely about Mill Street, it seemed to him it ought to stop first at the one man in the street it belonged to.

So he went up after the children had gone. She was wiping out the basin.

Dona Branca. You call these children cohanim.

I do.

I am a Cohen. From my father, and from his, back to Aaron. To you I am Senhor Daniel.

It is a good name.

I am not asking you for honors. I am asking what you mean by the word, when you give it to a candlemaker’s son and keep it from me.

She set the basin on the stool.

In my mother’s house we had no Cohen. Nobody in Trancoso knew anymore what he was. The books that said so were burned before my grandmother was born. So my mother said, whoever in this house has washed, and has done today what was his to do, and has no quarrel with anyone under the roof, and can sit down quiet, that one is our priest tonight, and he says the blessing. Some nights it was my brother. Some nights it was the girl who carried the water. Some nights it was nobody, and we ate without.

That was need. Here we have the thing itself.

Here we have your father’s name, senhor. I was fifty years in a country that called people by their fathers and their grandfathers, to the fourth generation, and looked in the registers to see whose blood was clean. I do not call anyone that way. I never learned how.

She asked him what the men had read in the synagogue on the first Sabbath afternoon after the Passover. He knew it as well as she did. She said it anyway, in the Spanish of the Amsterdam prayer book, and waited after it as she waited after a child’s portion.

Hillel said, Be of the disciples of Aharon; loving peace, and pursuing peace; loving mankind, and bringing them nigh to the Thorah.

— Pirqe Aboth i. 13

He does not say sons, she said. Hillel could count. He knew Aaron had sons. He says disciples. So wash, senhor. Do what is yours to do. Go and find whoever it is you are not speaking to, and run after peace with him until you catch it. Then come and sit down, and I will know what to call you.

He had not told her about the candlemaker. Among a hundred Jews nobody needed to be told. At Purim he had bought ship’s candles of Hirsch and found the cask a pound short, by his own steelyard, and had held back forty shillings of the price. Hirsch had called him a thief in two languages on the open street. They had not spoken since. Hirsch was a Levite, and it is the Levite who pours the water over the Cohen’s hands before he goes up to bless. At Passover Hirsch had stood in his place with his arms folded, and a firstborn had poured instead, and the whole room had watched it.

III. The Cup

The eve of the Feast of Weeks, and the morning

He counted the days with the rest of them each night after the evening prayer, the thirty-fourth, the fortieth, the forty-eighth. He did nothing. A man can turn a sentence over for two weeks and still call it thinking.

On the forty-ninth day, before sunset, he walked down to the shed behind the slip. It stank of tallow. The boy Leyb was on the floor sorting wicks by length. Daniel put forty shillings on the bench in Spanish silver and said, in Portuguese, that he had weighed that cask in a wet week, and that a wet week adds nothing to tallow but does things to a man’s temper. Hirsch looked at the money for some time. He had been expecting the constable. He answered in a Portuguese that was mostly German. He said his beam scale had been his father’s and might be a little kind to the seller. Then he took down a pound of candles, weighed it where Daniel could see the pointer, and put it on top of the money. Neither of them touched either pile. After a while the boy got up off the floor and took his father’s hand and looked at the Cohen’s shoes.

In the morning the upper room on Mill Street was hung with green boughs from the Bowery road, and the reader chanted the portion about the mountain, where every water carrier in Israel is told that the whole people shall be priests. When the time came Daniel Cohen Faro took off his shoes and went to the back of the room. Hirsch the candlemaker was already standing there with the cup. He poured three times over each hand, slowly, the way a child does who has been taught it lately. Daniel stood and looked at his own two hands with the water running off them, and he was a moment late going up.

•••

The day after the festival he climbed the widow’s stair at the children’s hour. He had no portion to say, and said so. He poured the water for himself at the stool and dried his hands on the cloth and sat down at the end of the bench beside Leyb, with his knees higher than the bench. He kept still.

Dona Branca looked along the row, the seven and the one, and nodded.

Bom, she said. Now my priests may begin.

She opened the book to the aleph.

Background

The verse

He who is thoughtful, blameless, settled, dutiful, without passions, and who has attained the highest end, him I call indeed a Brahmana.Dhammapada 386, tr. F. Max Müller (1881)

In the commentary a man who is a Brahmin by birth and lineage notices that the Buddha calls his own disciples Brahmins, and reasons that the title ought to be given to him as well. He goes and asks. The Buddha answers that he calls no one a Brahmin for birth or lineage, only the one who has reached the goal, and speaks the verse; the Brahmin is established in the first fruit of the path. Read the full commentary story. The nearest Jewish counterpart to a Brahmin by birth is the Kohen, the hereditary priest descended from Aaron, and Hillel’s saying in Pirkei Avot makes the same turn from sons to disciples. It is quoted from Charles Taylor’s Sayings of the Jewish Fathers (1897), where it is numbered i. 13; most editions count it 1:12. The tractate is traditionally read a chapter a week on the Sabbaths between Passover and Shavuot, the weeks of the counting of the Omer.

Mill Street, 1706

New York’s Jews, about a hundred people at the turn of the eighteenth century, worshipped in rented rooms, first on Beaver Street and by 1700 on Mill Street, now South William Street. They did not build a synagogue of their own until 1730. The congregation became Shearith Israel, the oldest in the United States. Most of its early families were Portuguese and Spanish Jews, many only a generation or two out of the Iberian peninsula, with a growing number of Ashkenazim, and the Gotham Center describes the friction and the shared life between the two. Dona Branca, Daniel Cohen Faro, Hirsch and the children are invented.

Branca’s childhood is the world of crypto-Judaism. After the forced conversions of the 1490s, families in Portugal kept what they could of Jewish practice in secret for two centuries, without books or clergy, and it was very often the women who carried it. Around them Iberian society sorted people by the statutes of limpieza de sangre, purity of blood, which traced a “New Christian” ancestor back through the generations. In the priestly blessing, which Spanish and Portuguese congregations perform on the festivals, the Kohanim remove their shoes and the Levites wash their hands, and a Kohen who is on bad terms with the congregation is not supposed to go up.
