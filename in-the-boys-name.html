Walking the American WayCommentary 25.6 · Pañcaggadāyaka

In the Boy's Name

In it there is nothing which is thine, Nanak preacheth.
— Guru Teg Bahadur, Slok XLI

Así me lo contaron, on the Holtville side of the Alamo River.

I. Primicias

East of Holtville, Imperial Valley · 1921 to 1933

Inder Singh came down to the Valley in 1913 with a gang of men from Jullundur to pick cotton, and stayed to grow it. By 1921 he had cut his hair, leveled eighty acres under the East Highline Canal with four mules and a Fresno scraper, and married Guadalupe Robles of Sonora, whose older sister had married his partner the spring before. The Mexicans on the place called him Andrés. The Anglos in town called him Andy, and called him and every man like him a Hindu, though he was a Sikh and had told them so.

He grew cotton for the bank. For the house he kept an acre along the head ditch in corn, melons, okra, chiles, and in winter the mustard greens of his own country, and it was that acre people talked about.

When the first of anything came ripe in it, he picked it and carried it out to the road and gave it to whoever came along. When he loaded the wagon for town, the first crate went off the tailgate at the first tent he passed. When he brought home the hundred-pound sack of flour, the first scoop out of it went down to the pickers' camp in a lard pail before the sack was leaned in its corner. When Lupe cooked, the first ladle out of the pot went out the kitchen door. And when his plate was in front of him he looked around the room and out into the yard for anybody at all, a child, a hired man, the ditch tender on his horse, and gave off the plate before he lifted his hand to it. Five times between the dirt and his mouth. He did not taste a thing until all five were done.

Lupe had been taught her catechism by a grandmother in Ures, and the fifth commandment of the Church was to pay tithes and first fruits. Primicias, she told him. God asks it once. Once, Andrés, and He is God.

Then four are from me, he said.

So the camps called him Don Primicias, and children learned to be standing in his yard at noon.

He was easy about food. He was not easy about the eighty acres. Since 1923, when the high court in Washington said that a man from India could not be a citizen, the land law of California had applied to him, and a man who could not be a citizen could not own a furrow or lease one. So the lease was written to Mr. Varney, a director of the bank in Holtville that people called the Hindu Bank because of how much ground its officers held this way, for how many men. Mr. Varney was honest. At Christmas Andrés took him two live hens.

Then in May of 1933 a grand jury sat in El Centro, and four of his countrymen were tried there with their Anglo partners for conspiring to get around the land law. By the end of summer three of the four were in San Quentin. Mr. Varney stopped coming out to look at the cotton. The lawyer in Brawley said there was one safe way left. The boy, Jesús, was seven and had been born at Holtville, and nobody could say he was not a citizen. Put everything in the boy's name. The father would stand as guardian and go before the judge once a year with his accounts.

Andrés had not slept a whole night since May. He walked the head ditch in the dark with the papers going around in him. Twenty years of his back were in that ground, and his name was nowhere on it, and now it would be on it even less.

II. The Door

The kitchen · a noon in late October 1933

They were picking. He had been at the scale since before light, and at noon he came in and sat down at the table with his back to the door, the way he always sat, because the glare off the yard hurt his eyes. Lupe set the plate down: chicken curry the way he had taught her, beans, and four rotis off the comal, which she made of his flour and went on calling tortillas.

The first of the garden had gone to the road in June. The first of the wagon had gone, and the first of the sack. The first of this pot had gone out to the weigh crew an hour ago, and the rest of the pot after it, because there were more of them than she had counted on. He looked around the kitchen for the fifth. The boy was at school. The yard, when he turned his head, had only chickens in it. He waited a little, and then he tore a roti and began.

He had eaten half the plate when the old man came to the door.

Everybody under the Highline knew him. He had come to California before any of them, and worked the railroad grades in the north, and somewhere along the way had let go of working for wages or for shares. He had no lease and no partner and no banker. He walked the canal banks from Calexico to Niland with a blanket over his shoulder and a brass bowl, in a turban washed so often it had gone the color of the sky at the edges, and he ate what he was given and did not ask for it. The men called him Baba. He came and stood, and if nothing was brought he went on.

Lupe saw him through the screen. She knew to the spoonful what was left in her kitchen, and it was on that plate. She knew her husband had been thin since May. If he turned his head now, he would be up out of the chair with the plate in his hands, and she would have nothing to put in front of him but beans.

So she stepped between them. She stood behind his chair with her back to the door and bent over him, moving the salt, spreading her elbows, a small woman trying to be as wide as a doorway. She might as well have held up her hand to cover the moon. She kept half an eye over her shoulder. Has he gone? He had not gone. She did not dare tell him to, because Andrés would hear.

When she could not stand it she backed up to the screen and said through her teeth, not turning around, Ándele. Pase.

The old man looked at her. Then he shook his head, slowly, side to side, with his lips shut, exactly the way Jesús did when she came at him with the castor oil.

It was the beard that did it. A white beard down to the middle of his chest, going left and right. The laugh came up out of her before she could get her apron over it, the kind of laugh that comes in church.

This is the part they tell carefully. As she laughed, light came in at the door past her and lay on the whitewashed wall in front of Andrés, where no light fell at that hour. It was not the white of the yard. It was blue, and yellow, and red, and a white cleaner than the wall, and the orange of a turban on a feast day, lying together like oil on ditch water, and it had the shape of a man standing.

He heard his wife laugh and saw the wall, both at once, and turned around.

Woman, I am ruined, he said. He was at my door and you let me eat.

III. The Second Half

The same noon

He carried the plate to the door and pushed the screen open with his foot.

Baba ji. I gave the first of the garden and the first of the wagon and the first of the sack and the first of the pot, and then I looked for someone and saw no one, and I sat down. I have eaten half of this. My hand has been in it. It is all there is in the house. Will you take it from me?

Any other holy man he had ever met would have looked at a half-eaten plate and remembered somewhere he had to be.

The first of the pot is good, the old man said. The middle of the pot is good. What is left on the plate is good. He said the next thing in Spanish, so that she would have it too. I am like the dead, hija. I eat what was set out for somebody else.

Lupe, who in a week would be laying bread and salt on a table for her mother and her two brothers, stopped laughing.

He took the plate in both hands, and said over it a couplet of Farid's that every man from that country knew.

Eat hard dry bread, and drink cold water;
Farid, on seeing another's buttered bread let not thy heart long for it.

— Farid, Slok XXIX

Andrés stood in his own doorway and was gladder than the five gifts had ever made him. He had given the top of everything for twelve years and been pleased with himself for it, and here was a man to whom the top and the bottom were one plate.

Baba ji, he said. At home they would call you a faqir. Here they call you a tramp, and me a Hindu, and my cotton Mr. Varney's. What does a man have to be, to be rightly called what you are?

The old man looked past him at the field, where the pickers had gone back down the rows with their long sacks dragging.

Whose land?

Mr. Varney's, on the paper. Next month my son's.

The county did that to shame you. The county does not know what it has shown you. Go on with it. This Andrés, this Inder Singh, this Hindu. This back that leveled the ground. Look for the paper with your name on it for those. When a man has looked everywhere and found no such paper, and does not go up and down the ditch at night crying for a thing that was never in the drawer, call him a faqir. He need not walk the canal.

Then he said two of the Ninth Guru's sloks, the ones read at the close, when the Book has been gone through to the end.

Why entertainest thou false pride? Know that the world is like a dream;
In it there is nothing which is thine, Nanak preacheth.

Be anxious only regarding what is not expected;
It is the way of the world, Nanak, that nothing is permanent.

— Guru Teg Bahadur, Sloks XLI and LI

Andrés had heard those lines as a boy, sitting on a swept floor behind his father, when they meant that the reading was over and there would be something sweet. Lupe had no Punjabi beyond the names of spices. But her grandmother in Ures had said one word over every broken dish and every grave, prestado, lent, and she heard the old man say it now in his own language as plainly as if he had said it in hers.

Neither of them could have told you afterward how long they stood there. The thing that had walked Andrés up and down the ditch since May was not in him. He looked for it, the way you feel for a tooth, and there was the place where it had been.

The old man sat down on the step and ate the half plate to the last bean. He washed the plate at the pump, and his bowl, and set the plate on the step, and went north along the canal bank.

IV. In the Boy's Name

El Centro · November 1933, and after

The lawyer from Brawley had seen a good many of these men sign, that fall, and knew the look they wore doing it. Andrés signed as guardian of the estate of Jesús Inder Singh, a minor, the way a man signs for a crate at the depot that is addressed to somebody else. Then he asked the clerk where a person could get a cold drink.

Once a year he put on a clean shirt and went before the judge with his accounts. He slept. He went on giving the five firsts, since there was no reason to stop, and Lupe went on telling him God asked for one. She cooked one roti over the count every noon from then on, and never mentioned it.

The law changed in 1946, and in 1949 he raised his right hand in the same courthouse and came out a citizen. The lawyer, an old man himself by then, asked whether he wanted the eighty acres moved over into his own name at last. Jesús was twenty-three and running the place.

Leave it, Andrés said.

He had turned his chair around that October. For the rest of his life he ate his noon meal facing the door, with the glare in his eyes and the plate set a little toward the edge of the table.

Background

The verse

He who never identifies himself with name and form, and does not grieve over what is no more, he indeed is called a Bhikshu.Dhammapada 367, tr. F. Max Müller (1881)

In the commentary, a Brahmin of Sāvatthī gives the first of his crop five times over: from the field, the threshing floor, the storehouse, the pot and the dish. He is called Pañcaggadāyaka, Giver of the Five First-Fruits. One day the Buddha stands at his door while he eats with his back to it. His wife, afraid he will give his dinner away, tries to hide the visitor and whispers to him to pass on; the Buddha shakes his head, she laughs aloud, a radiance falls into the house, and the Brahmin turns. He offers the half he has not eaten. The Buddha accepts it, saying that first, middle or last portion are all fit for one who lives on what others give, “like the petas,” the hungry dead. Asked what makes a bhikkhu, he speaks the verse on name-and-form, and husband and wife are both established in the path. Read the full commentary story, or about name-and-form (nāmarūpa).

The Imperial Valley, 1933

Punjabi men, most of them Sikhs, came to California's farm valleys from about 1907. Immigration law kept their families in India, and California law barred them from marrying white women, so several hundred married women of Mexican descent, often sisters marrying partners. Their households cooked chicken curry and roti beside beans and tortillas, spoke Spanish and English, and baptized their children Catholic. See Punjabi Mexican Americans and the University of California, Davis, archive on the hard years of 1923 to 1945. The nearest gurdwara was the one founded at Stockton in 1912, some five hundred miles north.

After United States v. Bhagat Singh Thind (1923) held Indians ineligible for citizenship, the California Alien Land Law barred them from owning or leasing farmland. They farmed on through verbal understandings with Anglo bankers, lawyers and farmers; a Holtville bank whose director held land for Punjabi clients was known as the “Hindu Bank.” In May 1933 an Imperial County grand jury brought a conspiracy case against four Punjabi farmers and their Anglo partners; the four were convicted, and three were sent to San Quentin before an appeals court ordered a new trial in 1934 that was never held. From 1933 on, most Punjabi farmers in the county registered as guardians of their American-born children and held the land in the children's names, as the historian Karen Leonard documented in “Punjabi Farmers and California's Alien Land Law”. The Luce–Celler Act of 1946 opened citizenship to them. The verses are from Shaikh Farid and Guru Teg Bahadur in the Sikh scripture; the Ninth Guru's sloks are read at the conclusion of a complete reading of the Guru Granth Sahib. Andrés, Lupe, Mr. Varney and the old man are invented.
