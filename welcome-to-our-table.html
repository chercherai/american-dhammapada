Welcome to Our Table

Sit down and feed, and welcome to our table.
— Shakespeare, As You Like It, II.vii

This is the way I had it, under the hill at Natchez.

I. The Trumpet

Natchez-Under-the-Hill, Mississippi · February 1836

Natchez was two towns then. The one on the bluff had the brick houses and the banks. The one under the bluff was a shelf of mud between the hill and the river, a single street called Silver Street and whatever hung off it: grog shops, dance houses, boatmen's lodgings, and at the landing an acre of flatboats tied gunwale to gunwale. A mile back of the bluff, at the Forks of the Road, the traders were selling people that winter by the hundred, and the cotton those people were bought to pick came down Silver Street on drays and went out by the same landing.

Tobe Finch and Dab Harker had come down the river in the fall as hands on a flour boat out of Cincinnati. Tobe was sixteen and Dab a year older. The cholera of thirty-two had made orphans of them both, and the township had bound them out, Tobe and his small brother Pim to a tanner and Dab to a man who made rope. They had run from their indentures the same week. There was a notice out for each of them in a Cincinnati paper, offering six cents reward, which came to a quarter of a cent less than one picayune a head.

They lived by their fingers. Dab was the hand and Tobe was the stall. Tobe went ahead into a crowd and stopped short, or stumbled, or asked a man the way to the ferry, and while the man looked at Tobe, two of Dab's fingers were in his pocket. Poor people on the river carried their small money tied in a knot, in the corner of a handkerchief or the tail of a shirt. Dab could have a knot open with one hand before its owner had finished answering about the ferry. He was proud of that. They kept two lean-tos side by side on the mud below Silver Street, Tobe and Pim in one, and in the other Dab and a girl of fifteen named Sukey whom he called his wife.

On a wet evening in February a trumpet blew on the river. A long low house was coming in to the landing on a flatboat hull, a hundred feet of it, with a flag at the bow that said THEATRE and a man on the roof with the trumpet. It was the Fenno family, eleven of them counting sons' wives, who had left Pittsburgh on the fall rise and played every landing down two rivers. An English family named Chapman had been first on the water with a theatre boat, and the Fennos did as the Chapmans did. Four bits a head, or a peck of anything that could be eaten. That night, As You Like It.

Two hundred people on benches in the dark, Dab said, all looking the same way.

II. The Banquet

Aboard the Floating Theatre · that night

They got in by the stern while the trumpet was still going. The room was as narrow as a covered bridge. There were plank benches, and a row of tallow candles in tin reflectors across the front of a stage no bigger than a wagon bed. The benches were full of Kentucky boatmen, draymen, steamboat hands, clerks down from the bluff, men with rifles on their way to Texas, and women from the dance houses. At the back by the door, where colored people were let to stand, there were hack drivers and market women with their passes.

The plan was the usual one. When the place was full and the play well along, Tobe would go up the gangway between the benches and block it, and Dab would come along behind him through the standing people, working.

Tobe got as far as the middle of the gangway. On the stage an old servant had lain down in a forest that was painted on a bedsheet, saying that he could go no further, that he was dying for food. Tobe had Pim at home. He stopped to hear what became of the old man, and he never went any further up the gangway that night.

What became of him was this. A duke who had been turned out of his own country was sitting down to supper in the forest with his people. The supper was real. It was the Fennos' own, a pone and a fried catfish and a dish of yams laid out on a plank, because the company could not afford to set a table twice in one evening. Before any of them had touched it, the old servant's young master ran in among them with a sword in his hand.

ORLANDO. Forbear, and eat no more.

DUKE SENIOR. What would you have? Your gentleness shall force
More than your force move us to gentleness.

ORLANDO. I almost die for food, and let me have it.

DUKE SENIOR. Sit down and feed, and welcome to our table.

— Shakespeare, As You Like It, II.vii

The young man with the sword did not know what to do then. Tobe did not either. He had been a thief for two years, and in that time he had been chased and cuffed, and locked a night in a smokehouse, and preached at once from the tail of a wagon. Nobody had ever answered him like that. He had not known there was such an answer. The young man said he had thought all things were savage here. He said, I blush and hide my sword, and went to carry in the old man on his back, and the Duke would not let anybody eat until the two of them had come.

Tobe stood in the gangway through the rest of it, with boatmen cursing him to sit down. He heard the clown, too. The clown was old Mrs. Fenno in a coat made all of patches. Her sons were wanted for the lovers and the wrestler, so she played the fools, and it was said along the river that there was no better one between Pittsburgh and the Balize.

Dab waited at the back for a stall who did not come. Then he worked by himself, as well as he could with nobody to draw eyes. By the door stood an old Black drayman who hired his own time from his master and had his week's small money knotted in the tail of his shirt. Dab had the knot open and shut again while the old man was laughing at the clown.

On the bank afterward he opened his hand under a pine-knot torch. Five picayunes.

Where were you?

I was hearing it, Tobe said.

III. The Cold Hearth

Below Silver Street · the next day

Five picayunes bought meal and a catfish and a twist of coffee. By noon there was a fire in front of Dab's lean-to, and Sukey had the fish in the skillet and dodgers in the ashes.

There was no fire in front of the other. Tobe had been at the landing since daylight asking for work. The cotton there was rolled by men whose masters were paid for them, and nobody would give a boy a picayune to do what he already owned a man to do. A storekeeper let him sweep and then said he had not asked him to. He had walked among a hundred pockets that morning with his hands at his sides, and he could not have told anyone why, only that the thing was finished. Pim sat on the sill of the lean-to with his knees up, watching Sukey's skillet.

Here he is, Dab said. Here's the scholar. He went to the play and got so wise he can't find his dinner.

Don't it smell good, Solomon? said Sukey. You're so almighty wise now, how is it there's nothing cooking at your house?

Dab laughed until he had to put down his cup. He was the one with fish in the skillet, and it stood to reason that he was the one with sense.

Tobe looked at him across the two yards of mud. He thought: he does not know. He is as big a fool as I was the day before yesterday, and he takes himself for the cleverest man under the hill, and that is all that ails him. I didn't know it of myself either, until last night.

He took Pim by the hand and went down to the boat.

IV. The Coat of Patches

Aboard the Floating Theatre · that afternoon

By day the theatre was a house like any other. There was washing on the roof. One of the sons was painting a bedsheet into a palace. Old Mr. Fenno, who was the Duke at night, was the cook by day, and he had the plank set up on the stage and was laying dinner on it for eleven. Mrs. Fenno sat on the edge of the stage with the clown's coat across her knees, sewing a red patch over a worn green one.

It was said of her that off the stage she did not use six words in a day, and that when she did speak it was a line out of some play if she could find one to fit.

Tobe stood in the gangway where he had stood the night before, holding his brother's hand, and told her. He told her that he and another had come aboard to rob her house, and what his part was to have been, and that he had not done it because he was hearing the play. He told her about the five picayunes and whose they were. He told her about the cold hearth, and what Dab and Sukey had said to him across the mud. He says I've got so wise I can't get my dinner. He thinks it, too.

Mrs. Fenno bit off her thread. She looked at him a good while, and then at the small boy. Then she gave him the line, in the clown's voice, which was her own voice.

The fool doth think he is wise, but the wise man knows himself to be a fool.

— Shakespeare, As You Like It, V.i

She shook out the coat and held it up to the light from the door to see how the new patch sat. Then she moved along the edge of the stage to make room.

Old Mr. Fenno had counted the two of them already. He set down two more tin plates on the plank and said the other line, the one he said every night to a young man with a sword.

Sit down and feed, and welcome to our table.

They sat down. The sons and the sons' wives came in from the roof and the river and sat down too, and nobody at that table asked the boys a question until the fish was gone.

Tobe carried wood and water for the Fennos until dark, and Mr. Fenno paid him a picayune. He took out his handkerchief and tied the coin into the corner of it. It took him three tries. He had helped open a thousand knots, and he had never before had anything to tie in one.

Background

The verse

The fool who knows his foolishness, is wise at least so far. But a fool who thinks himself wise, he is called a fool indeed.Dhammapada 63, tr. F. Max Müller (1881)

In the commentary two thieves, close companions, go with a great crowd to the Jetavana monastery where the Buddha is teaching. They are “knot-breakers,” pickpockets who undo the knots in which people tie up their coins. One of them listens and is changed; the other finds five small coins tied in the hem of a man's garment and takes them. The next day food is cooked as usual in the thief's house and none in his friend's, and the thief and his wife jeer at the friend for being so wise that he cannot get a meal. He reflects that the man takes himself for wise only because he is a fool, goes with his family to the Buddha and tells him, and the Buddha speaks the verse. Read the full commentary story.

Natchez-Under-the-Hill and the theatre boats, 1836

Natchez-Under-the-Hill was the landing below the bluff at Natchez, known up and down the Mississippi for its gambling rooms, dance houses and boatmen; a traveler said you could see an acre of flatboats lying there at any time. In July 1835 a mob at Vicksburg, upriver, hanged five gamblers, and committees in other river towns moved against theirs. Natchez in those years was also one of the great markets of the domestic slave trade, centered on the Forks of the Road east of town. Some enslaved men in the river towns, draymen among them, were allowed to hire their own time and keep what they earned above a fixed payment to their owners. The smallest coin that passed on the lower river was the picayune, the Spanish half-real, worth six and a quarter cents. Northern masters did advertise for runaway apprentices at a derisive “six cents reward.” In February 1836 volunteers for the war in Texas were passing down the river.

The first American showboat was the Floating Theatre built at Pittsburgh in 1831 by the English actor William Chapman, who with his family of nine drifted down the Ohio and Mississippi each season, playing at the landings for fifty cents or its worth in produce, and sold the boat at New Orleans. The Fennos and their boat are invented after that pattern. As You Like It had long been played on American stages. The lines here are from Act II, scene vii, where the starving Orlando bursts in on the banished Duke's supper, and from Act V, scene i, where the clown Touchstone quotes “a saying” to a country fellow who has just told him, “Ay, sir, I have a pretty wit.”
