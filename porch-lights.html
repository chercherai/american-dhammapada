Porch Lights

But all things excellent are as difficult as they are rare.
— Spinoza, Ethics, tr. R. H. M. Elwes

Here is how I got it, at the dark end of Salvia Court.

I. The Round

Arvada, Colorado · Thursday, January 9, 1986

The club had observed on the Saturday nearest the new moon every month since 1969, and since November those Saturdays had belonged to the comet. By January Halley’s was low in the southwest after supper, a smudge you could find in binoculars if somebody showed you where. In November sixty people had come out to the end of Salvia Court to be shown. In December it was three hundred, with cars parked down both sides of the street as far as the stop sign.

Gary Ochoa was the club’s secretary. He was thirty-four, a letter carrier out of the Arvada post office, and he had been made secretary because he could tell you a week later what everyone had said. On Thursday he finished his route at three, kept his parka on, and started up Salvia Court with a clipboard.

There were fourteen houses on the court. Mrs. Vogel’s was the last on the west side, where the pavement ended at a wire fence and the grass ran on toward the foothills. That was the direction the comet was in, and it was the only dark direction there was. The Astronomical League had been after every town in the country for a year to turn its lights down on the good nights. It called the campaign Dark Skies for Comet Halley. Gary’s part of the campaign was fourteen doorbells.

He asked the same thing at each one. Would they mind leaving the porch light off on Saturday, and the garage floods, from half past five until eight? The Akerses said sure. The widow in the split-level said she would if he would come and tell her when it was safe to turn it on again. A man named Dufresne, who had the big yard light on a pole, said he paid Public Service for that light and he would think about it. The ninth house had been empty and for sale since August, and the realtor left its porch light burning day and night. Gary wrote her number down off the sign.

He was coming down the walk of the eleventh house when Del Rainey’s Suburban pulled to the curb beside him.

Del owned three muffler shops and had the best eye in the club. He could star-hop to a twelfth-magnitude galaxy without a chart while other men were still leveling their tripods. In the back of the Suburban, with the seats folded down, lay seventeen and a half inches of mirror in a red cardboard tube the size of a water heater.

Gary. Tell Ruth something for me. He did not turn off the engine. Starting this new moon, I’m holding my own observing. Apart from Ruth, and apart from the club. Meetings too. I’ve got bylaws typed.

He had leased forty acres out past Deer Trail, seventy miles east, where there was no light from one edge of the sky to the other. He had five rules, and he gave them to Gary through the window. The dark site only. Members only, and no public nights. Nobody under sixteen. No instrument under ten inches. And you came at dusk and stayed until dawn, because he was not going to have headlights at midnight.

Three hundred people, Gary. Breathing on my eyepieces, asking me is that it. I’ve got five men who can find things for themselves. That’s a club.

Then he drove out of the court. Gary did the last three houses, because he had said he would.

II. The Garage

Mrs. Vogel’s · the same evening

Ruth Vogel was sixty-three. During the war the Army had run short of roof prisms for its gunsights, and eighty amateur telescope makers around the country had made them in cellars and garages, twenty-eight thousand prisms in all. Her father, in Dayton, was one of the eighty, and from the time she was nineteen Ruth had done his fine grinding. Afterward she ground eyeglass lenses for a wholesale house on Larimer Street until her eyes were sixty. On Tuesday and Thursday nights she taught mirror making in her garage to anyone who came. There were four oil drums filled with sand to work on, and a tester at the back made from a coffee can, a flashlight bulb and a razor blade.

She was at the tester when Gary came in. A boy named Kevin, who was thirteen, was at the near drum pushing a six-inch disk of glass back and forth across another one.

Gary told it the way he would have read back the minutes.

Mrs. Vogel. This afternoon, after my route, I took the clipboard and went up the court to ask about the lights. And while I was going up the court asking about the lights, Del Rainey saw me. He pulled over to where I was, and he said this to me: Starting this new moon, I’m holding my own observing, apart from Ruth and apart from the club. Meetings too. He stopped. He means it. Saturday he’s going to split the club in two.

She kept her eye at the razor blade until she had finished what she was looking at. Then she switched off the little bulb.

That was easy for him, she said. You couldn’t have done it. You’d have been a week getting your mouth open. And what you did today, the fourteen doorbells, Del couldn’t do if you held a gun on him. People say doing right is hard and doing wrong is easy. It depends a good deal on who’s doing it.

Kevin had stopped to listen. She went over and looked at his glass under the lamp.

You’re through with the two-twenty. Before you open the three-twenty you wash everything. The glass, the tool, the drum, your apron. Your fingernails. One grain of the coarse on the fine and you’ve put a scratch across forty hours. You don’t have to be careless to do it. You just have to not be careful, once.

She nodded at the long white tube standing in the corner, the six-inch the club kept for anybody at a public night who had come without a telescope.

Del made the mirror in that. Nineteen seventy-one, on that drum you’re at. He had it polished out, and there was one grain of the one-twenty in his shirt cuff. I made him finish it anyway. You can’t see the scratch at the eyepiece. It throws a little light around, that’s all. He never could look through it afterward. He gave it to the club the day his next one came back from the coater.

Kevin went to the laundry tub with the nail brush. Over the tub, where everyone who ground glass in that garage sooner or later stood scrubbing, was an index card gone brown, typed on her father’s machine in 1942. He had told her that the man who wrote it ground lenses for his living in a rented room, and that the dust had helped to kill him. Kevin read it with the water running, as they all did.

If the way which I have pointed out as leading to this result seems exceedingly hard, it may nevertheless be discovered. Needs must it be hard, since it is so seldom found. How would it be possible, if salvation were ready to our hand, and could without great labour be found, that it should be by almost all men neglected? But all things excellent are as difficult as they are rare.

— Spinoza, Ethics, Part V, Prop. XLII, note, tr. R. H. M. Elwes

What do I tell him? Gary said.

Tell him Saturday is at half past five, the same as it would have been. She turned the little bulb back on. And call that realtor.

III. The New Moon

Salvia Court · Saturday, January 11

At a quarter past five Gary walked the court once more, without the clipboard. The sun was behind the mountains and the cars had begun to come.

The Akerses’ light went off while he was still on their walk. The widow’s went off when he rang. At the house for sale he stood on Mr. Akers’s stepladder and backed the porch bulb out two turns, as the realtor had said he might. Up and down the court the windows went dim one after another as people pulled their drapes. By half past five there were thirteen dark porches and Dufresne’s yard light, blue-white on its pole, laying the shadow of his spruce the whole way across the street.

The telescopes stood in a row along the wire fence with their backs to it. There were two hundred people by six and more behind them. The bright thing low in the southwest was Jupiter, and half the line took it for the comet and had to be told. Mrs. Vogel ran the six-inch with the scratched mirror, because it was the one the children could reach.

At twenty to six the yard light went out. Nobody had gone to the door again. Gary saw Dufresne come down his driveway in a car coat and get on the end of the line.

A girl of nine or so got up on the step stool and put her eye where she was shown. She was quiet for some time.

Is that it?

That’s it, Mrs. Vogel said.

It looks like somebody breathed on the glass.

It does. It’s more than a hundred million miles from here tonight. It comes back in 2061. How old will you be?

The girl worked it out on the stool with her mittens off. Eighty-four.

Then look a little longer, Mrs. Vogel said, and the line waited.

Out past Deer Trail it was eleven degrees and black down to the ground. Del Rainey had the better sky that night, and nobody on Salvia Court ever said otherwise. In seventeen and a half inches the comet had a tail. There were five men there to see it, and they had each seen it by seven. In the spring two of them came back to the garage, and found their drums where they had left them.

At ten to eight the comet went down behind the foothills. Mrs. Vogel put the cap on the six-inch. Gary stood at the fence and watched the court. For a minute it stayed as it was. Then Dufresne’s light came on, and the widow’s, and after that the rest, up one side and down the other, as each house got home and decided.

Background

The verse

Bad deeds, and deeds hurtful to ourselves, are easy to do; what is beneficial and good, that is very difficult to do.Dhammapada 163, tr. F. Max Müller (1881)

The commentary story is a single paragraph. Devadatta, the Buddha’s cousin, has made up his mind to break the community in two. He meets the elder Ānanda on his morning round for alms and tells him that from that day he will keep the observance day, the gathering held at each new and full moon, and carry on the community’s business apart from the Buddha and apart from the monks. Ānanda goes back and repeats it word for word. The Buddha answers with a solemn utterance, that good is easy for the good and hard for the bad, and bad is easy for the bad and hard for the noble, and then with the verse. In the older account in the monastic code, Devadatta’s occasion for leaving was a list of five stricter rules that he wanted laid on everyone. Read the full commentary story. Arvada, 1986

The 1986 return of Halley’s Comet was the least favorable on record, with the comet on the far side of the sun from the earth at its brightest. For the northern states the best evening views came in the first half of January, before it sank into the twilight ahead of its passage round the sun on February 9: a dim patch low in the southwest, best found with binoculars, on the moonless nights around the new moon of January 10. Light pollution kept many people from seeing it at all. The Astronomical League, the federation of American amateur clubs, ran a campaign it called Dark Skies for Comet Halley, asking towns to dim their outdoor lights on the best nights. The comet is due back in July 2061. Arvada is a suburb on the northwest side of Denver. The club, the court and everyone on it are invented.

Grinding a telescope mirror by hand, glass on glass over a weighted drum with finer and finer grit, has been a hobby in America since the 1920s, and the knife-edge test can be made with a small lamp and a razor blade. A single coarse grain carried over into a finer stage will scratch the work. During the Second World War Albert Ingalls of Scientific American organized amateur mirror makers to supply roof prisms for military instruments; about eighty of them made more than twenty-eight thousand. Spinoza earned his living grinding lenses. The words on the card are the last lines of his Ethics, in R. H. M. Elwes’s translation of 1883.
