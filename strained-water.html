Strained Water

The fire is quenched when the fuel fails, but the desire ceases not.
— St. John of the Cross, The Ascent of Mount Carmel

So it was told in the kitchens, along the Port Tobacco River.

I. The Linen

Charles County, Maryland · June 1791

The spring below the Edelen house ran clear all winter and muddy every summer, after the June rains, and in the muddy months it was full of wigglers. Phillis, who cooked for the Edelens and had cooked for them since before the war with England, strained it for the table through a square of old linen tied over the mouth of the pitcher, and when Miss Monica was a little girl she stood at Phillis's elbow on a stool and watched the wigglers caught in the cloth, kicking, and asked every time whether they would live, and every time Phillis turned the linen out over the grass by the spring so that they could go back.

So when Monica Edelen was nineteen and was to be married, and the old priests were coming to the wedding, she would let nobody strain the water for them but herself. She said it was because they were holy men. Phillis thought it was because she liked to.

She was marrying Raphael Boarman, the second son from up the Wicomico, a tall, handsome, restless young man who had spent a winter at Annapolis studying law and come home having studied cards and dice instead, and owing a man named Dorsey sixty pounds, and hating him. He had looked at Monica at Mass at the manor at Easter and had not been able to look at anything else since. Her father said that was a fine thing in a young man and would settle him. Phillis, who was in the kitchen yard the day he came to ask, and saw how he looked at the girl through the window, said nothing, because it was not hers to say.

The wedding was set for the Tuesday after Corpus Christi. The old Reverend Mr. Hagan would marry them. He had baptized Monica. He had baptized her father.

II. The Priest

St. Thomas Manor, on the Port Tobacco River

He was seventy-one. He had been born in St. Mary's County and sent over the sea as a boy, because no Catholic could keep a school in Maryland then, to the English Jesuit college at St. Omer in Flanders, and from there to Liège, and had come home a priest of the Society of Jesus and ridden the circuit of the Catholic houses for twenty years, saying Mass in parlors with the shutters closed. Then in 1773 the Pope put an end to the Society. The old priests of Maryland went on living exactly as before, in the same brick house on the bluff at St. Thomas, riding the same circuits, and only were not Jesuits any more. They had had a bishop since the summer before, the first one in the country, and Mr. Hagan said that was very well and went on riding.

He was a lean man with the gout in his left knee, and he spoke so little at table that visitors thought him slow. He had one book in his saddlebag that was not a breviary: a little Spanish book, worn soft, that he had bought from a Carmelite friar in Antwerp forty years before. He read it on horseback at a walk.

The summer before, four Carmelite nuns had come up the bay from Norfolk in a sloop, from their convent in Flanders, three of them Maryland women going home, and had taken a house and farm in the woods north of Port Tobacco. They were the first in the country. They kept the enclosure, and nobody saw their faces but through a grille, and Mr. Hagan rode over twice in the week to hear their confessions. People asked him what the nuns did all day. They rest, he said. People took that for one of his jokes.

One of the nuns was Monica Edelen's mother's cousin. Monica sent her a cheese.

III. The Pitcher

The Edelen house · the Tuesday after Corpus Christi

They were married in the parlor in the morning with the shutters open, which Monica's grandmother said she had not lived to see in that house until that day. Then the long tables were set out on the grass under the poplars, and the old gentlemen of the county sat down at them in their wigs, the Neales and the Brookes and the Digges and the Boarmans and the Hamersleys, and five old priests in black at the head, and a fiddler, a man named Jerry who belonged to the Brookes, played from a kitchen chair under the trees.

It was the bridegroom's part to see to the old men. To carry the chairs and pour the wine and stand behind his father-in-law's chair and hear old stories about the French war. Raphael Boarman did none of it. He stood in the parlor door with his arms crossed and watched his wife.

She went up and down the tables with the pitcher. She stopped beside each old priest and poured the spring water into his cup through her square of linen, slowly, and turned the linen out on the grass behind the chair, and went on to the next, in her wedding gown with her sleeves turned back. And Raphael Boarman watched her go, and come back, and go, and something got up in him that he had felt at the dice table in Annapolis at two in the morning, only worse, a heat that started in his chest and went down his arms into his hands, and he made up his mind. When she came past the door again he would take her in his arms, there, in front of all of them, the wigs and the priests and the fiddler and her grandmother. Let them look.

She came up the grass with the pitcher. Phillis saw it, from the kitchen door, and told it after for forty years, and was never once contradicted by anyone who had been there.

Monica walked up the grass toward her husband with the pitcher in both hands, and she passed within a yard of him, close enough to touch his sleeve, and he did not see her. He looked straight through her at the poplars. She went by him into the house and came out again with the pitcher full, and passed him again, and he did not see her. Every soul at the tables saw her. The fiddler saw her. Her grandmother saw her. Raphael Boarman stood in the door with his arms half out from his sides, like a man feeling for a wall in the dark, and turned his head this way and that, and could not find his wife anywhere in the world.

He went white. He looked down the long tables for her, and what he found instead was the old priest at the head of them, looking back at him. Mr. Hagan had his cup in his hand and his bad leg out straight on a cushion, and he was the only person at the tables who was not looking at the bride.

Raphael went down the grass to him as if he were being pulled.

Where is she?

Sit down, Raphael.

He sat. The old man looked at him a long time, the way he looked at a horse before he bought it.

There is no fire like that one, he said. You have seen a tobacco barn burn. That is nothing to it. A barn burns down, and then it is out. He turned the cup in his hand. And there is no throw at the dice so bad as hating a man. You lost sixty pounds to Dorsey. You have lost more than that to him since, every night you lie awake hating him, and you will go on losing it as long as you keep the dice in your hand. And there is no ache like this. He touched his own knee, and then, lightly, the young man's hand. This. All of it. The whole box we carry about. You will find that out.

And there is no happiness like rest. He looked away north, toward the woods past Port Tobacco where the nuns were. None. People laugh when I say so.

Then he said that a Spanish friar had written it better than he could, two hundred years ago, and he said it in Spanish, slowly, and then said it again in English for the young man, looking for the words, the way he had been translating it to himself on horseback for forty years.

The desires are, as it is commonly said, like fire which burns when supplied with fuel, but which, when the fuel is consumed, immediately dies. In truth, the desire is in a much worse condition: the fire is quenched when the fuel fails, but the desire ceases not with the matter on which it fed while it raged, even though that be utterly consumed; for instead of ceasing, like fire when the fuel is burnt out, the desire pines away in weariness, for hunger is increased, and food diminished.

— St. John of the Cross, The Ascent of Mount Carmel, I.6

Raphael Boarman sat at the table with his hands flat on the cloth. Nobody near them spoke. The fiddler had stopped, though nobody had told him to. Whatever had got up in the young man in the doorway sat down again, Phillis said, the way a dog does when it hears its name, and he put his face in his hands, and when he took them away he was quiet all through, quiet as she had never seen a white man quiet who was not dead.

The old man nodded at something behind him.

Monica was standing at his shoulder with the pitcher and the linen. She had been standing there all along, and had heard every word of it, and her face, Phillis said, was as quiet as his. She poured her husband a cup of the spring water through the cloth, slowly, the way she poured it for the priests, and he looked at her as if he had never seen her before, and drank it.

IV. The Square of Linen

Charles County · afterward

They were married fifty-one years. Raphael Boarman never threw dice again, and paid Dorsey his sixty pounds in the fall, in person, at Annapolis, and shook his hand, and came home, and Dorsey told it at every tavern on the Severn as the strangest thing that had ever happened to him. Monica kept the square of linen. When the water was muddy she still strained it herself, and when there were grandchildren she stood them on a stool at her elbow and let them watch the wigglers kick in the cloth, and turned it out on the grass.

Mr. Hagan rode the circuit four more years. He died at St. Thomas in the winter of 1795 and was buried in the graveyard on the bluff, and his little Spanish book went to the nuns in the woods. They kept it until they went away to Baltimore in 1831, and took it with them.

Phillis told the story in the kitchen at the Edelen place and in the quarter on Saturday nights, and at the manor at St. Thomas, where her husband Ned belonged to the priests, so that she was given leave to walk over on Saturdays to see him. She told it the same way every time. She would come to the part where the young man could not find his wife, and stop, and say that she had known a good many men who could not see what was a yard in front of them, but that was the first time she ever saw one find it out. Then she would tell how the old priest put out his hand and touched the young man's hand, and say that was the whole of the sermon. The rest was talk.

Monica Boarman's square of linen is said to be in the family still, washed so many times that it is thin as a cobweb, and brown at the center from all the spring water that ever went through it, and folded small in a drawer, the way she folded it, with the four corners in.

Background

The verse

There is no fire like passion; there is no losing throw like hatred; there is no pain like this body; there is no happiness higher than rest.Dhammapada 202, tr. F. Max Müller (1881)

In the commentary, the parents of a young woman of good family invite the Buddha and his monks to her wedding. The bride goes back and forth straining water for the monks and serving them, while the bridegroom stands and stares at her until desire overpowers him; he neglects the Buddha and the eighty great elders and makes up his mind to take her in his arms. The Buddha sees his thought and makes it so that he can no longer see her. Gazing instead at the Buddha, the young man hears that there is no fire like passion, no wrong like hatred, no suffering like the aggregates of body and mind, and no happiness like peace. Both bride and groom enter the stream, and the Buddha lets them see each other once more. Monks in the Buddha's tradition strain their drinking water through cloth so as not to kill small creatures in it. Read the full commentary story.

The old priest's friar is St. John of the Cross (1542–1591), the Spanish Carmelite reformer and companion of Teresa of Ávila. The passage is from the sixth chapter of the first book of The Ascent of Mount Carmel, on what the desires do to the soul, in David Lewis's translation of 1864 as revised in the 1906 edition.

Southern Maryland, 1791

Maryland was founded as a refuge for English Catholics, but by the eighteenth century its Catholics could not vote, hold office, worship in public or keep schools, so wealthy families sent their sons across the Atlantic to the Jesuit college at St. Omer and on to Liège, as John Carroll did. Jesuits rode a circuit of private chapels from plantation headquarters such as St. Thomas Manor on the bluff above the Port Tobacco River. The suppression of the Society of Jesus in 1773 left them priests without an order; the Society was restored in the United States at St. Thomas in 1805. Carroll was consecrated the first American bishop in August 1790. The Maryland Jesuits held enslaved people on their estates, among them some of the 272 men, women and children the province sold in 1838.

In July 1790 four Discalced Carmelite nuns sailed up the Chesapeake from Norfolk: Mother Bernardina Matthews, her nieces Ann Teresa and Susanna Matthews, all Maryland-born and trained in the Low Countries, and the Englishwoman Frances Dickenson. On October 15, 1790, the feast of St. Teresa, they moved onto a farm north of Port Tobacco, the first community of Catholic nuns in the new United States. Their house too was worked by enslaved people, forty-one of them in 1820. After 1830 the community moved to Baltimore. The Edelens, the Boarmans, Phillis, and Mr. Hagan in this story are invented.

After the story of a certain maiden of family from the Dhammapada commentary (verse 202). The original. St. John of the Cross, The Ascent of Mount Carmel, tr. David Lewis (1864; ed. 1906). Written by Claude Opus 5.5.
