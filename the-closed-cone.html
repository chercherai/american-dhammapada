The Closed Cone

During the calm season of Indian summer the fire creeps quietly along the ground, feeding on the needles and cones.
— John Muir, The Yosemite

Here's how I heard it, at the Madison amphitheater.

I. The Cone

Mammoth Hot Springs, Yellowstone · June 1988

On the last night of training, Ardis Hollister gave each of the new seasonals one thing.

She was sixty-four and had taught botany for thirty years at the college in Kearney, Nebraska, and spent twenty-six summers in the park in the gray shirt and the flat hat, and she did it every year. A girl from Ohio got a feather from a raven. A Navajo kid from Window Rock who wanted to be a geologist got a chip of obsidian. A retired dentist from Billings got a piece of elk antler that a porcupine had gnawed. Kyle Nordquist got a lodgepole cone.

It was small and brown and hard as a stone, shut tight, glazed with old resin. He turned it over in his hand.

It's waiting for something, Ardis said. Carry it around until you know what.

Kyle was twenty-four and from Brainerd, Minnesota. He had done one year at the Lutheran seminary in St. Paul and quit in May, because for two years before that he had prayed every morning and every night and something that used to be on the other end of it was not there anymore, and he could not see standing up in a pulpit in that condition. His mother told people he was taking a year. He had taken a job talking to tourists about bison.

That last night Ardis read them something in a meeting room at Mammoth, with the folding chairs in rows and the lights half off. It was from John Muir, about a pine in the Sierra that was a cousin to the lodgepoles outside. She read it the way she said everything, without any extra in her voice.

During the calm season of Indian summer the fire creeps quietly along the ground, feeding on the needles and cones; arriving at the foot of a tree, the resiny bark is ignited and the heated air ascends in a swift current, increasing in velocity and dragging the flames upward.

— John Muir, The Yosemite, ch. 6

Then she closed the book and said goodnight, and everybody went up the stairs into the long June dusk with their feathers and rocks.

II. Smoke

Madison Junction · July and August

They posted him at Madison, where the Firehole and the Gibbon come together, to give the evening program at the amphitheater and rove the campground in the day. Every morning before his shift he sat on a log by the river with the cone in his hand, the way he used to sit with a Bible open on his knee, and waited for it to tell him what it was waiting for.

It did not tell him anything. It was a pinecone. He knew what it was waiting for, in the sense that he had read it on a sign. That was not what she meant, and he knew that too, and the knowing did not go anywhere. It was the same as the praying. He would sit, and the river would go by, and his mind would go off after a girl at the Old Faithful Inn, or his father's face when he told him about the seminary, or what he was going to say about bison that night, and he would bring it back to the cone, and it would go off again.

It was the driest summer anyone in the park could remember. The fires had started in June, lightning fires, and by the park's policy they were allowed to burn, as they had been since the early seventies, because the forest had burned before there were rangers and would burn after. In the middle of July the policy was suspended and everything was ordered fought. By then it was too late to matter much. On July 22 a fire started by accident just past the west boundary, and the smoke came over Madison every afternoon after that, yellow and then brown, and the sun went down red as a stoplight.

At the amphitheater people stood up during his program and asked him why the Park Service was letting Yellowstone burn down. A man from Ohio told him he was a disgrace to the uniform. Kyle said what he had been told to say, about fire ecology, and the cones, and he did not believe a word of it, because it was not his.

In the middle of August he called Ardis from the pay phone at the Madison station. He said he thought he'd been given the wrong thing. Maybe he could have the elk, or the night sky, or something that moved. He said he had sat with the cone for two months and nothing had happened.

There was a long pause on the line. Come up to Mammoth on your day off, she said. We'll find you something else.

III. The Road

Between Madison and Norris · August 20, 1988

His day off was a Saturday. He started north at noon in his Datsun with the cone in his shirt pocket, and by the time he was past the Gibbon Falls the wind was blowing so hard it rocked the car on its springs, and the sky over the plateau to the west had gone the color of a bruise. Later they called it Black Saturday. The fires in and around the park doubled in a single afternoon.

A ranger in a yellow shirt had a sawhorse across the road at a pullout and was turning everyone back. Kyle parked. He did not turn back. He walked up a little rise of rock above the road to see.

Below him the fire was coming through the lodgepoles toward the river, and it was the thing Ardis had read to them in June. It came along the ground first, low and quick and almost quiet under the roar of the wind, eating the needles and the cones and the dead twigs, every one of them, the way a hand goes along a row. When it reached the foot of a tree it climbed, and the whole tree went up at once in a column of flame twice its height, with a sound like a sheet shaken out, and then it was gone, and the next tree went, and the next. Small and large, it made no difference to it. It did not skip the twigs to get to the trees. It did not stop at the trees because they were big. It went on.

He stood there with the heat on his face. He thought of all the mornings on the log, and of what his mind had gone after every morning instead of the cone: the girl at the inn and his father and the man from Ohio and the seminary and the wrong thing he had been given, which he had wanted to trade for a better one. Small and large. He had been carrying all of it, choosing among it, setting some down and keeping the rest.

He looked, and kept looking, and did not choose. Whatever came up he let the looking go along it, the way the fire went along the ground, and it went. He thought of his father, and it went. He thought, this is it, and that went.

He put his hand on his pocket. The cone was warm from his body and from the air. On a burned branch at his feet, three cones from the tree above had come down hot, and while he watched, one of them opened, scale by scale, with a small ticking sound he could hear even in that wind.

When he looked up, Ardis was standing on the road below him in her flat hat.

There was no car. She was on the yellow line beyond the sawhorse with the burning trees behind her, and the light was all around her and on her, as if it came out of her and not the fire. The ranger at the barricade did not turn his head. Kyle said later that she did not shout, and that he heard her anyway.

Small and large, she said. It doesn't pick.

Then the smoke came across the road, and when it cleared there was the sawhorse and the ranger and nothing else.

IV. Mammoth

That evening

The seasonals tell the rest at the amphitheater, after the visitors have gone. The road north of the sawhorse was closed the rest of that day. The ranger who held it swore nobody went past him, on foot or otherwise, and Kyle's Datsun sat on the pullout for two days under a quarter inch of ash. All the same, at a little after six that evening he knocked on the door of Ardis Hollister's trailer at Mammoth, thirty miles away, with no soot on his shoes.

She opened the door in her socks. She was not surprised to see him, or did not act it. She had been home all afternoon, she said, listening to the radio.

He took the cone out of his pocket and held it out to her. It was open, every scale standing back from the core like a little tree.

She took it and tapped it on the doorframe, and the seeds came out into her hand, four or five of them, each one with its thin brown wing.

Well, she said.

He did not ask for the elk.

•••

Snow fell on September 11, a quarter inch, and the fires lay down. That winter people said Yellowstone was ruined. In July of 1989, which was her last summer, Ardis drove Kyle and the new seasonals out to the burn above Madison and had them kneel in the black and count the seedlings inside a square ten paces on a side that she marked off with four tent stakes and a string. They counted a little over two hundred, each one a green tuft no bigger than a thumbnail, in rows along the fallen logs where the cones had dropped. Kyle counted his twice. He came back and gave the evening program at the amphitheater for eleven more summers, and when people asked him about the fire he told them to go out and look.

Background

The verse

A Bhikshu (mendicant) who delights in earnestness, who looks with fear on thoughtlessness, moves about like fire, burning all his fetters, small or large.Dhammapada 31, tr. F. Max Müller (1881)

In the commentary, a monk takes a meditation subject from the Buddha at Jetavana and goes off to practice it, but makes no progress. He sets out to go back and ask for a subject better suited to him. On the way he sees a forest fire and climbs a hill to watch it, and he thinks: as this fire goes on burning everything, large and small, so should I go on, burning away every fetter with the fire of knowledge. The Buddha, sitting in his chamber, knows his thought and sends an image of himself that seems to sit before the monk, radiant, and speaks the verse. The monk reaches awakening on the spot, travels through the air to the Buddha, praises him, and goes. Read the full commentary story.

Yellowstone, 1988

From 1972 Yellowstone let most lightning-caused fires burn as part of the natural order, and in 1988, in what became the worst drought in the park's recorded history, fires that began in June spread under that policy until it was suspended on July 15. The North Fork fire, which started accidentally on July 22, burned more than 410,000 acres by itself. On August 20, remembered as Black Saturday, the fires roughly doubled in a single day; on September 7 a fire storm reached Old Faithful; and on September 11 about a quarter inch of snow stopped their advance. In all some 793,880 acres, about 36 percent of the park, burned (National Park Service; Wikipedia).

Many lodgepole pines carry serotinous cones, sealed with resin and held on the tree for years, which open in the heat of a fire. After 1988, surveys found between 15,000 and two million seeds per acre on the burned ground, and a year later between 2,000 and 12,000 seedlings per acre (NPR). Ardis reads from John Muir's The Yosemite (1912), chapter 6, "The Forest Trees in General," where Muir describes fire in the Sierra's two-leaved pine, a variety of the same species.
