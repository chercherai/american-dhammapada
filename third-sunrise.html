Walking the American WayCommentary 11.8 · Ānanda

Third Sunrise

Till thou at length art free,
Leaving thine outgrown shell by life’s unresting sea!
— Oliver Wendell Holmes, “The Chambered Nautilus” (1858)

This is the pass-down as I got it, at the big pool on Space Center Boulevard.

I. The Question

Clear Lake, Texas · 2024

Andy had been a diver at the pool for twenty-two years, and for eighteen of them he had worked June Tolliver’s classes. He set up her mockups and carried her checklists, and he hung in the water beside her students with a hand on their boots. He remembered everything she had ever said in a debrief. When two instructors disagreed about how Tolliver had taught a thing, they went and asked Andy, and he gave it back to them in her words. So when the history office wanted her on tape, she said she would talk to him and to nobody else.

They sat in her kitchen with the recorder between them. She was sixty-six. On the third afternoon he took a printout from his bag. It was the loop transcript from her second spacewalk, in 2002, when the station’s arm was a year old.

There’s a line here nobody has ever explained, he said. Phased elapsed time four hours fifty-one. You’d been hanging on the end of the arm for three revs. Then this.

EV1 There you are. I see you. You’re all done.

CAPCOM EV1, Houston. Say again?

EV1 Disregard, Houston. I’ve got sunrise.

Who were you talking to?

She looked at the page for a while. In twenty-two years nobody had asked her.

II. The Hold

Low Earth orbit · 2002

She was a framing carpenter’s daughter from Coos Bay, Oregon. That morning she was two hundred and forty miles up, on her way out to the end of a new girder to drive bolts. Her boots were locked in the foot restraint at the end of the arm. Okada was flying it from inside and swinging her out over the lab, and Hendry was somewhere behind her on the truss with the cable bag. Then the arm stopped.

It stopped so smoothly that it took her a second to notice. Then Okada said a word in Japanese, and Houston came on. The arm’s computer had safed itself and set the brakes. They would have to bring up the backup string from the ground, and they wanted several passes to do it carefully. Hendry asked if he should go out and get her. Houston said negative. Hendry was to press on with the cables, and EV1 was in a good config where she was. June, you’re go to hang out.

There was nothing within reach of her but the arm. She had the planet under her boots and the sun on her left shoulder, and about nine minutes of daylight.

The voice arrived at once. Every astronaut has it and nobody talks about it. It told her that she had no business in that seat. Thousands of people had applied the year she got in, and it could name the ones who ought to be standing here. It said the arm had quit because of something she had done. It told her to key the mike and say she was uncomfortable, so that Hendry would be sent to walk her home, and that would be the end of it, and nobody would blame her much. When she did not key the mike it threw things. A torn glove. A tether hook left open. Her father’s face when they phoned him. Each one came at her fast, and she let each come the whole way in. When they got to her there was nothing in them.

Then it asked what right she had to stay.

Her right hand was free. She put it out in front of her with the palm down, toward the Earth. Everyone who had ever fed her was down there, and everyone who had taught her or told her no. The Earth did not say anything. The voice had no more to say either.

The dark came across the Pacific at seventeen thousand miles an hour and went over her. She reached up and switched off her helmet lights.

III. First Night

The first pass through the shadow

With the lights off she could not see her own gloves. She began to remember.

It did not come the way memory usually does, in scraps. She remembered in order, and each time she was all the way inside it. She was nine and sitting on a roof deck in Coos Bay with a coffee can of sixteen-penny nails between her knees. Her name there was Junebug. She ate Fig Newtons and wanted her father to look at her. Then she was at Oregon State with a slide rule on her belt, and then she was at a desk in Renton doing wing loads. At that desk her name was Tolliver and she ate out of a machine. She wanted the corner office, and when she had it she wanted Houston. She had a husband in one of those lives. After that she was in the back seat of a T-38, and after that she was on her first flight. She had been certain every time that this one was the life. Each time she had moved in and set out her things, and then she had left without looking back at the one before.

She knew what it was like. Her grandmother had kept half a nautilus shell on top of the sewing machine. It was sawn through so that you could see the rooms. When June was thirteen she won a speech ribbon reciting the poem that went with it, and she still had all of it.

Year after year beheld the silent toil
That spread his lustrous coil;
Still, as the spiral grew,
He left the past year’s dwelling for the new,
Stole with soft step its shining archway through,
Built up its idle door,
Stretched in his last-found home, and knew the old no more.

— Oliver Wendell Holmes, “The Chambered Nautilus”

She had always taken that as praise of the animal.

Then the remembering went on past where it should have stopped. She was on a roof again, in 1946, and the hands holding the hammer were her father’s. She was in a kitchen in a sod house with a woman whose name she had never been told, and she knew what that woman had wanted. It went back further than she could count, and she did not try to count. In every one of them there was a name and a meal and a wanting, and then a move to the next.

The sun came up on her right like a welding arc. She had been in the dark for thirty-four minutes.

IV. Second Night

The second pass

She spent the daylight watching Hendry’s helmet go back and forth along the truss. Houston said the backup string was looking good and to give them one more rev. She said copy. When the shadow came over the second time she left her lights off and looked down.

She was a structures engineer and she did not have a word for the way she saw that night. She could see the storms over the Congo lit from inside like paper lanterns. The squid fleets in the Gulf of Thailand were green, and the Nile was a lit stem with a flower at the end of it. She had seen all of that before on her first flight. This time she could see the people too. She did not see them as dots. She saw each one, the way you see a face across a table. They were arriving and leaving the whole time. A light came on in a window in Karachi because a child had been born under it, and a light went off in Omaha. Nobody was put anywhere. Each one went on to the next place by what they had done and what they had wanted, the way water goes downhill. She saw a man who had been cruel for sixty years go where that went, and a woman who had fed strangers go where that went. She watched it for half an hour and nobody told her what it meant. It was like being shown how a truss carries a load.

A meteor burned out below her, between her boots and the sea.

V. Third Night

The third pass · elapsed time 4:17 to 4:51

By the third dark her feet were cold and her mind was clear, and she did what she had been trained to do with a failure. She ran the chain.

Why was she on this arm? Because she had been assigned the flight. She had the flight because she had wanted it and had worked eleven years to get it. She had wanted it because the first flight had been so sweet that the day she landed she was already hungry. And the first flight was sweet because of a July night when she was eleven, when her father pointed a hammer at the moon and said there were men walking on that, Junebug. That night had felt a certain way in her chest. The feeling came from seeing, and the seeing came from having eyes and a mind turned toward it, and that came from being born. She had been born because somebody before her had wanted something too. She could not find a place where it began.

Then she ran it the other direction, the way you check a load path. If there is no wanting, the next house does not get framed. And without the next house there is nobody who has to move into it, and furnish it, and stand in the door of it afraid.

She saw then that she had been wrong about one thing all her life. She had thought she was the tenant. In every one of those rooms she had believed she was the one who lived there. But there was a builder, and it had been the same builder every time. Its face was so familiar to her that she had never once looked straight at it.

She looked at it now.

Her father used to say that a roof is all one piece of wanting to fall down, and the ridge is what talks it out of it. If you pull the ridge, the rafters lie down by themselves. She watched them lie down. Ahead of her the edge of the world drew a blue line and then a gold one, and the sun came over.

She spoke out loud, because there was somebody to tell. Houston asked her to say again.

VI. The Answer

Clear Lake · the same afternoon

That’s who, she said. The builder. I’d been looking for him my whole life without knowing I was looking. I kept getting born into the next thing and it kept hurting. Once I’d seen him he couldn’t get a rafter to stand.

Andy checked that the recorder was running.

You want the rest of the poem, she said. Everybody learned it as a poem about self-improvement.

Build thee more stately mansions, O my soul,
As the swift seasons roll!
Leave thy low-vaulted past!
Let each new temple, nobler than the last,
Shut thee from heaven with a dome more vast,
Till thou at length art free,
Leaving thine outgrown shell by life’s unresting sea!

— Oliver Wendell Holmes, “The Chambered Nautilus”

He has it in the last two lines. I spent forty-four years on the first five. Every one of those domes shuts you in, Andy, and it doesn’t matter how big you build it. That was mine, she said, and tipped her head back, as though the helmet were still there.

And then what?

Then the arm came back. Okada flew me out to the end and I drove my bolts, and we went in. It was a good EVA.

The next winter Columbia was lost and nobody flew for two and a half years. By the time the line moved again she had asked the office to take her name off it. People assumed it was fear, and she let them. She went over to the pool and stayed eighteen years. She taught spacewalkers one thing that was not in any flight rule. Sometime in every run she would stop the task and have them hang where they were, with their lights off, until whatever was talking to them had finished.

You never put in for another flight, Andy said.

There wasn’t anybody left who wanted to go.

•••

He teaches the class now. The pool is forty feet deep and holds six million gallons, with a station lying on the bottom of it. On a Tuesday in the fall he had two new ones on the mockup arm, with their boots in the restraints and safety divers at their heels. Two hours in, he keyed the loop and told them to stop work and kill their helmet lights. Then he told them what she had said on the third sunrise, word for word, and he let the clock run.

Background

The verses

Looking for the maker of this tabernacle, I shall have to run through a course of many births, so long as I do not find (him); and painful is birth again and again. But now, maker of the tabernacle, thou hast been seen; thou shalt not make up this tabernacle again. All thy rafters are broken, thy ridge-pole is sundered; the mind, approaching the Eternal (visankhara, nirvana), has attained to the extinction of all desires.Dhammapada 153–154, tr. F. Max Müller (1881)

These two verses are traditionally the first words the Buddha spoke after his awakening. The commentary’s account is brief. Sitting at the foot of the Bodhi tree, he overcame Māra before sunset. In the first watch of the night he recalled his former lives. In the middle watch he gained the divine eye, which sees beings passing away and arising according to their deeds. In the last watch he worked through dependent origination forwards and backwards, and at sunrise he was awake and spoke the verses. The “builder of the house” is craving. Much later he recited the verses to his attendant Ānanda, who had asked. Read the full commentary story, or about Ānanda, who is remembered for his memory, and the Bodhi tree.

Orbit and Houston, 2002 and after

June Tolliver, her crew and her spacewalk are invented, and the story names no real flight. The setting is real. The International Space Station circles the Earth about every ninety-two minutes, so a crew sees some sixteen sunrises a day, and a spacewalk of six or seven hours passes through several orbital nights of roughly half an hour each. Spacewalkers wear the Extravehicular Mobility Unit, whose helmet carries its own lights. The lead spacewalker is called EV1 and the astronaut in Mission Control who talks to the crew is CAPCOM. The station’s robotic arm, Canadarm2, arrived in April 2001. It can carry an astronaut in a foot restraint at its tip, and its trouble in this story is made up. Columbia was lost on February 1, 2003, and shuttles did not fly again until July 2005.

Astronauts rehearse spacewalks underwater at the Neutral Buoyancy Laboratory near the Johnson Space Center, a pool 202 feet long and 40 feet deep that holds 6.2 million gallons and full-size station mockups, where safety divers attend each suited trainee. The center’s Oral History Project has recorded interviews with its people since 1996. Oliver Wendell Holmes Sr., physician and professor of anatomy, published “The Chambered Nautilus” in The Autocrat of the Breakfast-Table in 1858. Generations of American schoolchildren learned it by heart. The nautilus adds a new chamber to its shell as it grows and seals off the old one behind it.
