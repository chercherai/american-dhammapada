I. The Chief

Granby Street, Norfolk, Virginia · winter 1993

The Garnet Room was on Granby Street, upstairs over a uniform shop, eleven blocks from the water. It had a bar the length of the room, a stage two feet high, a mirror ball, and a show on Friday, Saturday and Sunday nights. It had been there since 1971. For most of those years the state of Virginia had held it unlawful to sell a drink to a homosexual, and a federal judge had struck that rule down only two winters before. Half the men at the bar on any night were Navy and sat with their backs to the door. Everybody knew about the cars that sometimes parked across the street while a man in them wrote down base stickers.

The old man started coming in that January. He was sixty-seven years old, thin, very dark and very straight. He wore a pressed white shirt buttoned to the neck and brown shoes that had been shined that day. He drank one ginger ale, tipped a quarter, and watched the show from the end of the bar as a man watches a ship he is not on.

After a week he asked the bartender whether there was a mop. Within a month he had the place. He came at four, when the door was unlocked for deliveries. He mopped the stage and the stairs and filled the ice wells, and he ran the follow spot from a stool at the back. What he did in the dressing room made him known. He could press. He could put an edge on a satin lapel that would cut bread, and he could steam six yards of beaded chiffon without losing a bead. He never said where he learned it, and nobody needed him to. A man of that age who pressed like that in Norfolk had been a steward. He had spent his thirty years in the wardroom mess, putting creases in officers' whites, in the branch the Navy had staffed with Black sailors and Filipinos, and almost nobody else, until the middle of the seventies. He had gone in at the bottom of it and come out a chief. The girls called him Chief. His name was Emmett Raines.

He lived in a room on Church Street on what was left of his pension after his sister's nursing home in Portsmouth took its part. He ate at the bar before the doors opened, when the cook made a plate for the staff. The girls were good to him. They brought him sweaters. They let him carry the gowns. They told him things they did not tell each other.

What they would not do was let him in the show. He had asked. He asked the way he did everything, once and correctly, standing up. He had asked Miss Coco Lashley, who was the lead of the cast and did the hiring for Sundays, and she had looked at him over her lashes with real kindness.

Chief, she said. Baby. You are sixty-seven years old.

He said he knew how old he was.

You can't walk in a heel. You'd be up there in your first face at an age when most of us have been dead ten years. They'd laugh, and I would have to cut somebody. No, sir. You stay back here with me where it's warm.

He said, Aye, and went and filled the ice.

He did not ask again. He went on mopping, and through February he began to lose flesh. The white shirts stood away from his neck. He ate the staff plate and it did him no good. The cook said so.

II. One Good Thing

The Garnet Room · the first week of March

The show director was Miss Geneva Starr. She was fifty-one that year and had run the cast for fourteen. She had been born in Suffolk, in the peanut country, and raised in a Holiness church where her grandmother was a mother of the congregation, and she had sung there until they put her out at sixteen. She did not lip-synch. She stood at the microphone in a plain dark gown and sang, and sailors who had come upstairs to laugh went down again not speaking. Offstage she said very little. In a house where everybody read everybody, all night, for sport, Miss Geneva had read perhaps six people in fourteen years, and each of them could give you the words.

She had a habit of walking the building at the end of the night, after the last number, when the girls were still at the bar. She said she liked to see what was left when the people went out of it. On a Sunday early in March she came through the dressing room at one in the morning and found Emmett Raines on his knees picking bugle beads out of the floorboards with a table knife.

Chief. What are you doing here?

Squaring away, ma'am.

I don't mean the beads.

He got up. It took him a moment.

Whatever wants doing, he said. The stage. The ice. I see to the gowns.

Do they treat you right?

Yes, ma'am. I get my supper. Nobody has said one hard word to me in this house. He set the knife down on the table. They won't have me in the show. That's all it is. I would like to have been in it once.

She looked at him for some time, and at the shirt collar. Then she went down the hall and brought the whole cast back with her, seven girls with their drinks, half out of their faces. She stood them in the dressing room in front of him.

I want to ask you all something, she said. Does anybody in this room remember one good thing this man ever did? I don't mean the mopping. I mean a thing he did for one of you.

Nobody spoke. They looked at each other. He had done every one of them a hundred small services since January, and not one of them could have named one.

Then Coco Lashley put her glass down.

I do, she said. She said it slowly, looking at him as if he had just walked in. Lord. I do, Mother. It was Naples. Nineteen seventy-eight.

She had been Seaman Apprentice Darnell Lashley then, nineteen years old, on a ship tied up at Naples, and she had come back aboard eleven hours late from a bar off the Via Roma that she did not care to describe to the executive officer. At captain's mast they gave her three days in the brig on bread and water. The Navy could still do that to a seaman then. It was bread, as much as you wanted, and water, and a steel bunk. On the second night she was sick with something besides hunger. She had understood by then what the ship knew about her and what the rest of her enlistment was going to be.

And the hatch opens, she said, and it's not the master-at-arms. It's the old chief from the wardroom mess. I never spoke to him in my life. Stewards didn't come down there. He's got a soup ladle in his hand with a saucer under it, and it's navy beans, still hot. He says, This is off my own plate, son, so it's nobody's business. Eat it and give me back my ladle. And he stood there with his back to the hatch till I did.

She had been put out of the Navy three years later, for the usual reason. She had not seen him again. She had looked at him across a dressing room every night for two months and seen an old man with a mop.

That was you, she said.

I recollect a ladle, Emmett said. I didn't know it was you. You've filled out.

Coco, Miss Geneva said. Do you think it's right to leave a man standing outside who did that?

No, Mother.

Well, then.

I'll take him, Coco said. He's mine. I'll bring him out myself.

III. The Wheel Book

March and April

She gave him the last place at the dressing table, the short end by the mop sink, where the bulb over the mirror flickered. She gave him the first number on Sundays. That was the spot nobody wanted, at nine o'clock, when the room was a third full and still sober. She gave him a name. He was to be Miss Emmaline Raines, since a lady of that age would not be anything shorter. Then she started on him.

Coco Lashley was the best reader in Tidewater. Her people were Lashleys out of Harlem. A great-uncle of hers had kept the door of a building on Edgecombe Avenue for forty years, and it was said in the family that he never forgot a face or what he owed it. She had his eye. She could take a queen apart from the wig lace to the shoe in one breath and be right about every piece. She had read grown performers off that stage and out of the business.

She read Emmett Raines every day for seven weeks.

Chief, you walk like you are carrying a tray. Put the tray down. Nobody is waiting on their coffee.

Your mouth is half a beat behind the record. The lady is singing, and you are reading her lips.

That is not a cheekbone. That is a stripe.

The wig is wearing you. Go back and put it on like you paid for it.

Don't thank them when they clap. Don't you bow. You have been bowing for thirty years. Stand there and take it like it's owed.

Stop. Go back. Again.

She said things to him that she would not have said to a girl of twenty, because a girl of twenty would have gone home. The cast heard it through the wall and winced.

He could not see his own face in the glass without his spectacles. They were drugstore readers, a dollar ninety-eight, with brown frames. He could not paint around them and could not go on in them. So he painted in them and took them off, and Coco told him what he had done wrong, and he put them on again to look. He kept a green memorandum book in his shirt pocket, the kind every chief in the Navy carries and calls his wheel book. Each time she finished he said, Aye, took out the book and the readers, and wrote it down. He wrote down the tray, and the stripe, and half a beat behind. He never once explained himself. He did not sulk or cut his eyes at her. He never told her what he had been in the Navy when she was in diapers, though it was true and she knew it. The next day the thing she had named would be gone, and she would find another.

He put some weight back on. The cook noticed that first.

IV. Sunday

The Garnet Room · the last Sunday in April

Nobody could say afterward which Sunday it had become a different matter. It may only have been that the room was fuller. A ship had come in.

He went on at nine in a gray gown that Coco had cut down for him, long in the sleeve and high at the neck. He wore a small gray wig and one strand of glass pearls. The number was an old Dinah Washington record, a slow one. He walked to the center, and he was not carrying anything. He did not work the room or beg it. He stood and let the song come through him. He was an old woman who had seen what the song said and was past minding who knew. His mouth was on every word. Somewhere in the second verse the bar went quiet, and the bartender stopped the blender with his hand.

The girls in the wings said that what struck them was that he did not want anything from the house. Every one of them wanted something out there. They wanted the dollars, or the noise, or one face at one table. He stood in the light and wanted nothing. When it was over he did not bow. The room got to its feet anyway, sailors and all, and stayed there.

Miss Geneva had watched from the back, beside the doorman, a man called Tiny who filled the stair and had never been seen to hurry. She came to the dressing room afterward. Emmett had the readers on and was looking in the glass to see what he had got wrong.

Coco, she said. This daughter of yours. Is she easy to tell?

Mother, I have read that man every way I know how, and a few I made up. He says aye and writes it in his little book. Whatever I name, he fixes. He has never once given me a look.

If you could get them like that, how many would you take?

Every one they'd send me, Coco said. I'd take a busload.

V. The White Mule

The dressing room · later that night

The cast sat late. They were pleased with him and somewhat ashamed. The talk came around, as it will, to Coco, who had carried one ladle of beans in her head for fifteen years and paid it back like that. One of them said that most people wouldn't remember their own mother that long. Another said the old man had been lucky in his teacher. The first one said the teacher had been lucky too, because Coco's mouth would have killed anybody else.

Miss Geneva was unpinning her hair.

That's nothing new for her, she said. She has always been that kind. Then she told them a story, which she almost never did. It was her grandfather's, who had cut timber in the Dismal Swamp.

There was a gang of men, he said, that went into the swamp every fall to get out juniper. They cut the logs and hewed them square on the spot. They marked every piece with a number and floated the frames down the canal to Suffolk, to be put up as houses. One fall an old mule came limping out of the reeds on three legs and lay down by their fire. She belonged to nobody. She had a splinter of cypress knee through the frog of her hoof and the leg was swollen to the shoulder. The men cut around the splinter with a chisel. They tied a line to it and drew it out, and washed the place with warm water, and kept her by the fire till it closed.

When she could walk, that mule went to work for them. Nobody had put a hand on her. She snaked logs. She stood where a man needed something to stand against. They said she would carry an adze to you in her teeth. Each man gave her a piece of his own corn bread at noon, and with a gang that size she lived well.

After some years she got slow. She went off into the reeds one morning and came back that evening with a colt behind her. He was white all over and had never worn a collar. She stood him in front of the men. She looked at them and she looked at him, and then she went back into the swamp, and they did not see her again. The colt worked for them as his mother had. He let the men's children ride him into the canal on Sundays.

Word of a white mule gets around. The man who owned the mill at Suffolk came out in a buggy to have him, and the gang said, Take him, Captain, he's yours. The mule would not move. The mill man was no fool. He counted out silver dollars and laid one down at each hoof. The mule stood still. He laid down a bolt of cloth for every wife in the camp, and the mule stood. So he laid down shoes for the children who had ridden him. Then the mule looked one time at all of them, the men and the wives and the children, and walked to the buggy.

There's more to it, Miss Geneva said. Years after, that mule saved the mill for the man's baby son, when the father was dead and some people from Richmond came to take it. That's another night. She put the last pin in the dish. My grandfather said there are people who are that old mule. You do them one turn, and they will send you the best thing they have. And he said some people are the colt. He will not walk off from where he's been fed till everybody there is paid.

She did not say which was which. Coco was looking at the floor.

VI. The Mirror

The same night

Over the long mirror in that dressing room there was a piece of shirt cardboard, gone brown, with four lines lettered on it in green ink. Miss Dorian had put it up, who ran the cast before Geneva. She had been a high school English teacher in Newport News until 1962, when they found out about her. She died in 1989 in the hospital on Colley Avenue, and four of the girls who had sat with her were dead since. Nobody had taken the card down, and nobody new had ever been told to read it.

Miss Geneva reached up and touched the corner of it.

Have you learn'd lessons only of those who admired you, and were tender with you, and stood aside for you?
Have you not learn'd great lessons from those who reject you, and brace themselves against you? or who treat you with contempt, or dispute the passage with you?

— Walt Whitman, “Stronger Lessons,” Leaves of Grass

Dorian had her poet, she said. My grandmother had it shorter. She stood with her hand on the back of Emmett's chair and said it as she had learned it, standing up in the Holiness church in Suffolk at seven years old.

As an earring of gold, and an ornament of fine gold, so is a wise reprover upon an obedient ear.

— Proverbs 25:12

Children, listen. When somebody tells you your slip is showing, she has put money in your hand. She didn't have to. It would have been easier for her to let you walk out there. When you find one who will look at you that hard and say what she sees, don't you get your back up. Stay by her. You'll come out better for it. I never knew anybody who came out worse.

She looked down the table at the seven of them, and at the old man at the short end.

He knew that when he came in the door. He was the only one in this house who did.

Then she said good night and went to walk the building.

The girls drifted out. The mirror ball had been left turning in the empty room, and through the dressing-room door its spots of light went slowly across the ceiling. Emmett sat at the short end of the table and took the gray wig off and set it on its block.

Coco had her coat on. She stopped behind him.

You came off stage left, she said. In this house we exit right. And you had lipstick on your tooth the whole second verse.

Aye, he said.

He put on the reading glasses and took the green book out of his shirt pocket and wrote it down.

The verse

If you see an intelligent man who tells you where true treasures are to be found, who shows what is to be avoided, and administers reproofs, follow that wise man; it will be better, not worse, for those who follow him.Dhammapada 76, tr. F. Max Müller (1881)

In the commentary, Rādha is a poor old brahmin of Sāvatthī who goes to live at the monastery, cutting grass, sweeping cells and fetching water. The monks treat him kindly but will not ordain him, and he grows thin. The Buddha, walking the grounds one evening, asks him what he is doing there, then calls the monks together and asks whether anyone remembers a good deed of his. Sāriputta remembers that in Rājagaha the man once gave him a ladleful of his own food, and agrees to ordain him. Rādha is seated in the outermost row. Sāriputta takes him everywhere and corrects him constantly, and Rādha follows every instruction without resentment and soon becomes an arahat. Asked how many such pupils he would accept, Sāriputta says as many as he could get. When the monks praise Sāriputta's gratitude, the Buddha tells the birth story of the elephant whose foot was healed by a camp of carpenters, who worked for them and then gave them his white son; the young elephant would not leave with the king until the carpenters, their wives and their children had all been paid. The Buddha then says that one who points out faults should be regarded as one who shows where treasure is buried. Read the full commentary story.

Norfolk, 1993

Norfolk is home to the largest naval base in the world, and its gay bars have always been full of sailors who could be discharged for being seen there. Until a federal court ruled in a 1991 case, Virginia's liquor regulations forbade licensed bars to serve homosexuals or become their meeting places. In 1993 the country argued over whether gay people could serve at all; the compromise known as "Don't Ask, Don't Tell" was signed into law that November. In June 1991 about eighty people had marched from Old Dominion University to the gates of the base. The city's drag shows go back at least to the Cue Club of the 1970s. In drag houses an experienced performer, a drag mother, names and trains a newcomer; to "read" someone is to tell her faults to her face, exactly and with wit, and it is counted an art. The Garnet Room and its cast are invented.

Until the steward and commissaryman ratings were merged on January 1, 1975, the men who cooked for, served and kept the quarters of Navy officers belonged to a branch that was largely segregated, filled by Black and Filipino sailors. Confinement on bread and water for up to three days was a lawful punishment for junior sailors aboard ship until January 1, 2019. Whitman's two lines were first printed in 1860 and stand among the last poems of Leaves of Grass. Miss Geneva's swamp tale follows the Alīnacitta Jātaka, with a mule where the old story has an elephant.
