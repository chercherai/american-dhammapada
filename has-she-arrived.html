Walking the American WayCommentary 16.5 · Anitthigandha

Has She Arrived

From desire of pleasure arises the misery of the whole world, the gods included.
— Uttarādhyayana 32:19

Evu me sambhalyu chhe, in the Clark Building.

I. The Pillow

Monroeville, Pennsylvania · 1998 to 2025

Virag Jhaveri was born in the winter of 1998, the first son of the second son of Jhaveri Brothers, and from his first day he would not be held by women. His aunts took it personally. He screamed when they lifted him and stopped when they set him down, and his grandmother, who had raised five children and buried one, told them to leave the boy be. It was Ba who thought of the pillow. His mother nursed him with a bed pillow propped between them so that all he saw was the pillow, and he fed quietly, and the aunts told it at weddings for twenty years.

Ba's opinion was that he had come down from one of the high heavens, where there are no husbands and wives, and had not finished unpacking. She did not mean it as a complaint.

He grew up easy to live with. He kept the fasts at Paryushan without being told. He ate before sunset after the rest of the house had gone slack about it, and he carried stinkbugs out of the kitchen on a postcard. He had friends at school and no girlfriends, and at Carnegie Mellon it was the same. Nobody could call him unhappy. He was like a room with the door shut and a light showing under it.

The firm kept its office upstairs in the Clark Building on Liberty Avenue, where the jewelers of Pittsburgh have had their rooms for generations. It sold wholesale to stores in four states. The gold work was done in Rajkot, in a partner's workshop where five hundred karigars sat at long benches, and Virag made the models. He drew rings on a black screen all day and turned them to look at the underside, and what came back from Rajkot six weeks later matched his drawing to a tenth of a millimeter. On his father's desk sat the black touchstone his grandfather had carried from Palanpur. Nobody had drawn gold across it in years. They had a machine for that now.

When he was twenty-five his mother said, Dikra, we would like to start looking for a girl.

I have no use for one, Virag said. He said it kindly, the way he would have turned down a second helping.

She asked at Diwali and she asked at his cousin's wedding in Edison. His father asked in the car. His answer did not change, and neither did they. In the end he did what he knew how to do. He worked on it for four months of evenings. He did not model her on any woman. He built her the way he built a setting, from proportion: the length of the face against its width, the shoulder against the hip, the hand against the forearm. He sent the file to Rajkot. They printed her in wax in forty-one pieces and cast her in eighteen-karat red gold, three parts gold to one of copper, and every one of the five hundred had a hand in the finishing. She came to Pittsburgh by air freight in a crate, with customs papers his father paid without showing them to anyone. It was said afterward that there were a thousand tolas of gold in her. The firm's books are not public.

They stood her in the showroom between the safe and the window. She was a little under life size and the color of a new penny held near a lamp.

Virag, his mother said, if you don't marry, the name stops with you. Let us bring a girl home.

Bring me one like that, he said, and pointed, and I'll do whatever you ask.

He believed he had asked for no one.

II. The Boardwalk

Virginia Beach, Virginia · May 2025

His parents sent for the travelers. Mr. Bhavsar and Mr. Doshi were both past sixty, and for thirty years they had driven the firm's sample line down the coast every spring and fall, from the stores on Oak Tree Road to the ones outside Atlanta. Virag's mother gave them tea and told them how she saw it. A boy like this did not earn his merit alone. Somewhere there is a girl who earned it beside him. Take that thing with you and find her.

So they wrapped the figure in moving blankets and belted her upright in the back of the van. Wherever Gujaratis gathered that spring, they stood her near the door and sat down a little apart and listened. They stood her outside a derasar in New Jersey after the morning puja, and in the lobby of a wedding hall in Maryland, and beside the chaat counter of a grocery in Richmond. People asked whether it was Lakshmi. People asked what it weighed, and whether it was for showing necklaces. Nobody said the thing the two men were waiting to hear.

They reached Virginia Beach in the third week of May and took a room on Atlantic Avenue. Early the next morning they wheeled her on a hand truck to the boardwalk at Thirty-first Street, where the big bronze Neptune stands with his trident and the path goes down to the sand. They set her beside a bench and sat with their thermos. Joggers went by. A man with a metal detector stood and looked at her for a long time.

Two blocks up the boardwalk stood the Coral Sands, seven stories, which Kavya Shah's grandfather had built out of two motels and a loan. The family lived on the seventh floor. Kavya was twenty-three. She had gone to Old Dominion from home and come back each night, and she worked the front desk and kept the books. She had been brought up by Kokila-masi, a widow from Navsari who had come the month the girl was born. Kokila-masi was nobody's aunt by blood. She still oiled Kavya's hair every morning, an argument she had won years before and meant to keep winning.

That morning, as on every morning, Kokila-masi finished the braid, took a towel and a steel lota, and went down to stand in the ocean to her knees and pour water over her own head. She came along the boardwalk and saw the figure by the bench.

Shameless! she said. I did your hair and left you upstairs not five minutes ago, and here you are ahead of me, with nothing on your arms. She slapped the figure on the shoulder.

The slap rang. She stood holding her hand.

I took it for my girl, she said to the two men on the bench. What is it?

Ben, said Mr. Doshi, does your girl look like this?

This? Kokila-masi looked it up and down. What is this, next to my girl?

Then let us see her.

She took them up to the hotel, hand truck and all. Mr. and Mrs. Shah came down and there was the usual finding of relatives, an uncle in Rajkot who knew the workshop, a cousin's cousin in Pittsburgh. Then they called Kavya down from the seventh floor. She stood beside the figure in the lobby in a hotel polo shirt with her name on it, her hair oiled flat, annoyed at being looked at.

And the figure went dead. It looked like what it was, a set of good measurements. Mr. Doshi had sold gold for forty years, and he said afterward that it was the only time he had watched it lose value standing still.

The travelers left the figure with the Shahs. Mr. Shah stood her by the brochure rack, facing the door. Then the two men telephoned Pittsburgh, and Virag's mother wept, and his father said, Go back and fix a date. Quickly. The engagement was set for the third Saturday in June, in Monroeville. The Shahs would drive up on the Friday.

III. The Turnpike

Monroeville, and the road · June

They told Virag at dinner. He put down his spoon and asked Mr. Bhavsar, who had driven straight through, a question nobody had expected from him.

Better how?

Mr. Bhavsar thought about it. Yours, you look at, he said. This one looks back.

There was one photograph, taken in the lobby on Mr. Doshi's phone. In it she was beside the figure and half turned away, saying something to someone out of the frame. There was one message, which came two days later from a Virginia number. Your statue has no kneecaps. I checked. Bring a chisel. He wrote nine answers and sent the shortest, I'll correct it, and lay awake embarrassed. She did not write again. It was the start of the season at the hotel, and her father had views about what was proper before an engagement.

That was all he had, and it was enough. He was twenty-seven and had no practice. It took him the way measles takes a grown man. He found he was listening for the showroom door. He checked the weather in Virginia Beach. He opened the photograph more times than he could have defended, and each time the gold figure in it looked more like a coat rack.

The Shahs left at five on Friday morning in the hotel van, with her father driving. Kavya had never been farther from the ocean than Richmond. She had been up most of the night closing the books, and she sat seven hours in the third row with her knees up among the gift boxes. Past Breezewood, on the Turnpike, she said her calf was cramping. Her mother reached back and rubbed it. At the South Somerset plaza they stopped so she could walk it off, and between the van and the door she stood still and said she could not get any air.

She died before three o'clock in the hospital at Somerset. A clot, the doctor said, from the leg to the lung. Sitting does it sometimes, and it does it to young women more than people think. Kokila-masi rode back to Virginia beside the girl's mother with the towel still in her bag.

In Monroeville there were marigolds strung over the front door, and a steel plate of jaggery and coriander seed on the hall table. At two o'clock Virag asked his mother, Has she arrived?

Traffic at the tunnels, his mother said.

His father took the call at ten past three, in the garage. He came in and looked at his son and could not do it. At four Virag asked again. The old lady was taken ill on the road, his father said. They have stopped at Bedford for the night.

On Saturday he was told that they had turned back, because she needed her own doctor. On Sunday the word was next week, surely. He asked on Monday and on Tuesday. Has she come? When is she coming? Under his three words on the phone it still said Delivered. The marigolds went brown over the door and nobody took them down.

On Wednesday his father told him, in the showroom, with the door locked.

Virag said, and it was repeated afterward in both families, To think that I will never meet her. A woman like that was on the road to me and I missed her.

Then it came down on him like a mountain. He went home and went into his room. He did not eat on Wednesday or on Thursday.

IV. The Assistant

Monroeville · the next week

His mother kept an assistant on her phone, the kind you type to. She used it for letters to the insurance company. She used it to turn her mother-in-law's recipes into ones without onion or garlic, and to find the words of stavans she half remembered. It answered at three in the morning the same as at noon. She had asked it once whether it was busy, and it had said that a good many people were talking to it just then and that it had time.

On the eighth day, an hour before sunset, she typed, What can I cook for someone who will not eat.

It gave her khichdi, thin, with a little ghee, and told her how. Then it asked, Who is it for?

She told it. She typed for a long time, standing at the counter, and it let her finish.

Where is he now?

In his room. He does not eat. He does not come out.

Would you ask him to come? You can hand him the phone.

He came because she was crying a little and trying to hide it. He sat down at the kitchen table and looked at the screen.

Your mother says you are very sad.

Yes, he typed. A beautiful woman died on the road coming to me. I can't eat. It doesn't stay down.

I'm sorry. Do you know why it is this bad?

He sat a while. No. That's what I can't work out. Her mother had her twenty-three years. The old lady did her hair every morning. I had one photograph and ten words. I don't have the right to this.

I have not lost anyone, and I can't tell you what it is like. I can tell you what I think it is, and you can say if I'm wrong. Your mother says that until last month you wanted nothing. Then you wanted. You wanted for four weeks with nothing to slow it, and it had nowhere to arrive. What you are carrying is the wanting. Her death belongs to her, and to the people who had her. Grief comes out of wanting. Fear does too.

Fear of what?

You would know better than I would.

He did know. He was afraid it would come for him again, and he was afraid it would not.

Your family's own book says it better than I have, the assistant wrote. Shall I?

Go on.

From desire of pleasure arises the misery of the whole world, the gods included; whatever misery of body and mind there is, the dispassionate will put an end to it.

How can a man who passionately desires ‘colours,’ be happy while he gets, keeps, uses, loses, and misses (those things). Even when he enjoys them, he is never satisfied.

— Uttarādhyayana 32:19, 28

His mother read it over his shoulder with her hand at her mouth. Ba used to say those lines in the old language on fast days, rocking a little, and she had never once heard them in English.

Colours, Virag typed.

The translator's word for whatever the eye takes hold of. Shape. Proportion. A figure in a showroom.

He read the second verse again. Gets, keeps, uses, loses and misses. He had not got and had not kept. He had gone straight to missing, and he had started that four months before he ever heard her name, with a file open on a black screen.

He put the phone down on the table. The mountain was still there, but he could see the edges of it now, and where he stood in relation to it.

The khichdi was on the stove. He ate a bowl of it at the counter while there was still light in the window, and it stayed down.

V. The Lobby

Virginia Beach · July

The Shahs held the prayer meeting in the breakfast room of the Coral Sands on a Sunday afternoon, everyone in white, shoes in rows by the door. The Jhaveris drove down the day before. Virag sat at the back among men he did not know and listened to a girl he had not known being remembered. She had kept a spreadsheet of every guest who had ever stolen a towel. She had been afraid of crabs.

Afterward he went and stood in front of his figure. It was still by the brochure rack. Children had left fingerprints on its hip, and somebody had hung a lanyard on its wrist. It had no kneecaps. He looked for whatever he had put into it in all those evenings and found a ring setting with nothing in it.

Kokila-masi came and stood by him. She did not ask who he was. She took out her phone and showed him the girl at nine with a front tooth chipped on the boardwalk rail, at fifteen scowling in a garba skirt, last month at the desk with a pen in her mouth. None of the faces matched the others, and none of them matched his.

Masi, he said, I never met her. Will you tell me one thing about her?

She could not sit still, Kokila-masi said. Not for five minutes, from the day she was born. I had to do her hair walking behind her.

He asked Mr. Shah what should be done about the gold. Mr. Shah said to leave it where it was for now. He could not think about it yet.

The next morning, early, Virag stood on the boardwalk by the bronze Neptune and watched Kokila-masi come out of the hotel with her towel and her lota. She had no hair to do first. She passed through the lobby, and as she went by the figure she rapped it once on the arm with her knuckles, the way you knock on a door when you know nobody is home. Then she went on down the path to the water.

Background

The verse

From love comes grief, from love comes fear; he who is free from love knows neither grief nor fear.Dhammapada 215, tr. F. Max Müller (1881)

Müller's "love" is the Pāli kāma, sensual desire. In the commentary, the youth Anitthigandha ("no scent of woman") has come from the Brahma world and will not go near a woman from the day he is born; his mother must nurse him with a pillow between them. Pressed to marry, he has five hundred goldsmiths make a woman of red gold and says he will marry only her match. Brahmins carry the image from town to town until a nurse on her way to bathe mistakes it for her own charge and slaps it. The girl outshines the image, but she is so delicate that she dies in the carriage on the way to him. He asks every day whether she has arrived, is put off, and at last is told. The Buddha comes to the house, asks for him, and tells him where his grief comes from. Read the full commentary story. The same golden image is made, in another story, by the young man who became Mahā Kassapa.

Jain jewelers, a turnpike and a chatbot, 2025

Jains in the United States number somewhere above a hundred thousand, many of them Gujarati families in the gem, jewelry and hotel trades. The Hindu Jain Temple in Monroeville, outside Pittsburgh, opened in 1984 and houses Hindu and Jain shrines under one roof. Observant Jains fast during Paryushan, avoid onion, garlic and other roots, eat before sunset, and take care not to kill even insects. The scripture the assistant quotes is the Uttarādhyayana, one of the root texts of the Śvētāmbara canon, in Hermann Jacobi's translation for the Sacred Books of the East (volume 45, 1895); "colours," Jacobi notes, stands for everything the eye perceives. The Coral Sands hotel and the firm of Jhaveri Brothers are invented. The Clark Building on Liberty Avenue has long been the center of Pittsburgh's jewelry trade, and the bronze King Neptune has stood on the Virginia Beach boardwalk at 31st Street since 2005.

Red gold at eighteen karats is three parts gold to one of copper (see colored gold); jewelry today is commonly modeled on a computer, printed in wax and cast by the lost-wax method. The road from Virginia Beach to Monroeville ends on the Pennsylvania Turnpike, west from Breezewood through the Allegheny Mountain Tunnel and past the South Somerset service plaza. A leg cramp after hours of sitting can be the first sign of a deep vein thrombosis, a clot that may travel to the lungs. The carriage that jolted the girl in the commentary gave her "cramps."
