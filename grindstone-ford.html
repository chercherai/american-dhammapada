Grindstone Ford

I find the Traces of abundance of Feet going into your Majesty’s Palace, and not one that comes back again.
— Aesop, A Fox and a Sick Lion, tr. Sir Roger L’Estrange (1692)

The way I had it told to me, on the Natchez road.

I. The Notice

Natchez Under-the-Hill, Mississippi Territory · May 1803

Mayhew Dane came down from Pittsburgh that spring with five broadhorns of flour, whiskey and bar iron. By the first week of May he had sold all of it at Natchez, and the boats too, for their lumber. What he had to show for the winter was Spanish dollars, in four small kegs with NAILS painted on the heads. Everybody under the hill knew what was in the kegs. A man can’t sell five boatloads in a town that size and keep it quiet.

Twice in one week somebody tried the storehouse he had hired by the landing. The second time they brought an auger, and they had three holes through the planking before the watchman’s dog made up its mind to bark. Dane sat on the kegs the rest of that night with a horse pistol across his knees. In the morning he wrote out a notice and had it nailed up at the post office on the bluff and at two of the grog shops below.

Mr. Dane of Pittsburgh starts for Nashville by the Natchez road on Monday. Any men of the upper country going home that way may travel with his wagon. They will not want for bread or meat while they are with him.

There were plenty to read it. The boats came down the river every spring and none of them went back. The men who had steered them walked home, five hundred miles up through the Choctaw and Chickasaw nations to the Tennessee and on to the Cumberland, and a good many left Natchez with nothing in their pockets but their hands. Absalom Leeper was one of those. He was twenty-two, from Redstone on the Monongahela. He had been paid forty dollars at the landing and could account for about six of them. Eleven others in the same case put their names or their marks under his.

Dane looked them over from the storehouse door. He was a short, careful man with ink on his cuff, and he counted them twice.

Twelve, he said. I had hoped for forty. Well, twelve eat less.

They went out on Monday at daylight behind an ox wagon, with the four kegs under the meal sacks. Some others who had read the notice, and had no thought of walking home, were gone from Natchez the night before.

II. The Ford

Grindstone Ford on Bayou Pierre

Seventy miles north of Natchez the road came down to Bayou Pierre at a place called Grindstone Ford, and there the settled country stopped. South of the bayou were cotton fields and cabins and such law as the Territory had. North of it lay the Choctaw nation. The road from there was a horse path through cane and big timber, with days between one roof and the next.

There was a stand at the ford, a log house of one room where a traveler could buy mush and milk and a place on the floor. A wagon could go no farther. Dane stopped three days to sell the wagon and the oxen and buy pack horses, and he was particular about horses. The twelve lay about the yard and ate. He had been as good as his notice. There was meal and bacon twice a day and coffee in the morning, and Leeper, who had not eaten twice a day since March, began to fill out his shirt again.

The stand kept a hired man named Luke Farrow, who worked the canoe when the bayou was up and saw to the stock. On the second evening somebody hallooed from the cane on the north bank to be fetched across, and Luke knew the voice before he knew the man. It was Wiley Harkins. The two of them had poled a keelboat up the Cumberland four summers before, and Harkins had once pulled him out of that river by the hair.

Harkins did not want supper. He stood by the canoe and looked up at the yard.

When’s the Pittsburgh man going on?

Day after tomorrow, I heard him say. What is it to you?

Harkins told him. He never had been able to hold a thing in. There were men camped a day up the path where it crossed a creek, he said, more men than Luke would guess, and some of them had been with Mason or said they had. They had tried a plank wall in Natchez twice. They did not mean to try it again when the kegs were coming up to them on horses.

Well, Luke said. He will be along directly. Go on back.

He set Harkins across and pulled the canoe up and stood by it a while. He owed Harkins something. He owed the Pittsburgh man nothing at all. Dane had not said ten words to him in two days, and had counted his change. Still it came to him that he could stop one side or the other of this, and that he had better pick. He looked up at the yard. Twelve men sat around Dane’s kettle with Dane’s bacon in their fingers, and one of them was singing. Luke thought of the same twelve somewhere up in the cane with no kettle.

What have I to do with thieves, he thought, and went to find Dane.

Dane heard him out, standing by a horse with its near forefoot in his hand.

How do you know it?

I know one of them. He told me.

Dane put the hoof down. He did not thank Luke and he did not doubt him.

Then I have no business going on at all, he said. I will turn round and go home by New Orleans and the sea.

III. Neither Way

The same place · that week and the next

Horses are sooner bought than sold, and Dane was still at the ford when Harkins hallooed again. Luke told him the plain truth. The Pittsburgh man had given up the path and meant to go back down to Natchez. Harkins went off with that. Inside of two days the men had come out of the cane, crossed the bayou above the ford in the night, and laid up along the Natchez road, where it ran sunk between high clay banks a few miles to the south.

Dane still had not gone. Harkins came a third time, and this time he came riding up from the south like any other traveler. Luke understood from that where they were lying now. He told Dane that too.

Dane sat on one of his kegs in the yard a good part of that afternoon. He looked at the bayou and the spring above it. He looked at the corn standing behind the house, and at the ford, where every man going up or down the country had to wet his feet. Three parties came through while he sat there. One wanted powder and one wanted shoes, and the stand had neither to sell.

I want for nothing here, he said, to nobody. I will go neither up nor back. I will stop where I am.

He went over to the twelve.

There were men laid for me up the path. When I thought to turn back they shifted and laid for me down the road. So I am going neither way. Any of you that wants to stop here with me may, and I will find him work. Any that wants to go on, go, and take a week’s meal with you.

They talked it over. They were men with people up the country and corn that somebody else was hoeing. In the morning they took the meal and thanked him and waded the bayou. Each of them carried everything he owned, which was a blanket and whatever stick he had cut for himself, and they went up into the cane in a line.

On the second day the path crossed a creek, and by it they found the ashes of a good many fires and nobody at all. A week after that, two men sitting their horses beside the path looked each one of them over as he came by. One of the two laughed. Nothing else happened to them the whole way.

IV. The Mail

Colbert’s ferry on the Tennessee · June

The mail between Nashville and Natchez went that year by one man on one horse. He had a pair of leather bags behind the saddle, a blanket, a sack of corn, and a tin trumpet to blow when he came near a house. The rider that summer was Josiah Vance. He had carried it since the road was made a post road, and had ridden express in Pennsylvania as a boy before that. He read everything that was nailed up at either end of his route. He had read Dane’s notice in Natchez. Going north, he had passed the ford while Dane was trading for horses, and had seen the twelve in the yard.

He met them again a fortnight later, coming south, at the Tennessee River, where the Colberts, who were Chickasaw, kept the ferry. The twelve were sitting on the south bank waiting for the boat. Their meal had run out in the second week and they were gaunt again. Vance led his horse off the ferry and stopped in front of them.

You are Dane’s men. Where is Dane?

Leeper told him all of it, the men up the path and the men down the road, and Dane sitting on his keg.

He is building himself a store, Leeper said. We came on.

And nobody troubled you.

They looked at us.

Vance let the horse drink. He was a man who had whole days to think in, and he did not hurry over it.

I ride through those men twice a month, he said. They have walked out of the cane and asked me the news as civil as deacons. They have never laid a hand on these bags. It is paper, and they cannot spend it. You went through the same way, with nothing on you. Dane has what they want and one pair of hands to keep it with. He knows that, so he stays off that road. He does not go a little way up it to see.

Then he told them about the lion. His mother had owned one book besides the Bible, he said, and it was the Fables. The lion in it was too old to hunt, so he lay down in his cave and gave out that he was sick. The beasts came one at a time to ask after him. The fox came last and stood well outside and wished him better. Why do you not come in? said the lion, and the fox said he had been looking at the ground.

I find the Traces of abundance of Feet going into your Majesty’s Palace, and not one that comes back again.

— Aesop, A Fox and a Sick Lion, tr. L’Estrange

Now, said Vance. You will get home, and next spring you will come down the river and be paid again. The cards will be there under the hill, and the women, and the whiskey, the same as this year. You know that ground. You have seen whose tracks go in. There is something laid up beside any road a man goes down wanting. Keep off the wrong ones the way Dane keeps off this one. None of you would eat jimson seed to find out if what they say about it is so.

That is hard talk to a man with six dollars, somebody said.

You had forty, said Vance.

He got up on the horse and blew the trumpet once for the Colberts’ people on the far side, out of habit, and rode up the south bank the way they had come.

· · ·

Absalom Leeper came down the river again the next spring, steering for a man from Wheeling. He was paid at the landing. He walked up the bluff with the forty dollars past every door under the hill, and it is told of him that he counted the doors.

At Grindstone Ford there was a store with a shingle roof and a counter made of wagon boards. He bought a pair of shoes there and paid in hard money. Mayhew Dane counted out his change, and then counted it again. Leeper went down to the water and crossed. In the mud on the north side were the tracks of the men ahead of him, all pointing one way. Over them, coming down to the ford, lay the print of one shod horse.

Background

The verse

Let a man avoid evil deeds, as a merchant, if he has few companions and carries much wealth, avoids a dangerous road; as a man who loves life avoids poison.Dhammapada 123, tr. F. Max Müller (1881)

In the commentary, five hundred thieves fail to break into the house of the merchant Mahā Dhana (“Great Wealth”). When he sets out with five hundred carts, he invites five hundred monks to travel with him and promises them food on the way. The thieves hide in a forest ahead. The merchant halts at a village at the forest’s edge, and the thieves’ scout asks a friend in the village when the merchant will leave. The friend decides to warn the merchant, because the monks are living on his bounty. The merchant resolves to turn back; the thieves move to the road behind him; warned again, he decides to go neither forward nor back and stays where he is. The monks return to the Buddha, who makes the merchant’s prudence a lesson: avoid evil as he avoids that road, and as a man who wants to live avoids poison. Read the full commentary story.

The Natchez road, 1803

Before steamboats, farmers and merchants of the Ohio valley floated their produce down to Natchez and New Orleans on flatboats, sold the cargo and the boat, and walked or rode home. The way home was the Natchez Trace, some five hundred miles of path through the Choctaw and Chickasaw nations to Nashville. The boatmen were called Kaintucks wherever they came from. Grindstone Ford on Bayou Pierre marked the line between the Natchez District and the Choctaw nation; a plain stand there fed travelers on mush and milk. At the Tennessee River the Chickasaw Colbert family kept the ferry. Men going north carried their season’s earnings in coin, and robbers worked the road and the river. The best known, Samuel Mason, was killed in 1803. Everyone in this story is invented.

Congress made the Trace a post road in 1800. The mail went by single riders, who carried a blanket, corn for the horse and a tin trumpet. John Swaney, who rode the route in these years, remembered that Mason often stopped him to ask the news and told him no mail carrier need fear his men, since mail was of no value to them; the reminiscence is given in Otto Rothert’s The Outlaws of Cave-in-Rock (1924). The first post office at Natchez opened in November 1799. The fable the rider tells is the old one of the fox and the sick lion, quoted here from Sir Roger L’Estrange’s English Aesop of 1692, a version much reprinted in the century that followed.
