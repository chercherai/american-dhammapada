Pipe Ash

So imprudent are we that we wander in the times which are not ours, and do not think of the only one which belongs to us.
— Pascal, Pensées 172

C’est comme ça que je l’ai entendu conter, on the River Raisin.

I. The Town Inside the Pickets

Detroit · May 1805

The four of them came up from the River Raisin at the end of April in a trader’s bateau with forty bushels of seed wheat and a crate of hens, and the curé of Detroit, who had asked the curé at the Raisin for girls who could read, looked them over on the landing and nodded.

They were Marie-Louise Couture, who was seventeen and the oldest; Archange Duval, who was sixteen and laughed at everything; Thérèse Bourdeau, who was sixteen and laughed at nothing; and Félicité Jacob, who was fifteen, and whose mother had cried on the bank as if the bateau were going to France. All four had learned their letters at the same kitchen table on the river road below the Raisin, from the widow Laforêt, who had been to the Ursulines at Quebec when she was a girl, before the English came. Now they were to learn how to teach other children their letters, at the curé’s academy for young ladies, and come home in the fall and do it.

Detroit was the largest place any of them had seen. It sat on its bank inside a fence of cedar pickets taller than a man, with gates that were shut at night, and inside the pickets the houses stood so close along the little streets that a woman on one gallery could hand a cup of sugar to a woman on the next. Above it, on the hill, was the old English fort with the American flag on it. Below it was the river, a mile wide and green, and on the other side the English shore, where the same French families lived under King George and kept their own church.

They boarded with the Beaugrands on the Rue Sainte-Anne, in a house of squared logs with a loft. Madame Beaugrand had five children and a husband in the fur trade and a heart, the girls agreed, like a bread oven. She gave them the loft and a feather tick. She gave them white bread on weekdays, which they had at home only on feasts. She gave them whitefish from the river, and pea soup with salt pork, and in the last week of May the first wild strawberries from the côte, a bowl of them on the table at noon with cream poured over.

In the mornings they sat in a room behind the church with four girls of good Detroit families and copied out fair hands, and did sums, and learned to cut a quill, and sang. In the afternoons they sewed. In the evenings they walked out the gate with the Beaugrand children to look at the pear trees in blossom along the côte, the old French pears that had been planted, people said, when Cadillac came, and that were taller than the houses.

It was the best month of Archange’s life, and she said so every night in the loft. Thérèse did not say so, but she ate her strawberries slowly.

The town had a law about fire, and Monsieur Beaugrand showed it to them, pleased with it, the way a man shows off a new gun. Every house had to keep its chimney swept. Every house had to keep a barrel of water full, and buckets, and a ladder long enough to reach the roof. The Beaugrands’ ladder leaned against the back of the house, and their barrel stood under the eave with a skin of pollen on the water. Between the law and the pickets and the fort on the hill, Monsieur Beaugrand said, Detroit was as safe as any place on the lakes.

II. The Eleventh of June

Detroit · a Tuesday

It started a little after nine in the morning, in a stable. The girls were at their copybooks. Marie-Louise smelled it first and thought it was somebody’s bread.

By the time they reached the street the stable was gone and the barn beside it was going, and the wind was up off the river, a dry hot June wind, and it took the burning hay out of the barn in pieces the size of a hand and laid them on the shingles of the houses all down the street, one roof and then the next and then the next, the way you lay out plates.

The men made a line from the river with buckets. The girls got into it. Marie-Louise stood between a Scotch merchant in a good coat and an old Panis woman who belonged to somebody, and passed the full buckets up and the empty ones down until her palms bled, and every bucket went up the line and was thrown at a roof and was nothing. Behind her somebody had pulled the Beaugrands’ ladder down from the back wall and was carrying it toward the fire, and then had dropped it in the street and was running, and the ladder lay there and caught.

They went out the gate with what they could carry. Madame Beaugrand had the baby and the clock. Félicité had the feather tick. Archange, who could never afterward explain it, had the bowl the strawberries had been in. On the commons outside the pickets the whole town stood in the grass with its furniture around it, beds and kettles and chests of drawers and a spinning wheel, a crucifix, a cage with a jay in it, and watched the town burn inside its own fence.

The church burned. The pickets burned, a whole wall of them standing up in fire at once like a row of candles. By the middle of the afternoon there was nothing inside the line where the pickets had been but chimneys and smoke, and nothing left standing in Detroit but the fort on the hill and one warehouse by the water. No one had died. People kept saying it to each other on the commons, as if it were a thing you could put in your hand. Nobody is dead. Nobody is dead.

Toward evening the curé came out among them with his sleeves black to the elbow. He had men and boys going up and down the river in canoes, carrying bread and blankets to the families who had gone out to the farms, and he stood a while in the smoke looking at where his church had been, and said something in Latin that the girls did not understand, and went back to the canoes.

That night they slept in the grass of the commons with the Beaugrand children in a heap around them, and Marie-Louise lay on her back and watched the ash come down out of the dark, soft and gray, and settle on her face, and on Archange’s face beside her, and on the strawberry bowl.

III. The Rest of the Summer

The côte above the ruins · June to August

Within a week every farmhouse for a league up the river was full. The Beaugrands went into the house of a cousin above the town, eleven people in two rooms, and the four girls from the Raisin went into the cousin’s hayloft.

Nobody had time for them. That was all it was. Nobody was unkind. The men were in the woods all day cutting new pickets and squaring logs, or in the ashes all day pulling out nails and hinges and the iron of stoves, or arguing at the top of their voices about whose lot had been where, because the stakes had burned. The women were cooking for twice as many people with half as many pots. At the beginning of July the new governor arrived with the judges and could not find a bed for a week, and after that there were surveyors in the ashes with chains, laying out streets that went every which way, wide as fields, and the men stopped cutting pickets to stand and shout at the surveyors. The academy did not meet. The room behind the church was not there.

The girls carried water. They minded the cousin’s children and the Beaugrands’ children together, which was nine children. They ate when the others had eaten, cold galette and whatever was left, and some nights nothing was left, and Madame Beaugrand, whose heart was still like a bread oven, did not notice, because she had not slept a whole night since the eleventh of June. The mosquitoes came up off the river in the evenings. The hay in the loft was last year’s and full of mice. Thérèse got a fever and got over it with nobody but the other three to sit with her.

Everybody on the côte had a story about how it started. It was a baker, people said, a man named Harvey, who had knocked out his pipe in his own stable against a post, the way a man does a thousand times, and the wind had done the rest. Others said no, it was set on purpose, by somebody with an interest in lumber. The governor himself was supposed to believe that. Monsieur Beaugrand believed it was the English. The girls heard every version a dozen times across the cousin’s table, and the men who told them looked toward the river and the English shore when they said it, and toward the woods, and never toward the stable.

On the feast of the Assumption, the fifteenth of August, Marie-Louise came back from Mass with the other three and said they were going home.

IV. The Widow’s Kitchen

The River Raisin · the end of August

They went down the river with the same trader who had brought them up, past the islands and the Wyandot town at Brownstown, and out along the low shore of Lake Erie where the marsh went on for miles with nothing in it but red-winged blackbirds, and turned in at the mouth of the Raisin, which wound back and forth so much that a man could walk across a bend in the time it took a boat to go round. They walked the last of it on the river road, under the orchards, with their bundles.

The widow Laforêt was in her kitchen, where she always was, at the end of a ribbon of land so narrow that her house took up most of the river end of it. She was seventy and small and brown as a walnut. She was sitting by her hearth in the summer evening with no fire in it but a few coals under the ash, smoking her short clay pipe, and she did not get up.

Well, she said. Sit. Did it go well with you? Was it pleasant?

They sat on the bench along the wall where they had sat with their slates when they were eight years old, and it all came out of them at once, the four of them talking over each other. The first month, the white bread, the strawberries, the pear trees, the copybooks. And then the fire. And then the hayloft and the mice and the nine children and the cold galette. How nobody had taught them anything after the eleventh of June, how they had not opened a book, how Thérèse had had a fever with nobody to sit with her, how the people of Detroit had had no time all summer for anything but their pickets and their lots and their quarrels.

The widow listened to all of it. When they were finished she took the pipe out of her mouth and leaned forward and knocked it out, three small knocks, on the hearthstone, inside the fire irons. Then she put her heel on the little heap of ash and turned it, back and forth, until it was gray powder. The girls had watched her do that their whole lives. There was a round place on the hearthstone, paler than the rest, where she had done it for fifty years.

Never mind, she said. Don’t take it to heart. A good bed is hard to come by, in any year, in any town. You were guests. Guests are fed when there is time.

But there was never time, said Archange.

No. They were busy. She filled the pipe again from a bladder of tobacco, slowly, pressing it down with her thumb. Tell me what they were busy with.

Marie-Louise told her. The pickets. The new pickets, higher than the old ones. The lots, the stakes, the governor’s streets. Watching the river. Watching the woods.

And where did the fire come from?

Nobody said anything. Thérèse, who laughed at nothing, looked at the round pale place on the hearthstone.

From a pipe, she said. Inside the pickets. In a man’s own stable.

The widow lit her pipe with a coal held in the tongs and drew on it until it caught, and the bowl glowed and dimmed. Then she reached behind her to the shelf over the hearth, where she kept her salt and her rosary and one book, and took the book down. It was small and brown and the corners were soft as cloth. Her father had given it to her at Quebec. She opened it where it opened by itself and read to them, first in French, and then, because Félicité’s French was not good enough for Monsieur Pascal, slowly, in her own words.

I have discovered that all the unhappiness of men arises from one single fact, that they cannot stay quietly in their own chamber. A man who has enough to live on, if he knew how to stay with pleasure at home, would not leave it to go to sea or to besiege a town.

— Pascal, Pensées 139

So, said the widow. Guard your town. All of it. The pickets, yes. And the stable.

She turned some pages.

So imprudent are we that we wander in the times which are not ours, and do not think of the only one which belongs to us; and so idle are we that we dream of those times which are no more, and thoughtlessly overlook that which alone exists.

— Pascal, Pensées 172

You were in Detroit four months, she said, and closed the book. For one of them you ate strawberries. For three of them you waited for somebody to give you your supper and your lessons. The people of Detroit had their whole town to build in one summer, and they built it. What did you build?

She was not angry. She said it the way she had once told each of them, at eight years old, that the letter on her slate was backward.

The time that belongs to you is this one, she said. It was the hayloft. It was nine children. Nine children, and four of you who know how to read.

Archange began to laugh, and then stopped, and put her face in her hands.

They did not go home that night. They sat on the bench in the widow’s kitchen with the door open to the river, and nobody said very much. The widow smoked. Toward midnight she knocked out her pipe, three knocks, inside the fire irons, and turned the ash under her heel, and drew the coals together and covered them, and went to bed. The four of them stayed on the bench in the dark. Something had come into the room that none of them could have named, and none of them wanted to be the first to stand up and lose it. It was not a feeling. It was more like the moment after a bell, when the whole air has been rung and goes on being rung. Marie-Louise could hear the river going round its bends, and the frogs, and each of the other three breathing, and she knew without any doubt that this, the bench, the dark, the breathing, was the only time she would ever have, and that it was enough, and that she had nearly let a whole summer of it go by her while she waited for supper.

In the morning they walked to their mothers’ houses. In October they went back up the river.

•••

The next summer the militia at the Raisin built a stockade of their own, two hundred feet on a side, with a blockhouse, and everybody on the river road helped cut the pickets. By then Marie-Louise Couture was teaching twenty-two children their letters in a rented room in the new Detroit, at the corner of two of the governor’s streets, and Thérèse was teaching in the room next to her, and there was a law in that school, which they had made themselves and wrote on the wall in a fair hand, that nobody, not the curé, not the governor, not the children’s fathers when they came to fetch them, knocked out a pipe anywhere in that house but on the hearthstone, inside the irons. Most of them laughed. They did it anyway.

At night, after the children had gone, Marie-Louise went round the two rooms with a candle the way the widow went round her kitchen, and drew the coals together and covered them, and stood a moment in the dark with her hand on the warm stone, before she went up.

The verse

Like a well-guarded frontier fort, with defences within and without, so let a man guard himself. Not a moment should escape, for they who allow the right moment to pass, suffer pain when they are in hell.Dhammapada 315, tr. F. Max Müller (1881)

In the commentary, a company of monks spends the rains retreat in a village on the frontier. The first month is pleasant. In the second, bandits raid the village and carry some of its people off, and from then on the villagers are so busy strengthening their town that they have no time to look after their guests, who pass the rest of the retreat in discomfort. Afterward the monks go back to the Buddha at Jetavana and tell him how hard it was. He tells them not to be troubled, since a comfortable lodging is hard to find at any time, and that a monk should guard himself as those villagers guarded their town, inside and out, and not let the moment pass. Hearing the verse, the monks are moved to urgency and reach awakening. Read the full commentary story, or about the rains retreat (vassa).

The widow’s book is Blaise Pascal’s Pensées, quoted here in the English translation by W. F. Trotter (1910).

Detroit, 1805

In 1805 Detroit was a town of a few hundred people, most of them French-speaking, packed into a few acres of log and frame houses inside a timber stockade on the river, with the old British-built Fort Lernoult on the rise behind. A town ordinance of 1802 required every household to sweep its chimneys and to keep a barrel of water, buckets and a ladder. On the morning of June 11, 1805, a fire began in or near the stable of a baker named John Harvey (the story that he knocked out his pipe there has never been proved), and by afternoon the whole town was gone except the fort and a warehouse. No one died. Michigan Territory’s new governor, William Hull, arrived on July 1 and wrote that every house was crowded and that he needed more than a week to find a place to stay. Judge Augustus Woodward laid out a new town of wide radiating avenues. See the Great Fire of 1805 and the Detroit Historical Society’s account.

The pastor of Ste. Anne’s, the Sulpician Gabriel Richard, had opened an academy for young ladies in 1804 to train teachers for the schools he planned; after the fire he sent canoes up and down the river with food and blankets for the burned-out families. His words in the ashes, Speramus meliora; resurget cineribus (“We hope for better things; it will rise from the ashes”), became the city’s motto. Forty miles south, French families from Detroit had settled the River Raisin from 1785 on long narrow “ribbon farms” running back from the water; in 1806 the militia there built a stockade with a blockhouse. See Frenchtown at River Raisin National Battlefield Park.
