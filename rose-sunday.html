Rose Sunday

… and others which, Sabbath after Sabbath, looked devoutly heavenward, and benignantly over the crowded pews, from the holiest pulpits in the land.
— Nathaniel Hawthorne, “Young Goodman Brown”

This is the way I heard it, on Ashmont Street.

I. Father Rafferty

Dorchester, Boston · 1959–2002

For thirty-five years Frank Dineen drew a priest. The strip was called Father Rafferty, four panels a week, and it ran in diocesan papers from Boston to Sacramento between 1959 and 1994. Father Rafferty was a curate in a city parish with a bad knee and a worse car. He lost at checkers to the altar boys. He hid from the pastor in the confessional to eat his lunch. He had a round face and a cowlick, and Frank drew around his head, whenever he was doing something kind, five or six short pen lines, the way you draw a lamp to show that it is lit. Readers wrote in about the lines. Nuns cut the strip out and pinned it to bulletin boards. Frank drew it at a slant-top table in the attic of a three-decker on Ashmont Street, and for the first eleven years the model for Father Rafferty's face was whichever curate was newest at St. Ita's down the hill.

He retired the strip when his hand began to shake, and after his wife died he kept on drawing for himself, people on the Red Line, his neighbors, the gulls at Malibu Beach. He had a way of seeing a face. His wife used to say he could look at a man across a wake and tell you what the man's mother had looked like.

In January of 2002 the Globe began to print what it had found, and it did not stop all year. By the fall there were court files on the kitchen tables of Dorchester, and names in them that people had been baptized and married by. Frank found that he could not draw a priest. He tried in the spring, with his nephew. Kevin Dineen was ordained in May, at twenty-seven, one of a small class, in the worst year anyone could remember to be a young man in a Roman collar on the Red Line, and Frank sat down to draw him as a gift and put the pen down after the cowlick. He could not get the face to hold still. It kept turning into somebody else's.

Kevin was sent to St. Ita's in June. Nora Healy was still the sacristan there. She had been the sacristan since 1951.

II. Parker Hill

Mission Hill · Saturday, December 14

On the Saturday after the cardinal resigned they buried Frank's old friend Tom Grealish from the Mission Church on Tremont Street, the big gray basilica with the two spires on the side of Parker Hill. Kevin concelebrated. Afterward, because it was a dry cold afternoon and Frank wanted the air, the two of them walked down through the streets below the hill toward the Orange Line, Kevin in his black overcoat and collar, Frank in his good hat. People looked at the collar. One man on a stoop said something, and Kevin nodded to him as if he had said good afternoon.

It was nearly dark. The sun had gone down behind the hill and the sky over the city was green going to blue. They had come to the corner of Tremont and were waiting for the light when Frank stopped talking.

Kevin looked at him and then looked where he was looking, up over the roofs, and saw nothing. A plane coming into Logan. The first stars.

Frank was smiling. It was not a big smile. It was the smile Kevin had seen on him at the drafting table when a face finally came right, a small private one, at one side of the mouth.

Uncle Frank. What is it?

Not here, said Frank. Ask me at Nora's. Ask me in front of Nora.

He did not say anything else all the way home on the train.

III. The Sacristy

St. Ita's, Dorchester · Sunday, December 15

The next morning was the third Sunday of Advent, Gaudete Sunday, when the purple of the season lets up for a day and the priest wears rose. Nora Healy had the rose vestments out on the long counter in the sacristy at seven, the chasuble on its padded hanger, the stole, the alb pressed the night before, the amice folded square, the cincture coiled in a flat white spiral like a rope on the deck of a boat. She was eighty. She had ironed for every priest in that house for fifty-one years, and three women from the altar guild who were not much younger were with her, folding purificators at the table under the window. Frank came in with Kevin at a quarter past seven and sat on the radiator in his coat.

Kevin started to vest. He put the amice on his shoulders and tied the strings, and then he stopped.

Ask him, said Nora, without turning around. She had been watching them since they came in.

Yesterday on Tremont Street, Kevin said. He smiled at something. Up over the roofs. I asked him what it was and he said to ask him in front of you.

The women at the table had stopped folding.

Frank took off his hat.

Six priests, he said. Going over the city at about the height of the steeple, toward the harbor. The first one in front and five behind him. They were vested for Mass, the way you vest them, Nora, alb and stole and chasuble, and every bit of it was on fire. The cinctures were burning like fuses. They had their chalices in their hands, and the chalices were on fire. And there was nothing inside the vestments. There was the shape of a man, and the fire went right through him, and you could see the bones.

Nobody said anything.

I knew the faces, Frank said. I drew two of them.

And you smiled, said Kevin.

For thirty-five years I put lines around their heads, Frank said. Last night they looked like themselves.

Nora Healy came around the end of the counter and sat down on the vesting stool, which no one had ever seen her do.

I'll tell you who they were, she said, and then I won't say it again.

She told them about the old days in that house, when there were four priests in the rectory and a Mass every hour on Sunday morning and boys waiting in line in cassocks to serve. She did not tell them anything that was not already in the newspapers, and she did not say the names, and everyone in that sacristy knew the names. She told them about a boy who came out of the rectory one evening in 1964 and sat on the curb by the sacristy door and would not tell her what was wrong, and how she had gone to the pastor, and the pastor had told her that her business was the linens. She said she had minded the linens for thirty-eight years after that. She said she had washed their albs and starched them and coiled their cinctures, and polished their chalices on Saturday nights, and that one of the six chalices was still in the safe behind her, where its owner had left it in 1979 when he was moved, and that she had not let it be used since.

Many, she said. I'm not saying all. I'm saying many. They wore it at the neck and it meant nothing to them. And they went where those deeds were going to take them. They were always going there. Frank just saw them on the way.

Then she said that Sister Gertrude had made them read a story in the eleventh grade at the Academy, in 1938, about a young man in Salem who goes into the woods one night and finds the whole church there in the firelight, the ministers and the deacons and the good women, and that she had never forgotten one sentence of it, and she said it:

Among them, quivering to and fro between gloom and splendor, appeared faces that would be seen next day at the council board of the province, and others which, Sabbath after Sabbath, looked devoutly heavenward, and benignantly over the crowded pews, from the holiest pulpits in the land.

— Nathaniel Hawthorne, “Young Goodman Brown,” Mosses from an Old Manse (1846)

And then Sister made us read to the end, Nora said, which is the part I want you to hear, Father. He never trusted anybody again after that night, the young man. Not his wife, not his minister, not one soul. He went to his grave like that. That's the other way to go to hell. I'm not having it in this house.

She got up off the stool.

You're late, she said to Kevin. Arms up.

He put his arms up and she dropped the alb over him and pulled it straight at the shoulders, and handed him the cincture, and he tied it. She laid the stole across his neck. The three women at the table had not gone back to the purificators. One of them, Mrs. Coughlin, had a grandson who had served in that sacristy in the sixties and had never come back to Mass, and she was crying without any noise, and folding, and not looking at anybody. The second one had put her hand flat on the table. The third, the youngest, who was seventy-three, got up and went into the church and knelt in the front pew with her coat on, and did not come back until the bell.

Kevin stood in his rose vestments in the middle of the sacristy. He was twenty-seven years old. He looked down at the cincture knotted at his waist, and then at the safe, and then at Nora.

All right, he said.

IV. Rose

St. Ita's · the same morning

Frank stayed on the radiator through the eight o'clock. He could see a slice of the sanctuary through the sacristy door, Kevin at the altar in rose, which on a young man in that light looks almost white, the color of the inside of a shell. Mrs. Coughlin's grandson was not there. The church was a third full, which in December of 2002 was a good Sunday.

There was a pad of Mass cards by the sacristy phone. Frank took one, and the ballpoint pen on its chain, and turned the card over and drew Kevin from where he sat, in profile, lifting the host at the consecration, the chasuble falling from his raised arms, the cowlick standing up at the back. It took him about a minute. His hand did not shake.

When he was done he looked at it a long time. Then he left it on the counter by the rose stole's empty hanger, face up, for Nora, with no lines around the head.

Background

The verse

Many men whose shoulders are covered with the yellow gown are ill-conditioned and unrestrained; such evil-doers by their evil deeds go to hell.Dhammapada 307, tr. F. Max Müller (1881)

In the commentary, the elder Mahā Moggallāna, known for his psychic powers, is coming down from Vulture’s Peak with the elder Lakkhaṇa when he sees hungry ghosts (petas) in the form of skeletons, and smiles. Lakkhaṇa asks why. This is not the time, Moggallāna says; ask me in the presence of the Teacher. Before the Buddha he tells what he saw: a monk flying through the air with his body on fire, and five companions behind him, their bowls, robes and girdles all burning. The Buddha explains that these were monks who had gone forth under an earlier Buddha, Kassapa, and had not lived by what they took on, and he speaks the verse to the monks sitting before him; many of them reach the first stage of awakening. The Pāli verse speaks of those with the ochre robe “around the neck.” Read the full commentary story. Nora’s sentence is from Nathaniel Hawthorne’s “Young Goodman Brown”, first published in 1835 and collected in Mosses from an Old Manse (1846); the story ends with Brown living out his life in suspicion of everyone he knows.

Boston, 2002

In January 2002 the Boston Globe’s Spotlight team began publishing its investigation of how the Archdiocese of Boston had handled priests who abused children, beginning with John Geoghan, who was accused of abusing more than 130 children and had been moved from parish to parish. The coverage ran all year. It led to criminal prosecutions, hundreds of civil suits and, in 2003, a Pulitzer Prize for Public Service; in 2011 the archdiocese published a list of 159 priests credibly accused. See the sexual abuse scandal in the Boston archdiocese.

In early December the archdiocese released thousands of pages of personnel files under court order. On December 9, fifty-eight Boston priests signed a letter asking Cardinal Bernard Law to resign, and on Friday, December 13, he resigned, as America reported. Two days later came the third Sunday of Advent, called Gaudete or Rose Sunday, when the priest may wear rose instead of violet. The Mission Church, the Redemptorists’ basilica on Parker Hill in Mission Hill, was dedicated in 1878. St. Ita’s and its people are fictional.
