The Hem

Because man shall go into the house of his eternity, and the mourners shall go round about in the street.
— Ecclesiastes 12:5

Así lo oí contar, in Agaña.

I. The Rounds

Agaña, on Guam · the hungry year, 1706

Nobody in Agaña knew how old she was. She had been an old woman already when the padres came, and that was thirty-eight years ago. The padres had baptized her Isabel, but most people called her the one from lågu, because she had come down from the islands to the north, from the north and from the sea side of everything, when the soldiers brought the last of her people to Guam in the boats that were not burned. She was the last one left of all those boats. Her husband was dead, and her sons, and her sons’ sons, in the fighting and the sicknesses and the crossing. She lived with a widow from Pago in a house of wood and thatch below the church, and every morning, while it was still cool, she went on her rounds.

She went to the houses where somebody was sick or somebody had died. There was always one. In the year of the spotted sickness there had been so many that she went to eleven houses in a morning, and in this year, the hungry year, they were dying again, slower, of the hunger and the sickness that comes after the hunger. She went in and knelt on the mat by whoever was lying there and led the prayers the padres had taught, the Our Father and the Hail Mary and the long ones for the dead, in a high hard voice that went up through the thatch. The household answered her. When she was young she had wailed over the dead in the old way, the way the women did it then, and some of the old people said that the new prayers in her mouth sounded like the old wailing and that was why she was asked for. She did not say whether they did.

Afterward the household gave her what it had. A little cake of fadang, washed many days in the stream until the poison was out of it. A handful of rice in a leaf. A piece of breadfruit. In the hungry year it was very little, and sometimes it was nothing, and then they apologized and she touched their faces and went on to the next house. What she was given she carried home in a little basket of coconut leaf, plaited tight, that she had made herself, and in the evening she and the widow from Pago ate it.

She walked slowly. She wore the long skirt that the padres had urged on all the women when they came, the cloth that came on the ship from Manila, and she had never in thirty-eight years got used to the weight of it around her legs. She had got smaller, too, the way the very old get smaller, so that the skirt she had worn for twenty years now dragged in the dust behind her heels. The widow from Pago kept saying she would cut it. She had not cut it.

II. The Boy

The plaza wall · the last days of May

On the way home on the first day she met a boy from the colegio.

The colegio stood beside the church on the plaza, and the boys of Agaña went there from the time they were small to learn their letters and the doctrine and to sing in the church. This one was perhaps eleven. He had the high voice that the padres put at the front of the singing, and he was so thin that his shoulders stood up under his shirt like the frame of a roof. He was sitting on the plaza wall in the shade with his knees drawn up, waiting for something or for nothing.

She stopped in front of him and held out the basket.

May I give you this?

He looked at it, and at her. Then he said yes, and took it with both hands, the way the padres had taught the boys to take a thing that is given, and opened it. There was a cake of fadang in it and a little rice. He ate all of it there on the wall, slowly, while she stood and watched him, and then he handed her back the basket. She went home with it empty.

The second day she met him in the same place, on the wall in the same shade, and asked him the same thing, and he said yes. The third day it was the same. On each of the three days she gave him all she had been given, and on each of the three evenings she told the widow from Pago that she had eaten at the houses, which was not true, and lay down in the dark with the hunger moving in her like a small animal looking for a way out.

III. The Procession

The plaza · Corpus Christi, June 3

The fourth day was the great feast, and all of Agaña was in the plaza.

They had built altars at the corners under arches of palm, and the soldiers of the garrison stood in two lines in their coats with their muskets. The bells began before the sun was over the hill. The procession came out of the church with the padres under a canopy and the Host in its gold sun, and the boys of the colegio walked ahead of it in rows, two by two, singing the doctrine, and the men with the trumpets and the shawms came behind. At each altar the whole crowd went down on its knees in the dust while a song was sung, and then got up again and moved on, and the soldiers fired a volley into the air that made the children shriek and the dogs run under the houses. Every Chamorro left on the island who could walk to Agaña seemed to be there, all of them in their Manila cloth, pressed together along the edges of the plaza in the heat.

She had not gone on her rounds that morning, because of the feast. She stood near the colegio wall with the widow from Pago and watched the boys come by. She saw her boy among them, in the front row, with his mouth open in the high part of the song. She was light in the head. The bright plaza moved a little, the way the sea moves under a canoe.

Walking at the side of the boys, keeping them in their rows, was the maestro.

He was a Chamorro of Agaña, a man of perhaps fifty, and he had been one of the first boys of the colegio himself, in the year it was built, when the walls were thatch and the padres who taught him were the ones who were later killed. He had learned the letters and the doctrine and the padres’ tongue, and when he was grown they had made him the teacher of the doctrine to the children, and he had done it ever since. He taught in the plaza in the mornings, sitting on a stone. He said very little. When the children did not know their answers he waited until they did. People who had a quarrel brought it to him, and he listened to the whole of it and said one thing, and the quarrel was usually over. He was the only one in Agaña, it was said, whom the padres asked for his opinion and the old people asked for theirs.

He came along the edge of the crowd beside the boys, and the crowd made room for him, and when he came to where she stood, the old woman stepped back to let him pass. She stepped back onto the hem of her skirt. Her heel came down on the cloth, and the cloth held, and she went over backward into the dust under the colegio wall, all at once, like a bundle set down.

The singing went on. The canopy went on. The maestro stopped.

He left the boys and came and knelt in the dust beside her, and put his hand under her head. The widow from Pago was kneeling on her other side. People were turning to look. The old woman lay with her eyes open, looking up at him, and felt how light she was, and how far away her feet were at the end of her, tangled in the cloth.

Grandmother, said the maestro. You have not eaten.

She did not say anything.

Three days you gave it to the boy. He did not ask how she knew he knew. This body is worn out, grandmother. It has carried you a long way, from lågu and all the way here. It is worn out, and soon it will come apart.

He said it plainly, the way he told a child the answer to a question in the doctrine, and then, kneeling there, he said to her the words of the Preacher that the padres had taught him long ago and that he had put into their own language for the old people, so that they could hear it. She had heard it before. She had heard it at the houses of the dead.

Remember thy Creator in the days of thy youth, before the time of affliction come, and the years draw nigh of which thou shalt say: They please me not:

Before the sun, and the light, and the moon, and the stars be darkened, and the clouds return after the rain:

When the keepers of the house shall tremble, and the strong men shall stagger, and the grinders shall be idle in a small number, and they that look through the holes shall be darkened:

And they shall shut the doors in the street, when the grinder’s voice shall be low, and they shall rise up at the voice of the bird, and all the daughters of music shall grow deaf.

And they shall fear high things, and they shall be afraid in the way, the almond tree shall flourish, the locust shall be made fat, and the caper tree shall be destroyed: because man shall go into the house of his eternity, and the mourners shall go round about in the street.

Before the silver cord be broken, and the golden fillet shrink back, and the pitcher be crushed at the fountain, and the wheel be broken upon the cistern,

And the dust return into its earth, from whence it was, and the spirit return to God, who gave it.

— Ecclesiastes 12:1–7

The mourners shall go round about in the street. She had gone round about in the street every morning for thirty-eight years. She had knelt by perhaps a thousand bodies, more, and touched them, and prayed them out of the world in the high voice, and walked home with what their people gave her. She had never once, in all of it, thought that the body on the mat was the same kind of thing as the body she knelt in. She thought it now, lying in the dust of the plaza with the shawms going by. The keepers of the house trembling. The grinders few. The hem gone long because the one inside it had grown short. It was not a thing that was going to happen to her. It was a thing that was happening, that had always been happening, to the whole of her, the way the stream works the poison out of the fadang a day at a time.

She began to laugh. It was a small sound, and the widow from Pago thought she was weeping, and then saw that she was not.

The people standing nearest had heard all of it. A woman from Agat who had buried four children in the spotted sickness knelt down in the dust beside the widow. An old man went down on his knees too, and then a soldier’s wife, and for a little while there was a ring of them there under the colegio wall, kneeling around the old woman while the procession went on to the next altar without them and the volley went off and the dogs ran. Some of them said afterward that they had understood something there that they had not understood at any of the altars. They did not say what. The maestro stayed until she could sit up. Then he went back to his boys.

IV. The Leaf

The house below the church · that evening

At dusk the boy from the colegio came to the door of the house below the church. He had a cake of fadang in his hands, wrapped in a leaf, from the padres’ kitchen.

He held it out to her.

May I give you this?

She said yes. She sat on the step and ate all of it, slowly, while he stood and watched, and when it was gone she smoothed the leaf flat on her knee and handed it back to him, and he went up the hill to the colegio in the last light, folding it smaller and smaller as he walked.

Background

The verse

This body is wasted, full of sickness, and frail; this heap of corruption breaks to pieces, life indeed ends in death.Dhammapada 148, tr. F. Max Müller (1881)

In the commentary, the nun Uttarī is a hundred and twenty years old and still goes on her alms round every day. One day, coming back with food in her bowl, she meets a monk in the street, asks whether she may give it to him, and gives him all of it. She does the same on the second day and the third, and has none left for herself. On the fourth day she meets the Buddha in a crowded place, steps back to make way, treads on the hem of her robe and falls. He tells her that her body is worn out with age and will soon break up, and speaks the verse. At the end of it she enters the stream, and many others who hear it are helped. Guam, 1706

The Jesuit mission of Diego Luis de San Vitores reached Guam in 1668, and the Spanish–Chamorro Wars that followed lasted almost thirty years. War, the destruction of the canoes, forced removals from the northern islands and repeated epidemics (among them smallpox in 1699 or 1700) brought the Chamorro people near to vanishing; deaths from hunger are recorded in 1706, and a Spanish census of 1710 counted about 3,500 Chamorros alive. The survivors were gathered into six church-centered villages, Agaña (Hågatña) chief among them (Guampedia). Women who had worn little before the mission began to cover themselves at the missionaries’ urging. On Guam, lågu means north, and in its older sense, toward the sea. Flour from the seeds of the native cycad, fadang, was a traditional food on the island until the 1960s.

San Vitores founded the Colegio de San Juan de Letrán in Agaña in 1669 for Chamorro boys, who learned their letters and the doctrine there; mission reports describe the children singing the doctrine in procession, and the Corpus Christi feast of 1677 with altars, songs at each station, dances and militia volleys. The techa, the lay prayer leader, most often an elderly woman, who leads the rosaries and novenas for the dead in a high, steady chant, is thought to date from at least the Spanish period; her chanting has been compared to the mourning of the dead at ancient Chamorro funerals. The old woman, the boy and the maestro are invented.
