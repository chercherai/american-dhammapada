Heart and Sap

A merry Life and a short one, shall be my Motto.
— Captain Charles Johnson, A General History of the Pyrates (1724)

This is how the old people told it, on Harbour Island.

I. Braziletto

Harbour Island and Spanish Wells, off Eleuthera · 1695 to 1716

Two women at the north end of Eleuthera were brought to bed on the same day. One was a Sands, of Harbour Island, and her son was named Tobias. The other was an Albury, of Spanish Wells, two hours' sail to the westward, and her son was named Cornelius. The Sandses and the Alburys had been friends for three generations, since the night their grandfathers came in off the reef called the Devil's Backbone holding to the same spar, and when one house had a christening the other house cooked for it.

Each was the first family of its settlement, which in those islands meant a house with a board floor and a sloop. The men cut braziletto. It is a crooked, thorny tree, nothing to look at, and all its worth is inside it. The dyers in London want the heart, which is as red as a cut thumb. The white sapwood round the heart gives no color and nobody will pay freight on it. So a braziletto cutter spends his days chipping sap from heart with a hatchet, and by the time the two boys were ten years old they could do it by the sound.

They grew up in and out of each other's boats. Whatever there was to learn in the islands, piloting, turtling, raking salt, working a wreck, they had it sooner than other boys and had it better. When Tobias Sands put out from Harbour Island every lad who could get away put out behind him, and it was the same with Cornelius Albury at Spanish Wells.

II. The Fair

Nassau, New Providence · 1716

In the summer of 1715 the Spanish plate fleet went ashore in a hurricane on the coast of Florida, eleven ships with the King of Spain's silver in them, and every sloop in the Bahamas went over to fish the wrecks. The fishing turned to taking. Those who took did not go home. A year later there were a thousand of them at Nassau, where there had been a hundred settlers and no governor, and the harbor was the merriest place in the Indies. A town of old sails stood along the beach, stretched over spars. There were fiddlers and punch houses and women from Jamaica. There was a man who sang the lament of Captain Kidd, all the verses. There was a great deal of silver, and it went from hand to hand like sand.

The two friends sailed over to see it, as the young men of all the out islands did. Room was made for them under the best awning, and for some days they saw the show as it was meant to be seen. When the company laughed, they laughed. When the singer came to the verse at Execution Dock, they wept with the rest. When the hat came round, they threw silver into it.

Then there came an evening when the fiddles struck up and there was nothing in it for either of them. Tobias sat and looked at the dancers. He thought: There is not a man on this beach who will be above ground in twenty years, and most of them not in five. What is there here to look at? I ought to be looking for the thing that keeps.

Cornelius said, You are not merry, as you were the other nights. What is in your mind?

I was thinking that none of this keeps. I mean to go and find what does. And you? You are as glum as I am.

I have been sitting here thinking the same thing.

Then we have had one good thought between us, said Tobias. We will look together. Who shall we go to?

The great man of the harbor that season was Captain Solomon Jayne, who had a ship of twenty guns and a hundred and forty hands, and the name of a deep man. We will go to Jayne, they said. They sent half the lads who had followed them home with the sloops, and went aboard with the other half. From the day those two signed his articles Jayne's fortunes ran high, for every young man in the islands wanted to be in the ship the Eleuthera men were in.

Jayne was no fool. He would not be pinned to anything. If a man asked him whether there was a reckoning after the rope, he would say, I do not say there is. I do not say there is not. I do not say it is otherwise. What he did hold he put in few words, and it was the creed of the whole harbor. Another captain of the trade was afterward set down in print as giving it so:

In an honest Service, says he, there is thin Commons, low Wages, and hard Labour; in this, Plenty and Satiety, Pleasure and Ease, Liberty and Power; and who would not ballance Creditor on this Side, when all the Hazard that is run for it, at worst, is only a sour Look or two at choaking. No, A merry Life and a short one, shall be my Motto.

— Captain Charles Johnson, A General History of the Pyrates (1724)

In a week the two friends had all of it, the ship and the guns and the taking and the sharing out. They went aft.

Captain, is this the whole of what you know, or is there something more?

That is the whole of it, said Jayne. You have it all.

III. Every Port

1716 and 1717

They talked it over on the forecastle. If that was all, there was nothing to stay for. But the sea was wide, and there were a good many men on it who had the name of knowing something. They would go round and ask.

They went in Jayne's ship while she served and in other bottoms when she did not. At Port Royal they sat with old men who had crossed the Isthmus behind Morgan. On the Mosquito shore they sat with a striker who could feed a hundred hands with one harpoon. They talked with a Spanish friar taken out of a prize, and with a ship's surgeon who had been to a university, and in Boston with a minister who came down to the jail to pray with men of their own trade before they were hanged. Whatever these people asked, the two friends could answer. What the two friends asked, nobody could. It was always the one question. What keeps?

After a year and a half they had been everywhere a keel would take them, and they came back to Nassau and to Jayne's ship, having found nothing. Before they turned in that night Tobias said, Whichever of us comes on it first tells the other. Cornelius gave his hand on it.

IV. Old Rope

Nassau · February 1718

While they were gone a shipwright had come down from Bermuda in a cedar sloop of his own building. His name was Jeremiah Darrell. He was an old man and a free man of color, forty years in the Bermuda yards, and it was said of him that he had once walked away from a good house and a wife and a young son, and had worked six years as no man ought to work, before he understood his trade. Now he kept a careening yard on the back of Hog Island, across the harbor from the town. He would heave down and clean a vessel for anybody and take his pay in whatever they had.

Men who went over to Hog Island to see a bottom cleaned did not always come back to their ships. Five came to him first, an old hand named Cundall before the others. Then a merchant's son from Charles Town with his friends, then thirty young wreckers in a body, then three long-haired brothers named Cash who had kept a turtlers' fire on a cay in the Exumas and brought their whole camp with them. Mr. Pinder, one of the old settlers, who had a storehouse on the bay, gave the ground the yard stood on. By that winter above a hundred men worked there.

At the end of February His Majesty's ship Phoenix came in with the King's proclamation. Any pirate who gave himself up would be pardoned for everything done before. The harbor split down the middle over it, and for some days nobody talked of anything else.

On one of those mornings Tobias Sands, walking up from the water, saw a man going along the row of tents with a sack. He was begging old rope, the worn-out stuff that caulkers pick apart for oakum. He stopped at each tent and stood, and took what was handed out, and went on to the next. He kept his eyes on the ground a little way ahead of him. He did not hurry, and he did not look in.

Tobias had looked into a great many faces by then. He had never seen one with so little trouble in it. He thought, That man has it, or he is on the road to it. But a man at his work is not to be stopped with questions. So Tobias walked behind him at a few paces, the way a man does who wants a favor. When the caulker had his sack full and looked about for a place to sit and eat, Tobias fetched his own keg from the boat and set it down for him in the shade, and when he had eaten gave him water from his own jar.

Friend, he said then, your face is easy and your eye is clear. Whose man are you? What does he hold by?

I am the old man's on Hog Island. But I am new in the yard. I have not been long at it, and I could not lay it out for you at any length.

Say little or say much, said Tobias, but give me the heart of it. The heart is all I want. What would I do with a great many words?

Then it is this. Whatever rots, he will show you where the water got in.

Tobias stood still. He had carried one question to every port between Newfoundland and Campeche, and here was the answer, in a dozen words, from a man with a sack of junk. He knew it for the answer all the way through before the man had finished.

And he says: keep the water out, and there the rot stops.

That is enough, said Tobias. Do not tell me more. Go on ahead of me. I have a friend, and I gave him my word. He stood with his hat in his hand until the man with the sack had gone on up the beach.

Cornelius saw him coming a long way off. That is not the face you went out with this morning, he said. You have come on it.

I have come on it, said Tobias, and gave him the words as he had got them, and before he had done Cornelius had it too.

Where is he?

Hog Island.

Then let us go over.

But it was a way Tobias had all his life, that he would never slight anyone who had taught him anything. We will tell Jayne first, he said. He has been our captain. He may see it. If he does not see it he may take our word, and come and hear for himself.

V. The Water Butt

Aboard Jayne's ship · the same day

Jayne was on his quarterdeck. Well, he said. In all your ports, did you find the man who could tell you what keeps?

We have found him, Captain. He is across the harbor. And the King's pardon lies open on the beach for any man to walk in under. You are sailing empty. There is no heart in the timber. Come over with us.

You go. I cannot.

Why not?

I have been captain to a hundred and forty men. For me to go and be a hand in another man's yard would be the water butt going to the well for a dipper. I could not do it.

Do not take it that way, Captain.

Never mind. You go. I stay.

Captain, with the pardon come, every man in this harbor will be crossing to him. What will you do?

Tell me one thing. Which are there more of in this world, fools or wise men?

Fools, Captain. Wise men are few.

Then let the wise men go over to your wise carpenter, and let the fools come to me. I shall never want for a crew. Go on, the pair of you.

You will be a famous man, Captain, they said, and went down the side.

As they pulled for the beach the ship emptied behind them. Men dropped into the boats and men swam. Jayne stood and looked at his bare deck, and then he leaned over the rail and brought up blood, hot, out of his chest. A hundred and forty followed the two friends along the sand. At the table where the King's officer was taking names, seventy of them thought better of it, and turned round, and went back aboard. The other seventy gave their names and walked on behind the two to the ferrying place.

VI. Right Hand and Left

Darrell's yard, Hog Island

Darrell was sitting on a baulk of timber in the middle of the yard, with a sloop hove down on the sand in front of him, talking to his hands and to some of the townspeople who had rowed over to listen. He saw the boats while they were still small.

Here come two friends, he said. Sands and Albury. Those two will be my right hand and my left.

They came up the beach with their hats off. Master, we would work under you, if you will have us.

Come on, then, said Darrell. The trade is plainly taught here. Work at it until there is an end of grief.

The yard always held that by dark those two were going over the hull with adze and maul like men forty years out of their time, and that nobody could say where they had got the tools.

That same evening, when the hands sat down together, Darrell put Tobias on his right and Cornelius on his left, and said that these were his two mates, and what they said about the work he said.

The seventy who had come over with them had the trade through and through within days. The two friends were slower. Cornelius was a week at it, and on the seventh day was nodding over the pitch kettle when the old man shook him awake and sat down beside him and took a ship to pieces for him in words, what in her was earth and what was water and what was fire and what was air, and by nightfall Cornelius had come to the end of it. Tobias took a fortnight. His sister's son, a lank young man who held with nothing, had come over from the town to dispute. I hold with nothing, old man, he said. Do you hold with that? said Darrell, and went on from there to speak of pleasure and pain and what is neither, and how a man who watches them come and go leaves off clutching. Tobias was standing behind with a palmetto fan, keeping the flies off the old man's neck. It was not said to him. He got his dinner, they said afterward, out of a pot put on for somebody else. As to why it took the quicker man longer, the yard had an answer for that too. A king's ship is longer getting to sea than a dory. There is more to stow.

But the seating was not liked. The hands talked of it by the fires.

It is favor. If he wanted mates, there was Cundall and the first five. If not them, the Charles Town men. If not them, the thirty, or the Cashes, who brought him a whole camp. He has passed over every one and taken the last two up the beach.

Darrell came by and asked what they were talking about, and they told him.

I have no favorites, he said. Every man of you has had from me the thing he asked for, and so have they. Listen.

He told them about Cundall, things Cundall had half forgotten. As a young man he had planted corn with his elder brother on the Eleuthera main, in a year when a preacher and his people were going hungry on that shore. He bit a kernel in the milk one morning and it was sweet, and he wanted to give the hungry people green corn. His brother said nobody had ever given away corn green, it would ruin the crop, and if he must be a fool he could be one on his own half. So they ran a line down the field. From his half Cundall gave the first of the ears in the milk, and the first of the ripe ears, and the first at the cutting, and the first in the shock; the first at the husking, and in the crib, and at the shelling, and from the mortar, and the first bread. That was nine times from one crop. Each time he looked again, his half stood as full as before. And each time they thanked him and asked what he would have for it, he said one thing: when what is worth knowing comes by, let me be the first to know it. He did not ask to be anybody's mate. He was the first man into this yard and the first who saw what I was about. That is Cundall, paid in full.

The Charles Town men, in the yellow fever year, had carried the poor dead out to burial when their own kin would not touch them. One was a woman near her time. Young Yates, laying her in the ground, saw what a body is, and showed his four friends, and they went back and told the rest. They had asked only to see it as it is. So one night in his father's fine house Yates saw it a second time, and got up and walked out, and kept walking until he came here. The thirty had been thieves, and had once overheard a piece of good advice that was meant for a hog on its way to the knife, and had kept it better than the hog's owner did. They had asked to be done with thieving. They were done.

As for the Cashes, that is an older business, said Darrell, and he told them the tale Eleuthera tells about the winter after the reef. The company were in the cave with nothing saved. Three brothers who had got a little ashore gave all of it to feed the preacher and the people for three months, and put the serving out of it under a storekeeper and his men. The storekeeper's children cried, and he fed them first out of the common pot. Then he fed his own people first. In the end he and his men were eating the best of it themselves, behind the rock, and the company had what was left. The three brothers asked to be free men one day and they are. The storekeeper's people have been hungry ever since. Ask Mr. Pinder.

Mr. Pinder was the storekeeper's grandson, and he told it himself. The night after he first gave a dinner to the yard, there was a noise round his house that he would not care to hear again, and when he looked out there were people standing at the wall, as thin as lath. He went over to Hog Island in the morning. They are your kin, Darrell told him. They stand outside the wall and at the gate post and where the roads cross. They come back to their own house, and there is plenty on the table, and nobody remembers them. They have asked every honest man who passed this way when they might eat, and each one told them, not in my time. Give the dinner again, and say it is for them. Pinder gave it and said so. The next night they stood at the wall again, filled out, and stark naked. You gave them no clothes, said Darrell. So Pinder gave every man in the yard a shirt and trousers in their name, and they were not seen at his wall any more.

Now, Sands and Albury, said Darrell. The same winter, the same cave. Somebody had to go for help, and there was one small boat, stove in. The carpenter mended her on the open beach, seven days in the sun. The first Sands on this island stood by him those seven days holding a palmetto thatch over his head, and did not go off to eat. He was watching the carpenter's mate, who had each tool in the carpenter's hand before it was asked for. When the boat was done the carpenter asked what he would have. He said, I do not want land and I do not want a share. Some day let me, or one of mine, be to such a man what your mate is to you. Then he went and told his friend, the first Albury, and Albury fed the boatbuilders seven days and asked for the second place. It has been seventy years coming in. I did not give it to them. They asked for it. I give each man what he asked.

VII. Heart and Sap

The same evening

When he had done, the two friends stood up, and told the yard the rest: the fair, and the evening the fiddles went dead, and Jayne, and every port, and the man with the sack. Then they told what Jayne had said on his quarterdeck, about the water butt and the dipper, and about the fools.

Darrell listened to the end. There was a billet of braziletto in the firewood by his foot, with the bark on, and he turned it over with his toe so that the cut end showed, the red in the middle and the white ring round it.

Jayne has taken the sap for the heart, he said, and he looks at heart and calls it sap. A man who cuts that way never comes to the heart at all. He goes where his wanting takes him, and it takes him wrong.

You two knew heart for heart and sap for sap. You were bred to it. So you chipped off what was nothing and kept what was something, and you have come to the heart. Your wanting was sound, and it brought you in.

A good many who sat by the fires that night, hands and townspeople both, got up from them changed.

•••

Jayne slipped out by the eastern passage with his seventy before the Governor came, and he was a famous man for the better part of two years.

In the morning there was a plank to come out by the sternpost of the sloop on the sand. It looked well enough under the paint. Tobias Sands went along it with the point of his knife until the knife went in to the handle, and found the place above it where the water had been getting in, and Cornelius Albury went up the beach for the cedar.

Background

The verses

They who imagine truth in untruth, and see untruth in truth, never arrive at truth, but follow vain desires.Dhammapada 11, tr. F. Max Müller (1881)
They who know truth in truth, and untruth in untruth, arrive at truth, and follow true desires.Dhammapada 12, tr. F. Max Müller (1881)

The word Müller renders "truth" is sāra, the pith or heartwood of a tree. In the commentary, Upatissa and Kolita, sons of the leading families of two neighboring villages and friends from birth, lose their taste for a hilltop festival and go looking for what does not die. They exhaust the teaching of the skeptic Sañjaya, wander all India questioning teachers, and agree that whoever finds the deathless first will tell the other. Upatissa meets the monk Assaji on his alms round and is won by a two-line summary: of things that arise from a cause, the Buddha has told the cause, and their ceasing. Sañjaya refuses to go with them (a great jar, he says, cannot become a dipper; let the wise go to Gotama and the many fools come to him), and half his following leaves. The Buddha names the two his chief disciples, Sāriputta and Moggallāna, the same day. When older monks complain of favoritism he tells what each of them once wished for: the giver of first fruits nine times, the friends who buried the poor, the three brothers whose stewards ate the monks' food and became hungry ghosts until a kinsman's gift fed and clothed them, and the two friends themselves, one of whom long ago held a canopy of flowers over a Buddha for seven days. Nassau, 1715 to 1718

The Eleutheran Adventurers, about seventy Puritan Independents from Bermuda, were wrecked on the Devil's Backbone reef off north Eleuthera around 1648 and sheltered in what is still called Preacher's Cave; their leader went for help to Virginia in a small boat. Their descendants on Harbour Island and at Spanish Wells lived by wrecking, turtling, salt and dyewood, above all braziletto, whose red heartwood was the only part the dyers bought. After a hurricane wrecked the Spanish treasure fleet on the Florida coast in July 1715, the salvagers and raiders it drew made ungoverned Nassau their base, the so-called Republic of Pirates: by one governor's count more than a thousand pirates beside about a hundred settlers. Vessels were hove down for cleaning on the harbor beaches and on Hog Island, now Paradise Island.

George I's proclamation of pardon reached Nassau in force with Captain Vincent Pearse of HMS Phoenix in February 1718. Pearse listed 209 men who said they would take it, fewer than half of those on the island, and the pirate company split between those who came in and those, like Charles Vane, who sailed out when Governor Woodes Rogers arrived that July. The creed in the second section is given to Bartholomew Roberts in Captain Charles Johnson's General History of the Pyrates (1724). Jayne, Darrell, his yard and everyone in it are invented, and the three tales of the winter in the cave are told here as island legend, not as record. Bermuda's cedar sloops, and the free and enslaved Black shipwrights who built many of them, were the best regarded in those waters.
