# Shared symbols and cross-references

Read this before writing. Add a line when you plant something later stories could pick up.
Keep echoes subtle: a detail a careful reader notices, never a wink or a crossover plot.

## Recurring symbols
- **The red heifer with the white star.** The commentary's man-killing cow (four victims across several stories). Told as legend: 8.1 Copper Teeth (New Orleans 1766), 5.7 The Blue Cup (Minnesota 1888, "one in Norway and one somewhere in the south"), 8.2 The King's Bowl (Michilimackinac 1745). Any story touching the cow's victims can use her.
- **The soiled white cloth.** 2.3 Little Wayland: the handkerchief rubbed grey, kept unwashed. Cloth that takes dirt from clean hands can recur as an image of impermanence.
- **The mustard seed / the door crepe.** 8.13 The Mustard Seed (Philadelphia 1918).
- **Chimneys with no houses.** 13.6 Stand Still (Missouri 1864): rebuilt houses around old chimneys = deeds covered by good deeds.
- **The mustard seed on the needle's point.** 26.24 No Guile (Alabama 1959): Brother Sutton sets mustard seeds on Hattie Greer's sewing needle and they will not stay; one is later found pressed in Wayland's 1959 roll book. Pairs with 8.13's mustard seed and with any story about a mind nothing sticks to.
- **Flowers caught on an invisible vehicle.** 1.11 The Izote (Maryland 2018): a stalk of izote blossoms thrown into the street hangs in the air on a heavenly bus no one else can see, then drives off down Riggs Road; a Folgers can collects money to send bodies home. Any story of a heavenly chariot, palace or rebirth (e.g. Dhammika-type deaths, devas' vehicles) can echo the hanging flowers.
- **New clothes and the pool.** 16.4 New Clothes (Rowland Heights 1996): a black-and-gold silk shirt washed clean of blood in cold salt water; the net of light on a pool's bottom; Lin Lǎoshī's bilingual Legge Analects. Usable for later stories about the Licchavis or Vesālī, or about delight turning to grief.
- **Water led down furrows.** 6.5 Four Redfish (Chicago 1952), 10.11 The Long Day (Gila River 1930): the irrigator image from the commentary.
- **The notch in the dam.** 15.1 Bean Bread (Cherokee Nation 1846): two kin families cut a mill dam down a foot so the water goes over to the downstream side; a creek that "talks" all night when it runs. Also the little brown Cherokee Gospel of Matthew (1829), co-translated by a man later killed, buried with the old preacher. Any later water-sharing or kin-feud story can let a notch or a talking creek recur.
- **The yellow silk quill / the snake and the stick.** 13.7 The Quills (Paterson 1921-24): a father keeps the last quill his daughter wound and carries it to the Great Falls; the teacher's image of a man who puts a snake off the path with a stick, said of anyone who meets death prepared. Later death stories can let a stick, a snake in the path, or a half-wound spool recur. (The Quills also lets a mustard plaster from 1918 pass by, a nod to 8.13.)
- **Gold leaf on the inside of the glass / the lotus pile on the bank.** 20.9 Gold Leaf (Washington 1972): letters gilded backward on the inside of a window so they read true from the street; gold curled black by the 1968 fires; lotuses snapped off at Kenilworth and wilting on the dike. Miss Odessa keeps her door open "until the last one comes through" (a quiet bodhisattva vow) and is seen at the ponds while the cook swears she never left the kitchen.

- **Māgandiyā's grudge.** 14.1 One Footprint (Frisco 2004) sets up the daughter's lasting grudge that drives 2.1 Udena (Castile Roses, New Mexico 1784). Different settings; let the grudge rhyme.
- **Kisā Gotamī** recurs in commentary 20.11 and 26.12; **Paṭācārā** in 20.12; **Aṅgulimāla** in 26.39; **Uggasena** in 26.35–36. Whoever writes those should read the earlier story (8.13, 8.12, 13.6, 24.6) and consider echoing its people, objects or places.

- **Mr. Thorne's two oxen.** 14.2 Three Ladders (Harlem 1927): the bull birth stories (Ja 28–29) told as a Barbadian porch tale about an old woman's black ox at a gully in St. Philip and a parson's ox in Christ Church parish. Reusable wherever the Nandivisāla or Kāḷaka bull comes up.
- **Harlem 1927 disciples.** Clement Mayers, stevedore (Moggallāna); Gus Lashley, doorman on Edgecombe Avenue (Sāriputta); blind Mr. Andrews (Anuruddha); young Mr. Inniss the caterer (Culla Anāthapiṇḍika); Pindar Bascombe, Pullman porter (Piṇḍola). A later Harlem or Sāriputta/Moggallāna story could find either.
- **The five places and the hollow clothesline pole.** 5.3 Five Places (Lowell 2004): a miser's gold hidden in five spots, one of them a capped iron clothesline pole; Pchum Ben rice balls thrown in the dark for the hungry dead. Fits any story of hoarding, rebirth or hungry ghosts (petas).
- **Uncle Jabez's Revised Version with pressed syringa.** 21.8: white mock-orange petals pressed at Matthew 5 and Psalm 11; young preacher Will Angove "stops" in Denver 41 years (to about 1933). Usable by any later Colorado or Cornish story (9.12 Butte 1919 has Cornish miners).
- **Threading a needle without looking.** 2.4 Folding Chairs (Fort Lauderdale 1985): Bella threads needles for women whose eyes have gone; the Maestro calls attention "the whole capital we had." An image for appamāda (heedfulness) that other Chapter 2 stories (2.5, 2.9) or any garment-trade story could pick up.
- **The brass tray with letters only its owner can read.** 21.5 The Brass Tray (North Dakota 1907): a Damascus tray, its center cut with words everyone else sees as scratches; later hung in a Toledo rectory kitchen. Any story about hidden writing, or about the Syrian/Lebanese Orthodox in the Midwest, can let it turn up.
- **The peddler's case (kashshi).** 21.5: Imm Jirjis's case, opened one drawer at a time, passes to Latifeh Malouf. A Syrian peddler woman crossing a later prairie story could carry it.
- **The fox who divides the fish.** 12.2 The Middle Portion (Columbia River 1914): the jackal-and-otters fable told as a fox, two otters and a red salmon below Celilo Falls; "the middle portion belongs to the court." The Pendleton robe walks off on a preacher's shoulders. Any later story with a greedy arbiter can half-remember the fox.
- **The goldsmith's touchstone.** 17.7 The Touchstone (Warren, Michigan 2023): a Baghdad goldsmith's black stone kept under the register; gold drawn across it leaves a streak, tested every day "even the pieces he made himself." Fits any story about testing, praise and blame, or the "gold coin" of Dhp 230.

## Mahayana callbacks (welcome)
- The bodhisattva vow: someone who could leave the burning house and goes back in for the others.
- Guanyin / Kannon / Quan Âm on a kitchen shelf, Jizo statues for lost children, a helper nobody can find afterward.
- Uncle No (1.2) and Heart Mountain (25.11) already carry Pure Land threads; Five Places (5.3) carries Cambodian Theravada practice.
- **Water led down furrows (again).** 23.2 Green Broke (Nevada 2014): an old colt-breaker learns to wait at the crank of a wheel line until his breath comes down; "that was where the water wanted to go." Taming oneself as irrigation, after Four Redfish and The Long Day.
- **The gray mustang Humo (Fish Creek, freeze-marked).** 23.2: "quit" under a tied-up foot and adopted by a family from Minden, Nevada, the girl told to go slow. A later Nevada or horse story could meet him old, or meet the girl grown.

## The Avatamsaka (Flower Garland) as a quarry
- **Indra's net:** a jewel at every knot, each reflecting all the others. It's the series' own image for cross-references. A spiderweb with dew, a chandelier, a pawnshop window full of mirrors, a rhinestone jacket, Christmas lights reflected in a puddle.
- **Sudhana's pilgrimage:** the boy who visits 53 teachers, among them a courtesan (Vasumitrā), a perfumer, sailors, a boy and girl, a night goddess, a cruel king and a householder. It's precedent for "wisdom is everywhere." A character on a long search who keeps getting sent to the next unlikely teacher fits here.
- **Names to mine, translated or Americanized:** Sudhana ("Good Wealth," a kid called Goodwin or Sonny Rich), Mañjuśrī ("Gentle Glory"), Samantabhadra ("Universal Good"), Maitreya ("the friendly one," waiting in a tower), Vasumitrā, Sāgaramegha ("Ocean Cloud," a sailor who watches the sea). Use them as quiet names, nicknames, boat names or street names, never with a footnote.
- **Maitreya's tower:** a building that holds whole worlds inside it. A library, a dollar store, an old Woolworth's, a server farm.
- **Pasenadi's table.** 4.3 Thin Shade (Texas 1915) and 15.6 The Last Course (New York 1884) both make Pasenadi a man who eats without tasting. In The Last Course he takes the doctor's grand-niece into his house and "did not ask who her mother had been," a quiet nod to Vasabhakhattiyā's hidden birth in Thin Shade. The worn prescription blank left on a dispensary stove "for the next one" can recur wherever someone is handed words to read aloud at a meal.

## Planted by fable-1 (1.3, 1.7, 1.13)
- **The sun a day late.** 1.3 No Winters (Koyukuk, Alaska 1923): the Devala–Nārada curse told as a tale of the frozen-in stampede winter of 1899, when the sun came back over the hill a day late and a lump of blue clay split in seven on a man's head in a warm-spring pool. The roadhouse's birch chair goes to whoever has the most winters; mitts are hung on the drying pole palms to the heat. Hans Lien of Goodhue County carries his mother's chipped blue cup (from 5.7). Any later sun-stopping, curse or seniority story can half-remember the late sun or the chair.

## Bodhisattva roster (use as quiet echoes, names translated or Americanized)
- **Avalokiteśvara / Guanyin / Kannon / Quan Âm:** "Regarder of the World's Sounds," the one who hears every cry. A night-shift dispatcher, a crisis-line volunteer, a ham-radio operator, a 911 operator.
- **Kṣitigarbha / Jizo / Dizang:** "Earth Store," who vowed not to rest until the hells are empty. Protector of travelers and of children who died young. Little stone figures by roadsides; a public defender; someone who works death row or a children's hospice.
- **Sadāparibhūta / Fukyō:** "Never Disparaging," who bowed to everyone he met, saying "you will become a Buddha," and was stoned and beaten for it. A greeter who blesses everyone at the Walmart door; a street preacher who only praises.
- **Bhaiṣajyarāja / Yakuō:** "Medicine King," who in the Lotus Sutra burned his own body as an offering of light. Nurses, healers, and a doctor who gives too much.
- **Bhaiṣajyasamudgata / Yakujō:** "Medicine Superior," the Medicine King's brother. Two siblings in medicine.
- **Mañjuśrī / Monju / Wenshu:** wisdom, the sword that cuts confusion, often riding a lion. A sharp-tongued librarian, a debate coach.
- **Samantabhadra / Fugen / Puxian:** "Universal Worthy," practice and vows, riding a white elephant with six tusks. A circus elephant (see 23.6 The Old Bull), or someone whose whole teaching is just doing the work.
- **Maitreya / Miroku / Mile:** the future Buddha, waiting. Also the laughing fat Budai on restaurant counters.
- **Mahāsthāmaprāpta / Seishi / Dashizhi:** "Great Power Arrived," the light of wisdom, paired with Guanyin beside Amitābha (Pure Land threads: 1.2, 25.11).
- **Vajrapāṇi / Kongōshu:** "thunderbolt in hand," the fierce protector. A bouncer, a bodyguard, a big man at the church door.
- **Ākāśagarbha / Kokūzō:** "Boundless Space Store," memory and open sky. An astronomer, a pilot, someone who remembers everything.
- **Gadgadasvara / Myōon:** "Wonderful Sound," who teaches in whatever form the listener needs. A musician, a voice on the radio, a ventriloquist.
- **Nityodyukta / Jōshōjin:** "Ever Vigorous." Someone who never stops working.
- **The four bodhisattvas who rise from the earth** (Lotus Sutra ch. 15): **Viśiṣṭacāritra / Jōgyō** ("Superior Practices"), **Anantacāritra / Muhengyō** ("Boundless Practices"), **Viśuddhacāritra / Jōgyō 浄行** ("Pure Practices"), and **Supratiṣṭhitacāritra / Anryūgyō** ("Firmly Established Practices"). Countless bodhisattvas well up out of the ground. Miners coming up a shaft, subway riders coming up stairs at dawn, farmworkers rising from the rows, a flood of volunteers out of nowhere after a storm.
- **The blind teacher who hears wheels.** 4.3 Thin Shade's blind Tomás Leal tells every wagon by its wheels; 26.40 Black Honey (Texas 2021) has a blind Coptic cantor who knows the cars in the church lot by their engines and every voice he ever taught. A blind teacher who remembers voices can be the "knower of former lives."
- **Hot water carried on a pole.** 26.40 Black Honey: pots slung from a broom handle across a boy's shoulders, "like the saqqa." Also there: a stranger in an orange safety vest who carries the pot up the stair and leaves no tracks (a bodhisattva helper), and a Vietnamese neighbor's Quan Âm over the television.
- **The Guanyin behind the register.** 26.38 Thirteen Pleats (Seattle 2019): a white porcelain Guanyin with oranges changed every Monday, left on the shelf of the Lucky Pearl on King Street "because it would know who came in." A Mahayana callback any later Seattle or Chinatown story can pass by.
- **The old woman at the tea stall (Deshan's dim sum).** 26.38: the Chan story of the woman who stumped the Diamond Sutra scholar with "which mind will you refresh?" A wise food-seller who out-answers a learned man can recur anywhere.
- **Thirteen pleats.** 26.38: Leung Wai-man's har gow; the restaurant changed hands on a promise to keep them. A sign of craft kept after the craftsman has let go.
- **The cloak whose dye runs.** 1.7 Four Votes (St. Louis 2017): a Foča judge's quince-yellow, fox-lined ćurak carried out of the war in a shopping bag, voted to the wrong man by four votes, cut down and dyed the color of tea; the dye comes off on his collar. Rasim the tailor on Delor Street keeps the cut-away pieces in a drawer. The elephant-hunter birth story is told as a hunter in a stolen dervish cloak and a stag that spares him. Bula Hatidža of Utica knits gray socks with a red band. Later Devadatta or Sāriputta stories can find the cloak pieces, the socks, or the stag at the spring.
- **The scythe in the oak.** 25.10 The Scythe (Mount Angel, Oregon 1963): a Hungarian refugee's scythe hung blade-in in a white oak at the edge of a Benedictine abbey; by 2009 the tree has grown over all but the tip, which looks like a new moon. It is the twin of the sugar-sack shirt in the cottonwood (10.10); a later story set at a monastery, or one about leaving and staying, could glimpse it.
- **Birds that leave no track / chimneys that hold the living.** 7.3 Sukhari (Portland 2021): Vaux's swifts pouring into the Chapman School chimney, a chimney with no fire (quiet rhyme with Stand Still's chimneys). A labeled bucket left washed and upside down on a shelf = a person gone with nothing stored. Usable by any story about leaving without trace.
- **The crows bailing the sea.** 20.8 Sweet Root (Iberia Parish 1988) tells Jātaka 146 straight, as an abbot's tale; vetiver/usīra root ("dig, the sweetness is in the root") and Louisiana cane fires with ash falling. Mae Wan (Sweet Cook) and her jars of jeow could be half-remembered in any later Lao or Gulf Coast story.
- **Hombre sincero / señor.** 26.13 Hombre Sincero (Miami 1980): the lector Cándido Valdés, a foundling (Valdés = nobody's son), reads aloud in Domino Park; dominoes face down "in the soup," all alike from the back. Any of the 26.x "Him I call a Brahmin" stories could echo the title-not-by-birth, a Valdés, or the reading voice.

## Planted by fable-5 (22.6, 24.7, 24.12)
- **The ear-mark and the mark book.** 22.6 The Ear-Mark (Marbletown, New York 1826): a farmer's registered livestock mark ("a crop off the left ear and a slit in the right") cut into a bound servant; the old voorlezer strikes the entry from the town book, and a Krom sheep is known afterward by its whole ears. - **Meat and fish.** 24.7 The Near Bank (Malin, Oregon 2022): the jackal who drops his meat for a jumping fish and loses both, told by a Tongan-American mission president who had it from his father, who had it from a ship's cook from Calcutta. - **One spoonful / the bean vine at the Scales.** 24.12 Thirteen Kettles (Chilkoot Trail 1898): expands the two-line Aṅkura–Indaka aside in 14.2 Three Ladders (the Harlem dead man who shared his soup with blind Mr. Andrews).
