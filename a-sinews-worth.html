A Sinew's Worth

That from the very woof thread unto the shoe latchet, I will not take of any things that are thine.
— Genesis 14:23

C'est de même que je l'ai entendu, on the Sheyenne.

I. Le Ramasseux

Pembina to the Sheyenne River · September 1847

Isidore was fifteen that fall and belonged to no cart. His mother had died the summer before, and his father he knew only as a name other people remembered. When the hunt went out from Pembina he went with it on foot, behind the last of two hundred carts, in the noise of their dry axles, which a person could hear for half a day's ride across the plain.

A camp on the move sheds like a dog. Something fell off every cart every mile: a linchpin, a child's moccasin, an awl, a tin cup, a twist of tobacco, a strap. Isidore walked at the back and picked it all up. When the guide's flag came down in the evening and the carts drew into their circle with the shafts turned out, he went around the inside of it holding each thing over his head and calling À qui? À qui ça? until somebody put out a hand. What nobody claimed by dark he used, or gave to whoever was nearest. His capote had been three men's before it was his. So they called him le Ramasseux, the one who picks up.

He ate at any fire that had room. He cut meat for any woman who handed him a knife. He was not sad, which bothered some people, and he never seemed to be holding anything, which they did not notice until later.

No priest rode with them that fall. Before they left Pembina the captains were chosen, and the soldiers under them, and the crier walked through the camp calling the law of the hunt, as he did before every hunt Isidore could remember. No buffalo run on Sunday. No party to go ahead or fall behind. No man to run before the word. For breaking those, the saddle cut up, then the coat cut off your back, then the whip. And at the end, the one the boys always waited for. For theft, even to the value of a sinew, the thief to be brought to the middle of the camp, and his name cried three times, with the word after it.

II. The Sash

Camp on the Sheyenne · the day after the first run

They ran buffalo nine days out, and the next morning the whole camp was meat. The women had it cut thin as cloth and hung on the racks, with slow fires under, and the smoke and the smell of fat lay over the carts and got into everything.

Narcisse Laframboise was twenty, from White Horse Plain, and owned the best sash on the hunt. It was an Assomption sash, wool, fine as a woman's hair ribbon, red with the arrow pattern down it in blue and white and yellow, and he had been two winters paying for it. He would not have it in that smoke, and he would not have it on him while he worked and take the smell of his own sweat. He carried it fifty steps outside the circle to the creek and hung it on a buffaloberry bush in the clean wind. Then he went and sat behind his cart, facing his tent, to scrape a hide.

Isidore came along the creek with two kettles. He saw a sash on a bush. He looked up the bank and down it and across the water and saw no one.

Another boy would have thought, that is too good to be lost. It did not come into Isidore's head. He had picked up a silver thimble and a broken trace in the same morning, once, and held them up exactly alike. The thing was there and its person was not. He set down the kettles, took the sash off the bush, and started for the carts with it over his head, drawing breath to call.

Voleur!

Narcisse came around the cart with the scraper still in his fist. That is my sash. You know the law, Ramasseux. Even a sinew.

It's yours?

Everybody in this camp knows it's mine.

I didn't see anybody. I thought it was left. Here.

He put it in Narcisse's hands and went back for his kettles. Narcisse stood there with a good deal more to say, and the sash, and nobody in front of him.

III. Long or Short

The same evening, at the fires

Isidore told it himself, at the first fire he sat down to, the way he would have told that he had found a cup. The boys his own age had it off him in a minute.

So, Ramasseux. This sash you took. Was it a long one or a short one?

Was it fine or coarse?

Did it have arrows on it? What color? Come on, you had it in your two hands.

I don't know, Isidore said. Red. Never mind if it was long or short. I thought it was left.

They howled. There was not a child on the hunt who could not have told you that sash to the thread: the length, twice around Narcisse and down to his knee, the fringe, the blue next to the white. But the older boys did not laugh, and two of the soldiers, who had been listening, did not either.

He's lying, one of them said. Nobody alive takes that sash off a bush and doesn't look at it. He looked. He wanted it, he took it, and he gave it back because he was caught.

That was another matter. That was the law, and the middle of the camp, and a name called three times. They got up and went to the captains' fire, and took the boy with them, and Narcisse came, and half the camp behind.

The captains listened and then sent for old Josephte. She said the prayers, evenings and Sundays, when there was no priest. She had baptized on the plains and buried there, and she was who they asked when a thing was not in the law. She came with her shawl over her head and had the boy stand where the light was on him.

What did you pick up today?

A linchpin. That was Gariépy's. An awl. A baby's moccasin, that was at the crossing. A sash.

What was the sash like?

Red.

Long?

I don't know, Grand-mère. I didn't have it long. Then it was his.

She looked at him a while, and then around the fire at the men.

I have watched every one of you look at that sash, she said. You cannot think how a person could have it in his hand and not measure it. That is how it is with you, and with Narcisse. It is not how it is with him. I have walked in front of this boy since Pembina. Half of what this camp owns has been through his hands, and he has given it back down to the awls. He is telling you the truth. He did not take it, because there is nothing in him that takes.

Then she said what Abraham swore to the king of Sodom, when the king offered him the goods of the city. She had it by heart out of the old priest's book, the way she had everything.

Je lève la main et je jure par le Seigneur, le Dieu très-haut, possesseur du ciel et de la terre, que je ne recevrai rien de tout ce qui est à vous, depuis le moindre fil jusqu'à un cordon de soulier.

— Genèse 14:22–23, Bible de Sacy

That from the very woof thread unto the shoe latchet, I will not take of any things that are thine.

— Genesis 14:23, Douay-Rheims

From a thread to a moccasin string, she said. He could swear that to this whole camp, and he is the only one of us who could.

Nobody's name was cried. The captains said it was finished. Narcisse stood a moment with his hand flat on the knot at his hip, and then he said Bon, to no one, and went to his tent. A good many people sat late at the fires that night without much talk, each with whatever he had been two winters paying for.

•••

In the morning the flag went up and the carts pulled out of the circle, one behind another, and began to scream. Isidore waited for the last of them. By a dead fire he found a length of sinew thread somebody had dropped from her sewing, as long as his arm and no thicker than a grass stem.

He held it up over his head and walked forward along the line. À qui? À qui ça?

They laughed all the way up the carts. It was old Josephte who put her hand out.

Background

The verse

Him I call indeed a Brahmana who takes nothing in the world that is not given him, be it long or short, small or large, good or bad.Dhammapada 409, tr. F. Max Müller (1881)

In the commentary, a Brahmin of Sāvatthī takes off his outer cloth so that it will not catch the smell of his body, lays it aside, and sits down facing his house. A monk who is an arahant passes, sees the cloth and no owner, and takes it for a cast-off rag of the kind monks made their robes from. The Brahmin runs up shouting that he is a thief; the monk asks if it is his and hands it back. When he tells the story at the monastery the other monks tease him: was it long or short, coarse or fine? He answers that he has no attachment to it either way, and they report him to the Buddha as a liar. The Buddha says he speaks the truth: those who are free of the pollutants do not take what belongs to others. Read the full commentary story.

The Pembina hunt, 1847

Twice a year through the middle of the nineteenth century the Métis of the Red River country, French- and Michif-speaking Catholic families of mixed Indigenous and European descent, gathered at Pembina, just south of the forty-ninth parallel, and went out onto the plains of what is now North Dakota after buffalo. The great hunt of 1840, described by Alexander Ross, counted 1,210 carts and 1,630 people and reached the Sheyenne River nine days out. The hunters elected captains, soldiers and guides; a flag raised in the morning meant march and lowered in the evening meant camp; the Red River carts, built without iron and never greased, were drawn up at night in a circle with the shafts outward. Women cut and dried the meat and made the pemmican.

The laws of the hunt in the story are those recorded for the hunt of 1840, ending with the one on theft: “even to the value of a sinew,” the offender was brought to the middle of the camp and the crier called out his or her name three times, adding the word “Thief.” Priests rode with some hunts and not others. The arrow sash, the ceinture fléchée, was finger-woven at L'Assomption in Lower Canada, sold through the fur companies, and worn over the blue capote. The autumn hunt of 1847 in the story, and everyone on it, are invented. Lemaître de Sacy's French Bible, made in the seventeenth century, was the most widely read French Catholic translation of the period.
