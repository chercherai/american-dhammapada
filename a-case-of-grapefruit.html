A Case of Grapefruit

And the children of Israel wept for Moses in the plains of Moab thirty days: so the days of weeping and mourning for Moses were ended.
— Deuteronomy 34:8

Here's how I heard it, on Bank Street.

I. Fourth of July

Bank Street, New London, Connecticut · March 2024

Royal Tate had tattooed on Bank Street since 1973, in a long narrow room between a bar and a shop that sold used anchors, and in all that time he had never put up a sign with his name on it. The sign said TATTOO. The window said TATTOO. People knew.

He had come to it sideways. He was a cook on submarines out of Groton in the sixties, a Black kid from Portsmouth, Virginia, who had gone into the Navy because his father had, and he had worn the silver dolphins and fed a hundred and twenty men in a galley the size of a closet. On leave in New York in 1965 he had gone looking for a tattoo and found that the city had outlawed them four years before. A man on a stoop sent him to a man in a back room on the Lower East Side, who did the work with the shades drawn, on a kitchen chair, and let Royal watch. Royal went back every leave for three years and swept the floor and watched. When he got out he knew how to build a coil machine from scratch and how to pull a line, and he knew that he would never do anything else.

The walls of the shop were flash from floor to ceiling, sheets of it in dime-store frames gone yellow: swallows, anchors, daggers through roses, panthers, hula girls, the Rock of Ages with a woman clinging to the cross in a green sea, submarine dolphins in every size. Fifty years of men off the boats at the base had walked out of that room with those pictures on them, and their sons after them, and the welders from Electric Boat across the river, and lately a great many young women from Hartford who wanted the swallows on their collarbones and had found him on Instagram, where Royal had never been in his life.

On a Monday in March he came in from the oncologist at Lawrence and Memorial, hung his coat on the hook, and told the shop.

Pancreas, he said. They give me about four months. Say the Fourth of July.

Then he sat down at his station and finished the anchor he had started on a machinist from Ledyard that morning, because the man had only half an anchor.

II. The Line

April and May

The shop filled up and stayed full.

The apprentices and the two other artists came in on their days off and sat around his station. Old customers came in from Norwich and Mystic and Westerly with their sleeves rolled up to show him what he had done in 1979. Somebody put a vase of white roses on the counter by the register, and the next day there were three vases, and by May the counter was covered and the room smelled like a funeral parlor over the green soap. Men he had tattooed on the boats stood in the doorway and could not say anything and went next door to the bar. A woman from Groton brought a framed picture of her husband, dead twenty years, showing the eagle Royal had put on his back. Kevin, the senior apprentice, made a list of everyone who wanted to get one of Royal's own flash pieces from his hand before the Fourth, and the list went to four pages. Somebody started a hashtag. People slept in their cars on Bank Street to be first in line on Saturdays.

What are we going to do, Kevin kept saying, at night, when they were cleaning the stations. What are we going to do after.

Dominique Ruiz did not come in at all.

She was twenty-six. She had grown up on Hempstead Street four blocks away, and she had come into the shop at twenty-three with a sketchbook under her arm, and every other shop from New Haven to Providence had already told her no. Royal had looked at the sketchbook for a long time without saying anything. Then he had handed her a broom. For two years she had swept, cleaned tubes, mixed green soap, drawn flash and redrawn it, and watched. Six months ago he had let her start practicing on skin, pig skin from a butcher in Waterford, and then on fruit, and then on her own left leg. Her color was good and her shading was good. Her lines were not.

Your line has got a shake at the end of it, he had told her in January, holding a grapefruit up to the magnifier lamp. Right here, at the tail, where you lift. You see it? That's you wanting it to be over. That's you thinking about the next one, and about who's going to see it. The line doesn't care who sees it.

After the Monday in March she came in once. She stood at the back of the room behind the people with roses and did not go up to his station. Then she went home to her apartment over the laundromat on Bank Street and did not come back.

She had bought a case of grapefruit at the Stop & Shop. She set them in a row on the kitchen table under a gooseneck lamp, and she sat down with her liner, and she pulled one line on each grapefruit, around the curve, from one side to the other, a single line with no shape to it. Then the next. When the case was gone she bought another. In the evenings she drove to Waterford for pig skin. She did not draw anything. She did not post anything. She pulled lines until the muscle in her right forearm went hard as a broom handle and then she pulled more, and under the lamp she looked at the tail of every one.

When people from the shop texted her she did not answer. When they knocked she let them knock.

She hasn't come by once, Kevin told Royal in the middle of May. He said it at the station in front of everyone. Not once, Royal. You took her in when nobody in New England would look at her, and you're sitting here dying and she can't walk four blocks. Marisol saw her at the Stop & Shop buying grapefruit. Grapefruit.

Royal wiped down the station and did not say anything.

The next day he took out his phone, which he used only to call his sister in Portsmouth, and asked Kevin how you sent a message.

III. Four Blocks

June

She came in on a Tuesday after closing, with her kit in one hand and a grocery bag in the other. The roses had been cleared off the counter that morning by the cleaning woman, who could no longer stand it. Kevin and Marisol and the two artists were still there, and they stopped talking when she came in.

Royal was sitting in his own chair, the old red barber's chair at the first station, because he did not like the bed. He was thin now. His hands were the same.

They tell me you quit on me, he said. Is it true?

Yes, said Dom.

Why?

She put the grocery bag on the counter and did not take anything out of it.

You said four months, she said. Everybody's in here getting your swallows. Everybody's crying. I can't do anything about the four months. And my line still shakes. You told me it shakes because I want it to be over, and because I'm thinking about who's going to see it. So I've been home pulling lines on grapefruit where nobody can see. I want to pull one straight before you go. While you can still look at it.

Royal looked at her for a long time, the way he had looked at her sketchbook.

Good, he said. Good.

Then he turned the chair a little so he was facing the rest of them, Kevin and Marisol and the two artists, and the wall of flash, and the cleared counter where the roses had been.

Y'all have been good to me, he said. I know it. But you're putting my roses on your arms and bringing roses in here, and that's flowers. You want to honor me, honor me like she's doing. Sit down and pull the line. He was quiet, and then he said, My mother read us the part where Moses dies. They cried for him thirty days on the plains of Moab, and then the Lord shut it off. Get up. Go over. And then He told the young one what to do.

He said it from memory, the way his mother had said it in the kitchen on Effingham Street, with the old King James words, and nobody in the shop moved.

This book of the law shall not depart out of thy mouth; but thou shalt meditate therein day and night, that thou mayest observe to do according to all that is written therein: for then thou shalt make thy way prosperous, and then thou shalt have good success.

— Joshua 1:8

Observe to do, said Royal. Not observe to cry. Observe to do.

Kevin looked at the floor. Marisol had her hand over her mouth. One of the artists got up and went to his station and opened a drawer, and took out a grapefruit he had been keeping there for a still life, and sat down with it.

IV. Dolphins

The first station · the same night

Royal rolled up his left sleeve.

On the inside of his forearm, among fifty years of pieces he had put on himself, were the dolphins he had put on himself in 1967 in a bunk on the boat, with a sewing needle and a bottle of India ink, the night he qualified. They had been the first thing he ever tattooed. They had gone soft and green and blurred the way homemade work does, and nobody had ever touched them, because he had never let anybody else put a needle in him.

Line them, he said. Just the line. Go over mine.

Dom set up her station on the counter where the roses had been. She bagged the machine and the cord. She poured the black into a cap and wiped his arm down with green soap. He watched her do every bit of it as if she were being graded, which she was. Then she put her left hand flat on his arm to stretch the skin, the way he had shown her two years before with his hand over hers, and touched the needle down at the bow of the submarine where the dolphins' noses met.

The machine ran. Nobody in the shop said anything. Kevin had come up behind the chair and stood with his arms folded, and then he unfolded them.

She did not think about who was watching. She did not think about the Fourth of July. She thought about the line. It went where it went, along the curve of the hull, up the dolphins' backs and out along the tails. At the tail she lifted, and it did not shake.

She wiped it. Royal pulled the magnifier lamp over and looked at it for a long time.

He did not say anything. He reached over and took the grapefruit out of her grocery bag on the counter, the last one in the case, with a single black line around it from pole to pole, and he turned it in the light and set it next to his arm.

The new line sat on his forearm, blue-black and clean, laid exactly over the soft green one he had put there with a sewing needle in a bunk under the North Atlantic when he was twenty-one. Around it, all the way up to his elbow, the old work had faded the way it does, into the color of deep water.

Background

The verse

He who dwells in the law, delights in the law, meditates on the law, follows the law, that Bhikshu will never fall away from the true law.Dhammapada 364, tr. F. Max Müller (1881)

In the commentary, the Buddha announces that in four months he will pass into final nirvana (parinibbāna). Thousands of monks crowd around him, weeping and asking each other what they will do. One monk, Dhammārāma, keeps away from them and will not say why. The others report that he has no love for the Teacher. Summoned, Dhammārāma explains that he has not yet freed himself from desire and means to reach the goal while the Buddha is still alive, so he has gone off alone to reflect on and remember the teaching. The Buddha praises him: those who honor him with garlands and perfumes do not truly honor him, and those who practice the teaching do. At the end of the verse Dhammārāma becomes an arahant. New London, 1961–2024

Across the Thames River from New London, Groton has been the Navy's submarine town since 1916, when the Naval Submarine Base New London became the first in the country. It calls itself the Home of the Submarine Force. Electric Boat builds submarines on the same shore. A sailor who completes the year-long qualification on a boat is designated "Qualified in Submarines" and wears the dolphins. Enlisted men have worn the silver pin since 1950. For a century the bars and tattoo shops along New London's waterfront served the men off the boats, and their shops were papered with flash, ready-made designs in black outline and a few colors, made for quick work on walk-in customers.

In 1961 New York City made it unlawful to tattoo a human being, citing a hepatitis outbreak that later critics said had nothing to do with tattooing. The ban held for thirty-six years. Tattooing went on behind drawn shades, and the city did not legalize it again until 1997 (the rise and fall of the ban). Apprenticeship is still the old way into the trade: years of sweeping, drawing and watching, then practice on fruit and on pig skin from the butcher, which comes closest to human skin for learning how deep the needle goes (how apprentices practice).
