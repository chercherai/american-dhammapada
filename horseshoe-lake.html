I. The Washers

Gravois Avenue, St. Louis · 2004 and after

Ramiz-efendija Kurtović came to St. Louis in 1998 from the Pešter plateau in Sandžak, where the winter is so long that the sheep come down off the summer grass in September. For six years he mopped floors at night in a hospital on Kingshighway and on Fridays led the prayer in a rented room over a car-parts store. In 2004 the džemat bought the old furniture store on Gravois and could pay him, a little. The first thing he asked for was young men to learn the washing of the dead.

He asked it from the minbar, in the plain way he said everything. At home the old men had done it. Here the old men were tired, or in the ground, or had never learned. Who is going to wash us, he said, when it is our turn?

Six came, all from the same few streets, all between twenty and twenty-five. The Mujkić brothers, Kenan and Mirza, whose father had the biggest house on Itaska Street and the first Mercedes on Gravois. Samir Karić, who was studying to be a pharmacist. Emir Selimović, just down from Utica, who drove a truck for Fadil Kovač. Nermin Hadžić, who had the best voice of any young man in the džemat and had been told so since he was nine. And Haris Mehmedović, who was from Goražde.

About Haris the street had a story. In the siege, when the food came down at night by parachute and there was never enough of it, his mother had never once said to him nema, there isn't any. Whatever there was she put in front of him and went without. One evening in the winter of 1994 there was nothing in the house at all, and he called up from the street that he was hungry, and she thought, well, now he will learn the word. She set an empty baking tray on the table and turned another tray over it for a lid and sent it down to him by his sister. He lifted the lid in the snow, and it was full of pita, hot, potato and cheese, enough for him and every boy on the street. He came up and asked her why she had never made that kind before. After that whenever he was hungry she sent down the covered tray, and it came full, until the war was over. Haris always said he did not remember any of it. His sister did.

The six went to the imam together, and they took Bego with them.

Bego Sejdić was a Rom from Tuzla. He had cut all their hair since they were boys in a chair at the back of the car-parts store, and swept up after the Friday prayer for six years without anybody asking him to. At the door of the washing room the six took off their watches and their gold chains and handed them to Bego to hold, the way you would to a boy, and told him to go on home, they would get them later. He went as far as the corner. Then he thought, if one chain goes missing they will say the Gypsy took it, and he thought, these six are going in there to learn to wash the dead, and I am going home to hold their gold? He hung everything on the hook inside the door of the hall where anyone could see it and came back.

It was Kenan Mujkić who said it, the elder brother. Efendija, we are proud. You know us. Bego has cut our hair for ten years. Teach him first. Then we will have to stand behind him.

So the imam taught Bego first, the water and the cloths and the order of it, right side before left, and the six stood behind a Gypsy barber and learned it over his shoulder, and something in each of them that had been standing up sat down.

They all learned it, in time. The Mujkić brothers went into their father's business and Kenan was president of the džemat by 2010. Samir got his license and filled the street's prescriptions for half price. Haris went to the optometry school at the university and fit half of Bevo with glasses, and was said to see things before other people did. Emir came when he was not on the road. Bego washed more of the dead than all the rest together. And Nermin learned the words fastest, the prayers for the washing and for the grave, and the forms at the county, and which window, and had his voice. That was what he learned. The imam never said what he thought of it.

II. The Lickspittle

Bevo Mill and Oakville · autumn 2017 to spring 2018

After the summer of the cloak, people on Gravois stood a little differently around Nermin Hadžić. Nothing was said. But when a family came into the hall now to ask for someone to wash their father, they asked for the imam, or for Bego, or for Emir if he was in town. They asked whether the hafiz was coming back for Ramadan. They asked after Haris, and after Kenan, and after the hafiz's young muezzin, whose name they had already forgotten. Nobody asked where Nermin was. He stood at the back of the hall in the tea-colored jacket with the fox collar and listened to them not ask.

He thought about it in the evenings. I went in with them, he thought. I learned it faster than any of them. What do they have that I don't? He thought about who he could go to. Not the Mujkić family; Kenan was president and had a long memory. Not Fadil Kovač, who had given the money for the building and the cloak both, and who had prayed every prayer in the hall since 2004 in the same place, behind the imam on the right. But Fadil had a son.

Dino Kovač was thirty-one. He had an MBA from Saint Louis University and an office at the company's yard in Oakville with a window onto forty trucks, and he knew what every man on Gravois was paid and nothing at all about what any of them was worth. His father would not retire. His father still drove a truck himself four times a year to remember how.

Nermin went to the yard one night in October without calling first. Dino was alone at his computer and turned around and found a man standing behind him in the dark office, close, in a hooded sweatshirt, and jumped so hard he knocked his coffee onto the keyboard.

Who is that?

It's Nermin. He pushed the hood back and laughed and helped him mop it up, and the next time he came he came in daylight, in his black robe, and was let in.

That winter he was at the yard more than he was at the hall. Dino gave him a title, chaplain to the drivers, and a salary, and a black Tahoe with the company name on the doors, and paid for him to go live on Facebook every Thursday night reciting, so that drivers in Texas and Ohio and Pennsylvania could listen in their cabs. Families who had stopped calling him began to call him again, because he was in Dino's office. He was invited to dinners. And on the night it first came to him whole, standing in the Kovač kitchen with a plate of baklava, that he should be the one leading the džemat and not an old janitor from Sandžak, his voice went. It did not go all at once. It cracked on the high note of the call to prayer the next morning, and after that it cracked whenever he reached for it, and a throat doctor in south county looked down his throat with a light and said nodules, rest it. He did not rest it.

Fadil Kovač had a small stroke in January, at the wheel of his own car in the yard, and was in the hospital a week.

People used to live long, Nermin said to Dino, in the office, with the door shut. Now they don't. You know how long his father lived? Ninety-one. He'll come back from this and go another fifteen years and you'll be fifty before you sign a check. Do it now, while you're young enough to enjoy it. You take the company. I'll take the džemat.

Dino had a lawyer by February and a power of attorney by March. He moved his father to a nursing home in south county, a clean one, with a fountain in the lobby, and told the front desk that only family could visit and that his mother was not to bring food from home, because of the old man's heart. Hajra Kovač brought it anyway, a little pita at a time in her handbag, under her scarf. Then the desk began looking in her handbag. Fadil stopped eating what the home gave him. He said it had no taste. He died at the end of April, and Ramiz-efendija, who had not been allowed in to see him once, washed him with Bego, and buried him, and said nothing to Dino at the grave but what is said to every son.

At the džemat's yearly assembly in March Nermin had stood up, before the budget, in the black robe, with Dino in the first row. Dino had pledged sixty thousand dollars for the new roof.

Efendija, Nermin said, and put his hand on his heart, and made his cracked voice soft. You are seventy-five years old. You have carried this džemat on your back for twenty years. You are tired. We can all see it. Rest. Sit with your grandchildren. Let me carry it for you.

The hall was quiet. Ramiz-efendija looked at him a while from the table at the front.

I would not give it to the hafiz, he said. I would not give it to Bego. And I should give it to an ulizica?

It is a word for a man who licks up after someone, a lickspittle. Nobody in the hall had ever heard the imam call any man a name. He said it the way he said everything, not loudly, and went on to the roof.

The board met the next week and voted that Nermin Hadžić was not to lead the prayer in that hall again, or recite in it, until further notice. Kenan Mujkić read it out on Friday. Nermin sat in the last row and heard his name read, and from that day he hated the old man, which he had not done before.

III. The Truck

Holly Hills and Gravois · summer 2018

Ramiz-efendija lived on Holly Hills Boulevard, in a brick house across from Carondelet Park, with his daughter and her husband and three grandchildren. In May two of Dino's drivers came to see him there at nine o'clock at night. They were Šemso Hodžić and a young one called Kemal, and they had been paid five hundred dollars each by somebody to go and talk to the old man, as it had been put to them, and make him understand that he was tired. Šemso had brought a tire iron and left it in the truck.

The imam opened the door himself in his slippers. He looked at the two of them a moment and said, Come in, the coffee is on, though it was not, and went and put it on. They sat in the front room for three hours. His granddaughter brought them Turkish delight on a plate. Mostly the old man asked Šemso about his mother, who was from a village near Sjenica where the imam had known a cousin, and Šemso, who had not cried since 1995, cried in the imam's front room, and Kemal looked at the carpet. On Friday both of them were in the hall, at the back, and came every Friday after, and gave the money to the džemat, and told Dino so.

In June, on the last Friday of Ramadan, the imam was at the side door of the hall taking off his shoes when a brick came off the parapet two stories up and broke on the concrete step beside him. A piece of it went into his foot through the sock. Nermin had keys to the roof, because he had always seen to the air conditioner, and he was up there, and said afterward he had been seeing to it. Zijad the roofer went up that afternoon and came down and said only that the bricks on that parapet were mortared and he had laid them himself. The imam had Bego pick the brick out of his foot with tweezers and wash it and tape it, and led the prayer in his socks with a little blood coming through, and that was all he said about it.

Dino had paid for the kurban that year. At Kurban Bajram in August, the meat of eleven lambs was to be given out in the lot behind the hall after the prayer, and he had brought a new tractor to the alley beside the lot to be looked at, a black Mack with chrome stacks and KOVAČ in silver on the doors, the newest truck in the fleet. The alley runs downhill into the lot. The prayer was in the lot, under the August sun, eight hundred men in rows on tarps and prayer rugs and flattened cardboard, the women under the tents at the side.

Somebody had pulled the chocks. Somebody had climbed into the cab while the rows were bowing and pushed in the yellow knob on the dash, and the truck came down the alley slowly, the way a big animal walks when it means it, and into the lot, and toward the front of the rows, where the imam was standing alone facing the wall with his hands raised.

Emir Selimović was in the second row. He had driven all night from Memphis to be there. He did not decide anything. He was up and out of the row and standing between the truck and the old man with his arms out before the people behind him had finished screaming, and he had time to see the chrome of the grille coming, and the bulldog on the hood, and his own face in the bumper.

The imam had turned around. He put up one hand, the palm out, the way you would to a horse.

The truck stopped. Eight hundred people saw it. It stopped a hand's width from Emir's chest, with a long sigh of air, and then the front of it went down, slowly, until the bumper was nearly on the asphalt, the way a camel goes down, or an elephant in a picture book, onto its knees. The imam lowered his hand. He turned back to the wall and began the prayer again from the beginning, and after a while, one by one, the rows behind him did too.

Afterward people talked about the air brakes, and about the springs, and about the yard man Dino had brought with him, who was fired that same afternoon and by Christmas was driving for a company in Chicago. It did not matter what they said. The brick could be a loose brick and the drivers could be two men who had stopped by for coffee and Fadil could have been an old man with a weak heart. But eight hundred people had seen a truck come down that alley at the imam, and they knew whose truck it was, and who had been riding around for a year in Dino Kovač's Tahoe with Dino Kovač's name on the doors. Hajra Kovač said in the lot, in front of everyone, what Dino had done to his father. Nobody took the Kovač meat home. It went to the shelter on Chippewa.

Dino took the Tahoe back the following week, and the title, and the salary, and stopped paying for Thursday nights on Facebook. The families stopped calling Nermin to wash their dead. When he went to the houses where he had always been welcome at iftar, nobody came to the door, though the lights were on and he could hear the television.

IV. The Clipboard

Gravois and Affton · October 2018 to June 2019

In October he asked to speak at an extraordinary meeting of the džemat, and the board, because it was the rule, let him. He had five things to put before them. Every man in the džemat should pray all five prayers in the hall and none at home. Every man should fast Mondays and Thursdays, all year. No meat should be eaten in any house but at the two Bajrams. No man or woman should wear gold. No wedding should have music. He said them in his broken voice, standing, with a sheet of paper.

The imam heard him out. The Prophet fasted Mondays and Thursdays, he said. Whoever wants to, let him fast. Whoever wants to eat no meat, let him eat bread. Whoever can come to all five prayers, I'll be glad to see him. I will not make a law where God made none.

Nermin turned to the hall. Brothers. Whose words are stricter, his or mine? Whoever wants to be saved, come with me. And he took a clipboard from under his arm, with a sign-up sheet on it, and walked out to the parking lot and stood there under the light with a pen on a string.

Forty-three young men signed it. Most of them were under twenty-five, sons of drivers, boys who had been born in St. Louis and learned their prayers on Saturdays and had never had anybody ask anything hard of them in their lives. They went with him to a building in Affton that had been a Pizza Hut, out on Gravois Road past the city line, with the red roof still on it, and on Fridays they prayed there on carpet laid over the old floor, and Nermin preached.

The imam said nothing against it from the minbar. Once, in his Wednesday lesson, when somebody told him that Nermin had begun sitting at the front of the Pizza Hut with a young man on either side of him, the way the imam sat at Ramadan with the hafiz on one side and the muezzin on the other, and was holding his hands the way the imam held his, the old man told a story instead.

On the Lim, he said, below my village, there was a cormorant who fished the deep pool under the bridge, and a crow who came to watch him. The cormorant fed him the little fish. One day the crow said, he's black and I'm black, he has a beak and I have a beak, what do I need him for? And he went into the pool after the fish himself, and the weeds took him, and that was the last of him. It's not the color that fishes, the imam said, and went on with the lesson, which was about the conditions of the fast.

•••

In May the hafiz Senad Mehić came back from Sarajevo for Ramadan with Adem the muezzin. On the fifteenth night, after the iftar, the two of them borrowed Emir's minivan and drove out Gravois to Affton. Senad wore the gray socks with the red band that his old teacher had knit him, and when he took off his shoes in the doorway of the Pizza Hut, the boys saw them.

Nermin was at the front, between his two young men. He looked up and saw the hafiz in the door, and it went through the room that the hafiz had come over to Nermin, and Nermin smiled and waved him in to sit beside him and said, Senad, you're tired, recite for us, I'll rest my voice. He lay down on a cushion by the wall and slept, as he did most nights now, early.

Senad did not sit up front. He sat down on the floor among the boys and recited. He recited the sura of the Cave, the whole of it, from memory, too fast, the way he always had, and when he finished, Adem went out into the parking lot under the Pizza Hut sign and gave the call to the night prayer in a voice that the people at the gas station across Gravois Road stopped pumping to listen to. Then the two of them got up and walked out to Emir's minivan. Thirty-nine of the forty-three boys went with them, some in the van and the rest in their own cars behind.

Mrs. Suljić, who lived on the corner of Gravois and Delor and did not sleep, said she saw them come back up Gravois a little after midnight, the van in front and the cars behind it in a line, and that the van's wheels were not on the street. Her son said she needed new glasses. She went to Haris for them, and he gave her the new glasses, and she said the same thing.

Hamza Ćosić, who had been Nermin's friend since the refugee center in 1997 and had never liked the hafiz, woke Nermin on the cushion. Get up. Senad's taken your boys. Didn't I tell you? Didn't I say, don't trust the Sarajevo ones?

Nermin sat up. Senad wants what I have, he said. He always did. On the same bench he wanted it. And he struck himself in the chest with his fist, hard, twice, the way a man does who means it, and the blood came up out of his mouth onto the old Pizza Hut carpet.

At the hall that night, when the boys came in, the old men stood up from the tables. The hafiz had gone out with one young muezzin and come back with a crowd behind him. Ramiz-efendija told a story then too, standing at the tea urn.

On the Pešter, he said, there was an old shepherd with two sons, and when he got too old to take the sheep up to the summer grass he gave each boy half the flock. Up there on the high pasture there are wolves, and below there are villages, and the people of the villages know when the flocks come down in September, and they wait by the road for a stray. One boy brought his sheep down by daylight, the short way, past every house, and every house had one of his sheep for supper, and he came home to his father with eleven. The other brought his down at night, the long way, around by the ridge, and never once went near a village, and came home with every sheep he took up and the lambs besides. You should have seen the old man's face, the imam said, and laughed, and looked at Senad, who was standing at the sink with his sleeves rolled up, washing the big pots.

V. Horseshoe Lake

Affton and Carondelet Park · February 2020

It was in his stomach, and by the time the doctors at Barnes looked, it was in his liver too. He was sick for nine months. Dino did not come. Of the four boys who had stayed at the Pizza Hut, three went home by Christmas, and the lease ran out on the building in January, and Nermin lay in a rented room above a nail salon on Gravois Road with Hamza and the last boy, Edin, who was seventeen, to bring him water.

In February he asked to see the imam.

Take me to him.

Hamza said no. When you were on your feet you threw bricks at him. You sent a truck at him. Now you want him?

I hated him, Nermin said. I'll give you that. But he never hated me. Not this much. He held up his finger and thumb with nothing between them. Not one hair. Take me.

He said it every hour, and on the third day they did it. They laid him on the mattress in the back of Hamza's minivan with the seats folded down, and drove him in along Loughborough Avenue toward Holly Hills, and Edin called ahead to his cousin on Itaska Street, who called the imam's son-in-law, who told the imam.

Nermin is coming to see you, efendija. Hamza's bringing him.

The old man was in his chair by the front window. He won't see me, he said. He did not say it as a man says he will not let somebody in. He said it the way you would say it was going to rain.

The phones went on all afternoon. They're on Gravois. They've turned off Gravois. They're on Loughborough. They're at the park. His grandchildren were at the window. Even if he gets to the door, the imam said, he won't see me.

It was the time for the afternoon prayer, and Hamza would not take a man to the imam's house without the abdest, so he pulled into the lot by Horseshoe Lake, on the south side of the park, and he and Edin went down to the edge of the water to wash, though it was February and the water was black and cold. They left the side door open. Nermin got himself out of the van. He walked the few steps to a bench at the top of the bank, and sat down on it, and put both his feet on the ground.

Carondelet Park is old ground. Under it the rock is full of holes, and the lakes themselves were sinkholes once that the city joined up and filled with water a hundred years ago. It had rained all week and thawed. Edin had his hands in the water when he heard a sound behind him like a sheet of plywood being slowly laid down, and turned, and Nermin was in the ground to his ankles, sitting on the bench, which was going too.

He went down slowly. Edin said that afterward to everyone who asked, and to the police, and to the man from the city, and to the imam. To his knees. To his waist. Edin ran up the bank and caught him under the arms and could not hold him, it was like holding a man in a river, and Hamza came and they both pulled and it took him to his chest, and then to his neck, and Hamza was on his knees in the mud and Edin was crying. Nermin's face was quiet. His jaw was on the grass. He looked up the long slope of the park toward Holly Hills, where the imam's house was, and with the ground holding his jaw he said the words he had said into a hundred dying ears, in the bad cracked voice and then, for the last of it, in the old one, the one he had when he was nine.

Ešhedu en la ilahe illallah, ve ešhedu enne Muhammeden abduhu ve resuluhu.

Then the grass closed over the place like a mouth.

The fire department came, and the city, and dug for two days with a backhoe in the rain while the hole kept filling, and found the bench, upside down, nine feet down, and nothing else. A man from the city said the clay down there was like pudding, and that the rock under the clay was like a sponge, and that you could lose a car in it. They put a fence around the place and in March they filled it with crushed rock, load after load, and tamped it, and put the dirt back over the top.

VI. Ah, Welladay

Gravois and Holly Hills · March 2020

Ramiz-efendija went to the fence the first night, after the night prayer, alone, with a flashlight. He stood at the chain-link with the lake behind him and recited the Yasin over the hole, the whole of it, the way it is recited over the dying, and when he came home he called Kenan Mujkić and told him to take the džemat book out of the safe and write Nermin Hadžić's name in it again, where it had been struck out in 2018, as a member in good standing.

Efendija. After everything?

He was on his way here, the imam said. Write it.

There was no washing, because there was nothing to wash. There was no grave.

On Gravois it was not the same as a death. People who had not said Nermin's name in a year said it over and over now, at the bakery and at the butcher's and on the phones between the trucks, and some of them said it was God's justice and said it with satisfaction, and a girl on Facebook posted the verse about Korah, whom the earth swallowed, and four hundred people shared it. On the Friday after, the women brought trays of baklava and tulumbe to the hall and set them out after the prayer, the way you would for a wedding or a boy coming home from the army, and the men stood around the tables eating and talking about the ground in the park.

Bego Sejdić did not eat. He stood by the door with his coat on, and he was crying.

Bego. What's the matter with you? You of all people. He slapped your neck every time he came into your shop, coming and going. Cigo, he called you, for twenty years.

Every time, Bego said. Going in and coming out. I'm not crying because he's gone. He wiped his face with his hand. I'm crying because he'll be too much for them down there. He'll slap the angels. And they'll say, take him back, we can't stand him, send him up. And he'll come back in my door.

Ramiz-efendija had been watching the tables from the minbar steps, with his shoes off, in his socks. He stood and asked for quiet, and recited, and then said it again in Bosnian for the young ones, the verses that come after Korah, which the girl had not posted.

So We caused the earth to swallow him and his dwelling-place. Then he had no host to help him against Allah, nor was he of those who can save themselves.

And morning found those who had coveted his place but yesterday crying: Ah, welladay! Allah enlargeth the provision for whom He will of His slaves and straiteneth it (for whom He will). If Allah had not been gracious unto us He would have caused it to swallow us (also). Ah, welladay! the disbelievers never prosper.

— Qur’an 28:81–82

Us also, he said. That's who is talking in that verse. The ones eating the baklava. And he sat down, and after a while people began to put the baklava back on the trays.

•••

Hamza Ćosić came to the hall for the first time in two years the next week, and sat in the back, and afterward he told the imam a dream. He had dreamed of Nermin standing in a room of black iron, up to his chin in an iron floor, with iron bars going through him from all four walls and one more down through the top of his head, so that he could not turn it to see who was coming in the door.

Edin was with him. He had not slept much since the park. He asked it straight out, the way the boys born in St. Louis ask things.

Efendija. Where is he now?

The old man looked at the boy for a long time. Then he gave him two verses, from two places in the Book, one after the other, the way you hand somebody two things to carry.

For them is torment in the life of the world, and verily the doom of the Hereafter is more painful, and they have no defender from Allah.

— Qur’an 13:34

And hell is brought near that day; on that day man will remember, but how will the remembrance (then avail him)? He will say: Ah, would that I had sent before me (some provision) for my life!

— Qur’an 89:23–24

Here, and there, and remembering, the imam said. That's where he is. He put his hand on Edin's head. And he said the words. You heard him. God knows the rest, and the rest is long.

In April the city seeded the fill. The grass came up thin over the crushed rock, a paler green than the grass around it, in a ragged round the size of a room, and the Canada geese that winter on Horseshoe Lake liked it better than any other place in the park. They stood on it in the mornings in a crowd, forty or fifty of them, pulling at the new grass, and when anyone came down the path from Holly Hills they all lifted their heads at once and looked, and then went back to eating, and did not move off.

Background

The verse

The evil-doer suffers in this world, and he suffers in the next; he suffers in both. He suffers when he thinks of the evil he has done; he suffers more when going on the evil path.Dhammapada 17, tr. F. Max Müller (1881)

The commentary gives Devadatta's whole career. He goes forth with five Sakyan princes, among them Anuruddha (whose mother once sent him an empty covered bowl to teach him the words "there isn't any," and the gods filled it), and with their barber Upāli, whom the princes have ordained first to humble their pride. Devadatta gains only lesser powers. When visitors ask after every great disciple but him, he wins over Prince Ajātasattu, appearing to him first in a frightening form; loses his powers the moment he wants to lead the order; asks the aging Buddha to hand it over; is refused and called a lickspittle. He urges Ajātasattu to kill his father the king, hires assassins who are converted instead, hurls down a rock that wounds the Buddha's foot, and sends the drunken elephant Nālāgiri, before which Ānanda stands. After the elephant the people turn on him and the king withdraws his support. He demands five stricter rules, is told that whoever wishes may keep them, leads five hundred young monks away by a vote with tickets, and imitates the Buddha's manner until Sāriputta and Moggallāna bring the monks back. He coughs up hot blood, is sick nine months, and asks to be carried to the Buddha, who says he will not see him; set down by the lotus pool at Jetavana while his bearers bathe, he sinks into the earth, taking refuge in the Buddha with his last breath. The people celebrate, and a birth story tells of a doorkeeper who wept at a cruel king's funeral for fear he would be sent back. This story continues Four Votes (1.7). South St. Louis, 2017–2020

Refugees from the Bosnian war settled in St. Louis by the tens of thousands from the 1990s on, first in Bevo Mill and then increasingly in the suburbs of south St. Louis County, and founded coffee shops, bakeries, auto shops and trucking companies (see History of Bosnian Americans in St. Louis). Some came from beyond Bosnia, from the Bosniak region of Sandžak in Serbia and Montenegro, which takes in the high Pešter plateau. Goražde, in eastern Bosnia, was besieged from 1992 to 1995; when the convoys were cut off, food was dropped to it from the air. The džemat, its people, the Kovač company, the old Pizza Hut and the collapse at the lake are invented.

Carondelet Park, some 180 acres on the city's south side, was bought by the city in 1874 and opened the next year. Its rolling ground was shaped by caves and sinkholes in the limestone beneath it: Horseshoe Lake was made in 1913 by joining nine sinkholes, and the boathouse went up in 1918. The Qur'an tells of Korah (Qarun), one of Moses' own people, who grew proud of his wealth and was swallowed by the earth with his house (28:76–82).
