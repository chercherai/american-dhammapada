The Rock Spring

The Moon doth with delight
Look round her when the heavens are bare;
— William Wordsworth, Ode: Intimations of Immortality, II (1807)

As it was given me, at Cassadaga.

I. The Dinner Pail

Pomfret, Chautauqua County · 1840

Annis Wheelock, before she was anybody, was the hired girl on Sumner Hale's farm on the ridge above the Cassadaga lakes. She was seventeen. She milked, churned, scrubbed, carried, and in haying time took the men their dinners in the field and then went back for her own, which was corn bread and a piece of salt pork in a tin pail with a cloth over it, set aside for her on the back of the stove. Sumner Hale was the richest man in the town of Pomfret. He gave a great deal away, at the church and on the roads, with his name on it, and he was proud of what he gave.

One noon in July, coming back across the field for her pail, Annis met an old man on the road. He was a tin peddler by the look of his pack, but he had no tin in it, and he had a tin cup in his hand with nothing in it either. He did not ask for anything. When she said, Have you not had your dinner? he looked at her a long time and said, I expect to have it, daughter.

Then wait, said Annis.

She ran to the house. The cook had kept her pail. She ran back with it and gave him all of it, the corn bread and the pork. She remembered all her life what she was thinking while she ran. That when she had wanted to give anything, she had never had anything to give, and when she had anything, there had been nobody to give it to, and today there were both at once. As she put the pail in his hands she said a thing she had not known she was going to say. I hope I never in my life have to say there isn't any.

So be it, said the old man, and ate, and went on down the road toward Fredonia, and nobody saw him after.

The hired man, who was forking hay in the barn, swore afterward that a voice in the empty mow cried out three times, Well given! Well given! Well given! and that he looked and there was nobody in the mow but the swallows. He told it at supper. Sumner Hale heard it and was put out, because he had given away a great deal in his life and no voice had ever called out from a haymow for him.

He called Annis into the front room. I'll give you a dollar for that dinner, he said. She said it was not for sale. He went to five, and to ten, and at last to a hundred dollars in gold, which was more than she would earn in five years, and she said it was not for sale. Then sell me the good of it, said Sumner Hale. Whatever you got by it. A hundred dollars.

Annis did not know what to say to that, so she went out and found the old man, who was sitting under a beech tree on the Fredonia road, and asked him.

If you light your lantern, the old man said, and every house on this road comes and lights its lantern off yours and carries it home, is there less light in yours?

There's more light on the road, said Annis.

That's all it is, said the old man.

So she went back and told Sumner Hale he could have the good of her dinner and welcome, but she would not take his money for it, and he said in that case he would not have her work for him with her hands anymore. He paid for her schooling at the Fredonia Academy. She taught school after. She married and was widowed young. In 1850, when the rappings were going all over western New York, she sat in a circle for the first time and found that she could see. The Spiritualists called her a clairvoyant. She could see a long way, and into rooms she was not in, and sometimes, she said, a little way into what had been.

And from that day in the hayfield until the day she died, it was said, Annis Wheelock never once had to say there wasn't any. Children came to her door and asked for bread, and she lifted the cloth on the bread bowl, and there was bread. Her hired girls swore to it. One of them said she had looked into the bowl herself before the cloth went on, and it was empty.

II. The Season

The Hale farm and the camp grounds · June–August 1897

By the summer of 1897 Annis Wheelock was seventy-four, and her gift was the most famous in the camp at Cassadaga. She came every season for the June picnic and stayed until the grapes were picked. Old Sumner Hale was dead thirty years. That spring, sitting in her parlor in Buffalo with her eyes shut, she had looked for him, the way she sometimes looked for people, and found him. He was standing behind a small boy in a grape vineyard on the ridge above the lake, with his hand on the boy's head. The boy was his grandson, Hiram Hale's youngest, and his name was Sumner too. He would be seven in July.

She wrote to Hiram Hale, whom she had known since he was a baby, and he wrote back that he would count it an honor to have her for the season. So she boarded that summer at the Hale farm and was driven down to the camp each morning in the Hales' democrat wagon, past the gate where the ticket man took a quarter from visitors and touched his hat to her. She sat on the platform of the Auditorium under the great roof on its posts, with the curtains drawn up and the lake shining beyond. She gave sittings in the parlor of the Grand Hotel. She heard Mrs. Twing and Mr. Hull, and the young man from Ceylon, in white, who spoke about the Buddha. In the evenings she sat on the Hales' porch with her eyes shut, and the Hales were very careful to be quiet, and Hiram's wife Ruth brought her tea and saw that her shawl stayed on.

Little Sumner watched her. He was a thin, grave, fair-haired boy with his hair still in long curls to his collar, the way his mother liked it. He sat on the porch step at her feet in the evenings and did not fidget, and once, when she opened her eyes, he said, Who was the man with the tin cup? and she looked at him a long while and did not answer.

When the camp closed on the twenty-ninth of August, Hiram Hale brought out what he had laid by for her: a ten-dollar gold piece, a crock of honey, two gallons of maple syrup from the spring sugaring, and the first baskets of Concords off the south slope. He set them at her feet on the porch.

I have no use for them, Hiram, said Annis.

It's what's done, Mrs. Wheelock. For a season's board, the other way around.

I have no use for them.

Why not?

I have nobody to see to me, said Annis. My eyes are going and my legs are gone. I need somebody to fetch and carry.

Then take Hi, said Hiram. Hi was twelve and strong.

I don't want Hi.

Hiram looked at her, and then at the porch step. Then take Sumner.

Yes, said Annis. Him.

Ruth Hale cried, but she said yes, because it was Mrs. Wheelock and because it was only to the camp ground, three miles off, where Annis kept a cottage by the lake and wintered when the weather allowed. And the next morning, because a boy going out to work should look like a boy, Ruth took Sumner down to the barber in Cassadaga village to have his curls cut off for the first time. The barber put him up on a board across the arms of the chair. Ruth said afterward that she was watching his face in the glass when the scissors went through the first curl, and that his face changed. It did not change much. It was only that he looked, all at once, as if he had heard his name called from a long way off and had answered. By the time the barber had swept the curls into a paper for her to keep, it was done, whatever it was. Mrs. Wheelock said so as soon as they came in at the gate. She said he had come out all at once, without any developing at all, the way she had heard of only once or twice in fifty years, and that he could see farther already than she could.

She stayed at the farm a fortnight more. Then she and the boy went down to the cottage on the grounds.

III. The Rock Spring

Saratoga Springs · September 1897

The camp was empty by then. The hotels were shut, the boats pulled up, the Auditorium curtains let down and tied. The two of them had the grounds nearly to themselves. Annis kept her hours as she always had. She sat up half the night on the cottage porch, walking up and down it when her legs allowed, sitting for the spirits, and by the middle of September she had brought on the dyspepsia that came on her every fall. She went yellow and could not keep her supper. Sumner found her on the porch in the gray of the morning with her hand pressed under her ribs.

What ails you, Mrs. Wheelock?

My old trouble.

Have you had it before?

Many a time.

What cures it?

She smiled. Water from the Rock Spring at Saratoga, child. I drank it when I was a girl and it cured me, and nothing else ever has.

Then I'll fetch you some.

She looked at him. Saratoga was three hundred miles away across the state. Can you?

Yes, ma'am.

The spring belongs to a man named Asa Pennock now, she said. He knew me once. Tell him your errand, and ask him for a jar of water for medicine.

Sumner took a quart Mason jar off the pantry shelf and went out of the cottage. That is all anyone on the grounds saw of it. The man who kept the South Park House, which stayed open the year round, said he saw the boy cross the park toward the lake at a little after five in the morning. In Saratoga, a milkman on his route said he saw a fair-haired boy with a jar come down out of the air and light on the roof of the pump house at the Rock Spring as the sun came up. He told it for the rest of his life.

•••

The Rock Spring lay in a little ravine at the edge of the town, below the bluff, where the water had come up out of the ground since before there was a town and the Mohawk had come to it before there were any white men at all. Asa Pennock had bought the ground in the eighties, drilled it and sunk his pipes. He pumped the gas out of the water and sold it in iron cylinders to the soda fountains of New York, Boston and Philadelphia. It was good business. The water that had once come up in the basin by itself now came up only when his pumps let it, and he had built a pump house over the place and capped the old spring with an iron bell, and there was a padlock on the door. Half the old springs of Saratoga had gone dry that way in ten years. People complained. Pennock was a big, florid, short-tempered man who had made his money hard and liked to stand in his own doorway in the morning and look at it.

He was standing in the doorway that morning when the boy lit on his roof. He heard the small boots on the shingles over his head.

Here's some camp-meeting brat walking around on top of me, he said to his engineer. Come for water, I suppose. Well, he won't get it. And he went out and locked the pump-house door behind him and stood in front of it with his arms folded.

Sumner climbed down and stood in front of him in the wet grass.

Mr. Pennock, he said, I've come for a jar of water from the Rock Spring. It's for medicine. Mrs. Annis Wheelock sent me. She says you knew her.

Did she. Pennock looked down at him. There's the Hudson River twelve miles east of here, boy, running down to the sea. Plenty of water in it. Go dip your jar in that.

She said this water and no other, said Sumner. So I'll take this. If you can stop me, stop me.

Pennock laughed. If you're man enough to get it, take it.

You're sure?

Take it if you can.

You're sure? said Sumner a third time, and Pennock, who was beginning not to like the way the boy looked at him, said he was sure.

What Sumner did then was told on the camp grounds afterward by people who said they had it from the other side, and they told it plainly, and so it is set down here. He went up. He went up through the spheres, one after another, the way you would go down a street knocking on doors. He went to the spirits who keep near the earth, and to the ones above them, and above those, all the way up through the bright ones to the edge of where anything can be told about at all, and at every door he said the same thing. There's going to be a contest between me and Asa Pennock at the Rock Spring at Saratoga. Come down and see who wins. And they came. They came down over the ravine at sunrise until the air above it was packed with them, close as shot poured into a measure.

Nobody in Saratoga could see them. But every night clerk on Broadway remembered that morning. In the great empty parlors of the hotels, the pianos sounded a note by themselves. On every door along Circular Street there came a knock, and when people opened, there was nobody on the step, and still the knocking went on, door after door, as if a crowd too big for the street were passing by. The milkman's horse would not go another step and stood in the road with its ears back.

Then the boy asked Pennock a fourth time, in front of all of them, and Pennock said what he had said before. And the milkman said that for the space of a breath the boy was as tall as the elms on Broadway, so tall his head was in the sun while the ravine was still in shadow. He came down on the roof of the pump house with one foot. The roof folded under him like a wet glove under a boot heel. The iron bell over the old spring split along its seam. Water came up through every seam, joint and bolt hole in the place, in jets as tall as the elms, a dozen of them, white, roaring, smelling of iron, the way Saratoga's spouting springs had gone up in the old days before the pumps. The boy, standing in the air above all of it, held out his Mason jar and filled it.

IV. Mr. Pennock

The camp ground, Lily Dale · the same day and the next

Sumner was on the cottage porch by noon. He unscrewed the jar and put it in Mrs. Wheelock's hands. Drink it, ma'am.

Asa Pennock was a day behind him. He took the morning train to Albany, then the New York Central west to Buffalo, and changed twice more. He got down at the Lily Dale station on the evening of the next day with his eyes red as sumac and his collar sprung, and walked into the closed camp ground asking at every cottage for Mrs. Wheelock. He had shut down his pumps and come straight on. In the train, he had told the conductor, he had decided he would either wring the boy's neck or carry him to Niagara and throw him over the Falls, he had not yet decided which.

Annis had not drunk the water yet. She was sitting on the porch with the jar in her lap. Pennock stood at the foot of her steps and took off his hat, because he could not help it, he had known her when he was young. Mrs. Wheelock, he said, that boy of yours took my water without my leave. Don't drink it.

Sumner, said Annis, is that so?

No, ma'am, said the boy. Mr. Pennock gave it to me. He said so three times.

Annis knew that the boy could not lie. So she unscrewed the jar and drank. It was cold and tasted of iron, the way it had when she was a girl, and almost at once she felt the knot under her ribs let go.

He made a fool of me, said Pennock. He brought down I don't know what, the whole sky, and stood on my roof, and made a fool of me before the whole town. I'll have satisfaction for it.

Asa, said Annis, you can't fight that child. You'd do better to beg his pardon and go home. And she said to him what was written:

Out of the mouth of babes and sucklings hast thou ordained strength because of thine enemies, that thou mightest still the enemy and the avenger.

— Psalm 8:2

Pennock already knew it, of course. He had known it on his roof. He had come three hundred miles only because he had been shamed, and a shamed man has to do something. He stood at the foot of the steps a while with his hat in his hands. Then he begged the boy's pardon, and shook his hand, a great hairy hand around a small one.

Another time, he said, don't you trouble to come yourself. You send me a wire. I'll put the water on the express myself, and it won't cost you a cent.

Then he walked back through the empty grounds to the station and took the night train east.

V. The Lyceum

Library Hall and the Grand Hotel · opening week, July 1898

The camp opened the next summer on the fifteenth of July. Annis came down to the Grand Hotel on the first afternoon, on the boy's arm, to pay her respects to Mrs. Electa Barnard.

Mrs. Barnard was eighty-four, the oldest trance speaker in the movement and the one people came farthest to hear. She had been on the platform since the year after the rappings, in every hall from Boston to San Francisco. She was a small, wide old woman in black silk, almost blind, and off the platform she hardly spoke at all. On the platform, when the spirits had her, her voice filled the Auditorium to the back rows and out onto the lake. People said there was nothing she did not know. That afternoon she was sitting on the long veranda of the Grand Hotel in a wicker chair with her hands folded on her cane, and everyone who came in at the gate came up the steps to her.

The mediums and lecturers and patrons on the veranda made a great deal of Annis, and then of the boy. Some of them had heard about the Rock Spring and did not believe it, and some had not heard. They patted his cropped head. A test medium from Cleveland took hold of his ear between two fingers and gave it a shake. Well, little medium, aren't you homesick? Don't you want to go home to your mama? A lecturer from Toledo tweaked the other ear. The boy stood there between them and let them, with his hands at his sides.

Mrs. Barnard turned her face toward the sound. She thought that they were picking up a copperhead by the neck and did not know it. She thought that it was time they knew.

Orrin, she said, to Mr. Orrin Bell, who was Conductor of the Children's Lyceum and stood always near her chair to do what she asked. My feet are bad. I should like to bathe them tonight in water from the Rock Spring at Saratoga. Ask the Lyceum children to fetch it.

Mr. Bell went down to Library Hall, where the Lyceum met every morning of the season and had met that first morning, a hundred and forty children in their twelve groups, and he called them together. The Lyceum went by groups as the old manual laid it down. The littlest were Fountain Group, who wore red badges. Then came Stream, River, Lake, Sea, Ocean, Shore, Beacon, Banner, Star and Excelsior, and at the top the big boys and girls of Liberty Group, in white. Mr. Bell went to the eldest boy of Liberty Group first.

Mrs. Barnard wishes to bathe her feet tonight in water from the Rock Spring at Saratoga. Will you fetch it?

I can't, sir, said the boy, and went red.

Mr. Bell asked them all, group by group, down through Excelsior, Star, Banner and the rest. Every one of them said they could not. Some of them, it was said afterward, could have gone. There were children in that Lyceum who had come out young and could do things, and their mothers knew it. They said no anyway, because they knew whose errand it was. The ones who could not have gone said no because they could not.

Sumner Hale was the youngest in Fountain Group, and so the last in the room. Mr. Bell came to him.

Mrs. Barnard wishes—

If Mrs. Barnard wants it, I'll fetch it, said Sumner, and went up to the hotel and stood in front of her chair. Ma'am, they tell me you want water from the Rock Spring.

Yes, Sumner.

The great ice-water pitcher stood on a table in the hotel parlor, where it had stood since Mrs. Lavinia Pettibone of Titusville, whose husband had struck oil on Pithole Creek, gave it to the camp. It was silver-plate gilded all over, chased with lilies, and held two gallons, and full it took two men to lift it. Sumner took it off the table. He did not trouble to put it on his shoulder. He took it by the rim in one hand and let it hang, and went out through the parlor door and down the veranda steps. The people on the veranda watched him go up over the lawn, out over the lake and away east above the trees, the pitcher swinging from his hand and catching the sun.

At Saratoga, Asa Pennock saw him coming while he was still a long way off. He came out of his new pump house at a run, bareheaded, and met the boy in the ravine and took the pitcher out of his hand.

Now why, he said, when you've got a man like me in the world, would you come yourself? Why didn't you wire? He filled the pitcher at the spring with his own hands, and settled it on his own shoulder. You go on ahead, son. I'll carry it.

Stay where you are, Mr. Pennock, said Sumner. Mrs. Barnard sent me. He took the pitcher back by the rim and went up.

Mrs. Barnard could not see him come. But she lifted her face on the veranda as if she could, and said to the people around her, Look at him. He comes like a wild goose coming in to the water. They looked. He came down over the lake out of the evening with the gilt pitcher swinging from his hand, lit on the lawn, carried it up the steps without spilling a drop, and set it at her feet.

How old are you, Sumner? said Mrs. Barnard.

Seven, ma'am. Eight on Wednesday.

Then on Sunday you'll sit on the platform, said Mrs. Barnard. With the mediums.

The people on the veranda were very quiet. Nobody sat on the platform of the Auditorium at Cassadaga but the speakers and the chairman and the tested mediums whose names were printed in the program. They said afterward that in all the years of the camp only two children had ever been given a chair there as mediums in their own right. One was a little girl from Ohio in the early days, whose name nobody could agree on. The other was Sumner Hale.

VI. The Platform

The Auditorium · Sunday, July 1898

On Sunday the Auditorium was full, fifteen hundred people on the benches and more standing on the grass beyond the posts with the curtains rolled up and the lake bright behind them. The band played. The speakers came up onto the platform and took their chairs, and the boy came up with them in a clean collar, with his red Fountain badge pinned to his jacket, and sat in a chair that was too big for him. His feet did not reach the floor.

In Library Hall that week there was no other talk among the mediums. They had all seen the pitcher come in over the lake, or knew someone who had. A medium from Cleveland, the same one who had shaken the boy's ear on the veranda, said she had been in the work thirty-one years and had never seen a power like it. Nobody had. They were still saying it when Mrs. Barnard came in on her cane one morning to hear the Lyceum, and asked them what they were talking about, and they told her.

She sat down. She was quiet a long time. Then she said that such a power was given to any child, any child at all, who came to it whole and gave it everything he had. That was all she said in her own voice. Then, because she was old and had been a schoolteacher in Vermont before ever she heard a rap, she said a piece of a poem that the Lyceum children all knew, and the mediums knew it too. It was the poem by Mr. Wordsworth about the child who comes trailing clouds of glory, the one Spiritualists had loved since before there were Spiritualists, and she said the part that is spoken to the child.

Thou, whose exterior semblance doth belie
Thy Soul's immensity;
Thou best Philosopher, who yet dost keep
Thy heritage, thou Eye among the blind,
That, deaf and silent, read'st the eternal deep,
Haunted for ever by the eternal mind,—
Mighty Prophet! Seer blest!
On whom those truths do rest,
Which we are toiling all our lives to find,

— William Wordsworth, Ode: Intimations of Immortality, VIII (1807)

The medium from Cleveland put her hand up to her mouth. A good many of the people in Library Hall went home from the camp that summer different from the way they had come, and some of them could have said why.

•••

Annis Wheelock lived three more summers. Sumner stayed with her and saw to her, fetching and carrying. In the evenings he sat on the step of the cottage porch at her feet while she sat with her eyes shut. She told him once, near the end, that his grandfather was standing behind him, as he had been the first time she saw him, with his hand on the boy's head. Sumner said he knew. She said they were the same two as always, the old man and the girl with the pail, and she did not say any more about it.

Every July after that, as long as the camp ran on the old lines and for some years after, a crate came up on the express to the Lily Dale station from Saratoga. It held twelve quart bottles packed in straw, and it was stenciled ROCK SPRING, with a tag wired to the slats in a big rough hand, and the tag always said the same thing. For the Hale boy. No charge.

The verse

He who, even as a young Bhikshu, applies himself to the doctrine of Buddha, brightens up this world, like the moon when free from clouds.Dhammapada 382, tr. F. Max Müller (1881)

In the commentary, a poor grass-carrier named Annabhāra gives his only meal to a solitary buddha and wishes never again to hear the word "there isn't." His rich employer, Sumana, tries to buy the merit of the gift. The solitary buddha compares merit to a lamp that lights a whole village without growing less, so Annabhāra gives it away freely and is made rich. Reborn as Anuruddha, the Buddha's cousin, he grows up so sheltered that when his mother sends him an empty covered bowl, the gods fill it with cakes. As a monk he becomes foremost in the divine eye. Seeing that his old friend Sumana has been reborn as a boy, he takes the child as his novice, and the boy becomes an arahat as his hair is cut. When Anuruddha falls ill, the seven-year-old flies to Lake Anotatta for water. The nāga king Paṇṇaka covers the lake with his hood and tells him to fetch water from the Ganges. The boy calls the gods of every heaven to witness, grows huge, treads on the nāga's hood, and fills his jar from the jets that spring up. The nāga pursues him, is told to beg the boy's pardon, and becomes his servant. Later, when monks tweak the novice's ears, the Buddha asks for water from Anotatta to bathe his feet. Of five hundred novices only the youngest goes, carrying a vast golden vessel and refusing the nāga's offer to carry it. The Buddha ordains him on the spot at the age of seven. Cassadaga and Saratoga, 1897

Lily Dale, on Upper Cassadaga Lake in Chautauqua County, was incorporated in 1879 as the Cassadaga Lake Free Association, a summer camp for Spiritualists and freethinkers. By the 1890s its season ran from mid-July to the end of August, with lectures and mediums in an open-sided Auditorium that seated about fifteen hundred, hotels, a library, and a Children's Lyceum that met each morning in Library Hall. The Lyceums followed the manual written by Andrew Jackson Davis in the 1860s, which sorted children into twelve groups named from the youngest up: Fountain, Stream, River, Lake, Sea, Ocean, Shore, Beacon, Banner, Star, Excelsior and Liberty, each with its own badge color. Women spoke from the platform on equal terms with men, and suffragists including Susan B. Anthony came to speak. In 1897 the Buddhist reformer Anagarika Dharmapala of Ceylon was among the season's speakers. The National Spiritualist Association, founded in 1893, has its headquarters there today.

Saratoga Springs, whose mineral springs the Mohawk had used long before European settlement, was by then the country's grandest spa, with its waters bottled and sold as cures for dyspepsia and much else. By the 1880s more than two hundred wells had been drilled around the town, most of them to extract carbonic acid gas for the soda fountains of the cities, and the pumping stopped the flow of many springs. In 1908 the state passed laws against the pumping, and the springs were taken into a state reservation, now Saratoga Spa State Park.
