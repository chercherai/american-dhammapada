Crowns on Their Heads

The righteous sit enthroned, their crowns on their heads, and enjoy the lustre of the Shekinah.
— Talmud, Berakot 17a

Azoy hob ikh gehert, on the Hill.

I. The Suits

Los Alamos, New Mexico · September 1950

Morris Halpern had come out to the Hill from Bayonne in the spring of 1945 with a toolbox, a wife, and a five-year-old daughter who was sick on every curve of the road up from the river. He ran a lathe. He had run one at the Standard Oil works on Constable Hook and he ran one on the mesa, in a shop behind a fence, making parts he was not told the use of and did not ask about. When the war ended the people who had come for the war began to leave, and the Halperns did not. There was work. There was a house in the Western Area with a yard of pine needles and a view of the Jemez, and a school, and the gate.

Everybody on the Hill knew the gate. You showed your pass to the guard at the guardhouse on the road in, and if your mother was coming from Bayonne for Passover you went down in person and signed for her a day ahead, and the guard looked at her face and then at the paper and then at her face. Nobody locked a door. Children left their bicycles lying in the street all night. The fence kept everything out, people said, and in the evening the floodlights came on along the fence by the pond and lay out across the water, white, so that the ducks slept in light.

That summer the newspapers came up the hill full of names. A man in England in February. A chemist in Philadelphia in May. In June a machinist from New York who had worked on the Hill during the war, in the shops, and then a man named Rosenberg, his brother-in-law, and then in August the wife. Morris read every word at the kitchen table and folded the paper small and did not say anything. He had not known the machinist from New York. Thousands of people had passed through the Hill in those years. He said so once, to the wall, and then not again.

The two men in gray suits came on a Thursday in September, in the afternoon, and sat in the front room with their hats on their knees while Esther did her arithmetic in the kitchen and listened. They were polite. They asked about 1945. They asked about a union meeting in Bayonne in 1937, and about Morris's brother Hymie, who sold the Freiheit on Broadway in Bayonne and always had. They asked who had come to the Seder. When they left they shook hands with her father and with her mother and nodded to Esther through the doorway.

On Monday Morris Halpern's clearance was suspended pending a hearing, and he came home at ten in the morning with his toolbox.

After that the street went quiet around them the way snow goes quiet. Nobody was unkind. Mrs. Dorsey in the steel house on Fairway Drive still said good morning, and then went in. The women who had stood in the Halperns' kitchen every Saturday drinking coffee stood in other kitchens. At school the teacher moved Esther's desk, for the light, she said. Esther was ten. She wrote to her grandfather in Bayonne every Sunday, and her grandfather, who had pressed pants for forty years and studied a page of Gemara every night of them, wrote back in Yiddish in a hand like barbed wire, and her mother read it to her, and at the bottom of every letter he wrote, in English, for her, Be happy, Essie, it costs nothing.

II. The Doors

Tuesday, October 31

Her mother bought her a mask at the commissary, a black cat on a string, and washed a pillowcase that morning and ironed it flat on the board with the sprinkle bottle, the way she ironed everything, so that it was folded in a square on the kitchen table when Esther came home from school, smelling of the iron.

You don't have to go, her mother said.

It's Halloween.

She put the mask in her coat pocket and did not put it on. Her mother saw that and said nothing. Her father was sitting in the front room with a lamp on in the daytime and a lathe catalogue open on his knee. He got up and walked her to the door and stood in it.

Who are you supposed to be? he said.

Me.

It was dark by six and cold, with the smell of piñon smoke coming down from the stovepipes of the older houses. Up and down the Western Area the porch lights were on, every one, and the children were out in packs, cowboys and skeletons and hoboes with burnt-cork beards, and more Hopalong Cassidys than anyone could count, because that was the year of Hopalong Cassidy, and the commissary had sold out of the black hats by the twentieth.

The Dorseys' porch light went out when Esther was halfway up the walk. She stood on the step a while in the dark and then she knocked anyway, and nobody came.

At the Kittredges' the door opened and Mr. Kittredge stood in it with a mixing bowl of Tootsie Rolls held against his stomach and looked down at her face, the bare face, and said, We're all out, honey, and shut the door.

At the Lindgrens' Mrs. Lindgren had made popcorn balls in waxed paper, and said, Oh, and looked over her shoulder into the house, and said, Oh, Esther, I'm sorry, and closed the door slowly, as though that were kinder.

She went up one side of the street and down the other. It was the strangest thing anybody had seen on the Hill, people said afterward, stranger than anything that came out of the Tech Area, and nobody could account for it. Doors that opened for every Hopalong on the street did not open for her. Hands that were full went empty when she came up the walk. One man who did not know her from Adam, a visitor from Albuquerque signed in for the week, stood in the doorway of the guest apartment holding a whole carton of Bit-O-Honey and could not, he said later, for the life of him lift his arm.

And the children in masks found her. They came round her at the corner of Forty-Fourth Street, eight or nine of them, rubber faces, a Frankenstein, a skull, three Hopalongs, and they did not touch her. They clapped. They clapped in her face, close, and laughed behind the rubber, and one of them said it, and then all of them said it, in time with the clapping, the word everybody's father said at the dinner table that year, and Esther stood still until they were finished and walked on. In the morning, every one of those children swore they had not been out on that corner at all. They had been at home. They had been at the party. Their mothers backed them up.

The pillowcase was as clean as when her mother folded it. It had the creases in it still.

III. The Fence

Ashley Pond · the same night

The road home went past the pond, where the floodlights were on along the fence on the far side, and at the corner where the fence came down to the road there was a man.

He was leaning on the post by the little gate there, the one the guards used, in a good camel-hair coat with the collar up, and he had a red devil's mask on, the kind with the horns molded in, and he was smoking a cigarette under the chin of it. He was not a guard. The guard was in his booth a hundred yards off with the radio on. Afterward nobody on the Hill could say who the man had been. The commissary had not sold a red mask that year, only the cats and the skulls. Nobody's husband owned a camel-hair coat.

Hiya, Essie, he said. Nothing, huh?

She stopped. She looked at the mask a long time.

Why'd you do that? she said.

He laughed. He had a nice laugh, a radio laugh.

Go around again, he said. Go on. Go around the whole Western Area one more time. Maybe they'll have come around. He tipped his ash. He was thinking, Esther understood, about the clapping, and about how much more of it there could be the second time, and the porch lights going out one after another down the street like a row of teeth, and he wanted to see it.

Then the girls came down the hill from the school.

There had been a party in the gym for the Brownies and the Girl Scouts, with a witch's cauldron of cider and a washtub for apples, and they came down the road together still laughing, eleven or twelve of them, Margie Ostrom and Patsy Delgado and Kay Lindgren and the Sheffield twins and the rest. Their hair was wet to the shoulders from bobbing, and every one of them was a Cinderella. That was the other thing that year. The picture had come up from Santa Fe in the spring and played at the Hill theater for two weeks, and their mothers had sat up nights making blue gowns out of old taffeta and pillowcase muslin, and the dime store in Santa Fe had been cleaned out of the rhinestone crowns, which came on a little comb and cost fifteen cents, so that a whole troop of wet-headed girls came down the road in the floodlight with crowns in their hair, and the crowns caught the light coming across the water and threw it back.

They saw Esther and they stopped. Some of them had not spoken to her since September. Their mothers had said not to.

Hi, Essie, said Patsy Delgado.

Hi, Essie, said Kay Lindgren, whose mother had closed the door slowly.

And the rest of them said it, and stood around her on the side of the road with their pillowcases, which were full, and their crowns, which were crooked, and did not know why they had stopped.

The man at the gate pushed himself off the post.

Kid hasn't got a single Tootsie Roll, he said to the girls. Not one. Look in her bag. And to Esther, softly, as if he were sorry for her: You must be starving.

Esther looked at the girls, at the wet hair and the crowns. She thought of her grandfather on Avenue C in Bayonne, at the table under the light with the big book open, and of the thing he said at every meal when somebody pushed back from the table complaining they were full, or complaining they were hungry, which in her grandfather's house came to the same thing. He said it in Yiddish and then he said it again in English, for her, the way Rav said it in the old book, and it was the only piece of the Gemara she knew by heart.

My zeyde says, she said to the man in the devil's face, that's not how it works.

Not like this world is the world to come. In the world to come there is neither eating nor drinking; no procreation of children or business transactions; no envy or hatred or rivalry; but the righteous sit enthroned, their crowns on their heads, and enjoy the lustre of the Shekinah.

— Talmud, Berakot 17a

Then she sat down on the low stone wall along the pond, with her empty pillowcase in her lap, in the light.

The girls looked at each other. Then Patsy Delgado sat down beside her, and Margie Ostrom on the other side, and the Sheffield twins, and the rest of them all along the wall in their blue gowns, and nobody opened a candy bar. The floodlights came across the water in long white bars and lay on them. Their crowns went on and off as they moved their heads. Somebody laughed, and then somebody else did, at nothing, at the cold, at Kay Lindgren's crown that had slid down over one ear, and then all of them were laughing, twelve girls and Esther on a wall in the light with their wet hair steaming, and none of them could have said what was so funny, and none of them could stop.

When they looked up the man was gone. The guard in the booth, asked about it the next day by more people than he could remember, said he had seen nobody at the little gate all night. He had seen the girls on the wall. He had heard them laughing from his booth, over the radio, he said, and he had turned the radio down to listen to it, because it was the best thing he had heard in that place.

They sat until the cold came through the taffeta. Then they walked Esther home, all of them, up Forty-Fourth Street and past the Dorseys' and the Kittredges', where the porch lights were on again, and left her at her door.

IV. The Guardhouse

The road in · 1951 and after

The hearing was in January. Morris Halpern sat at a long table in a room in the administration building and answered the same questions as in September, and some new ones, for two days. In March they gave him his clearance back, and he went back to his lathe, and nobody on Fairway Drive ever said anything about it, then or later, which was how things were done on the Hill. Mrs. Dorsey brought over a coffee cake. Esther's desk was moved back, for the light.

As for the girls, the people who told the story afterward could never quite say what had happened to them, only that it had. They were not saints. They grew up and argued with their mothers and went to dances in Santa Fe. But it was noticed that not one of them, ever after, could be got to say the word the masked children had clapped. Not as a joke. Not when their fathers said it at dinner. And it was noticed that when somebody on the Hill was having a bad time, a husband let go or a boy in trouble with the police, those were the girls who turned up at the door. They did not bring anything. They sat on the step and talked, and laughed, and went home.

On the eighteenth of February, 1957, the Atomic Energy Commission took down the gates. People turned out on the road in the snow to watch the governor drive through without a pass, and Esther Halpern, sixteen, stood among them in her school coat beside Patsy Delgado and Kay Lindgren. Afterward the guardhouse stood empty, and in time somebody opened it up as a drive-in and sold hamburgers out of the window where the guards had looked at your mother's face.

She kept the pillowcase. Her mother put it back on Esther's own pillow the week after Halloween and it stayed there until it wore through, and then her mother cut it into dust rags, and Esther, home from college, found the last square of it in the rag drawer under the sink and kept that. It was soft by then as a handkerchief, and it had never once, in all those years, had anything in it.

Background

The verse

Let us live happily then, though we call nothing our own! We shall be like the bright gods, feeding on happiness!Dhammapada 200, tr. F. Max Müller (1881)

In the commentary, the Buddha sees that five hundred young women of the brahmin village of Pañcasālā are ready to understand, and goes there for alms on a festival day. Māra, the tempter, takes possession of every villager, and the Buddha leaves with his bowl as clean as when it was washed. Māra waits at the village gate, asks whether he got nothing, and urges him to go round again, meaning to have the villagers clap and jeer in his face. Just then the young women, fresh from bathing and dressed for the festival, arrive at the gate and greet him, and Māra remarks that he must be suffering from hunger. The Buddha answers that though he received nothing, he will pass the day in joy, like the gods of Streaming Light who feed on rapture, and the women enter the stream. Read the full commentary story.

Esther's grandfather's saying is from the tractate Berakhot of the Babylonian Talmud, a saying of Rav about the world to come, in A. Cohen's 1921 translation.

Los Alamos, 1950

Los Alamos was built in secret on a mesa above the Rio Grande in 1943 for the Manhattan Project and stayed a fenced, guarded town for twelve years after the war. Under the Atomic Energy Commission, civilian guards replaced soldiers at the gates, outsiders came in only by invitation with a pass arranged in advance, and residents famously left their doors unlocked. The gates came down on February 18, 1957, when Governor Edwin Mechem drove through first without a pass, and the main guard station was later replaced for a time by a drive-in burger stand. The postwar Western Area included six experimental Lustron houses of porcelain-enameled steel, put up in 1949 on Fairway Drive and 44th Street.

The year 1950 brought the atomic spy cases home to the Hill. Klaus Fuchs, of the wartime British mission, was arrested in England in February; the courier Harry Gold confessed in May; David Greenglass, an Army machinist who had worked at Los Alamos from 1944 to 1946, was arraigned in June; and his brother-in-law Julius Rosenberg and sister Ethel were arrested in July and August (FBI history; the Rosenbergs). Clearances were reviewed across the weapons complex. It was also the year of Hopalong Cassidy, when more than a hundred companies made some $70 million worth of his merchandise. The Halperns, their street and their hearing are invented.
