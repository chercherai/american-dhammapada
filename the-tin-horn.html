Walking the American WayCommentary 20.5 · Padhānakammika Tissa

The Tin Horn

Shake off dull sloth, and joyful rise.
— Thomas Ken, “Awake, my soul, and with the sun”

As I heard it told, on the old meeting ground at Station Camp Creek.

I. Across the Creek

Sumner County, Tennessee · August 1802

Brother Josiah Oakes had come over the mountains from Botetourt County, Virginia, with an anvil in the bed of his wagon, and he had shod horses on Bledsoe’s Creek for twenty years and preached on Sundays for nothing. He was sixty-eight that summer. He had a voice that had been ruined by smoke and did not carry, so at the meetings he let the younger men do the hollering from the stand, and he kept a tin horn instead, a long one, made by his own hands, which he blew to call the camp to preaching and to wake it at daylight. People said you could hear it at Gallatin. People said that about a good many things.

The meeting at Station Camp Creek that year ran four days, the second week of August, in a beech flat cleared out of the cane, with a stand of poles and boards at one end and split-log seats in rows on the ground before it. Families came forty miles in wagons and pitched by the creek. At night they built fires on platforms of logs packed with earth, so the trees overhead stood lit from beneath like the pillars of a house, and the preaching and the singing and the crying went on until two or three in the morning.

On the first evening Brother Oakes gathered the young men who had come seeking, and there were forty-one of them, and he gave each one a single verse of Scripture, out loud, one at a time, a different verse for each, so that no two had the same. Then he sent them across the creek into the big woods on the far side.

Stay with that one, he told them. Don’t go looking for another. Stay with it till it opens.

They went over the creek on the stepping stones, forty-one of them, in the last of the light.

Absalom Kittrell came back the first night. He was nineteen, the tallest of them, broad as a door, with a white scar that ran through his left eyebrow and into the lid. He had sat under a beech with his verse until it got dark and the whippoorwills started, and then he thought that he could pray as well lying in his father’s wagon as sitting on a root, and better, and he waded back across in the dark and lay down in the wagon bed on the feed sacks and slept until the horn.

He meant to go back over in the morning. He meant to go back over at noon. He spent the second day helping his mother at the cook fire and the third watching the preaching, and he told himself he was getting religion as well where he was as anybody was getting it in the woods.

II. Coming Through

Station Camp Creek · the third evening

On the third evening, at about sundown, the singing started on the far side of the creek. It came out of the woods a long way off and got nearer, and the people at the stand stopped what they were doing and turned, and the forty came out of the trees and down to the water. They had not eaten in three days. Their shirts were torn by the cane and they had leaves in their hair, and they were singing a hymn that most of them could not have told you the words of the week before, and they came over the stepping stones singing it, and up through the camp to the stand.

Brother Oakes came down off the stand to meet them. He did not say anything at first. He went to each one, all down the line, and took him by both arms and looked into his face, and said his name, and the name of his verse, and then he held each one against his chest a moment, the way you would hold a son come back from the Indian country, and let him go and went on to the next. It took a long time. Nobody at the stand minded. Women were crying. A man from the Presbyterian side began to pray out loud and then stopped, because there was no call for it.

Absalom stood at the edge of the crowd by the seats. Brother Oakes came down the line and passed within an arm’s length of him on the way back up to the stand, and looked at him, and did not say anything to him at all.

On their way out of the woods the forty had come down past the spring on Nathan Hedge’s place, two miles below the meeting ground, and Hedge’s wife had come out with corn bread and buttermilk and stood at the spring while they ate. Hedge had asked them, every one, to come and take breakfast at his house in the morning before they scattered home. Come at sunup, he said. She’ll be killing chickens tonight. So after the preaching they told Brother Oakes they would be gone early, at first light, to Hedge’s, and would be back by the forenoon preaching.

Go on, then, the old man said. Eat. You’ve earned it.

III. The Night

The meeting ground · that night

Absalom lay in the wagon bed and could not sleep. Out there among the fires the forty were lying down where they could, wrapped in blankets, some of them still singing low. The old man had looked at every one of them and had not looked at him. He had held every one of them, and not him.

Then I’ll come through tonight, he thought. Tonight. And in the morning I’ll go up to him, and he’ll have to say my name.

He got out of the wagon. He did not go back over the creek, because it was dark and the creek was a long way and it seemed to him that it was not the woods that mattered. He walked the meeting ground instead. He walked up between the rows of seats to the stand and back down to the fires and up again, saying his verse over, and then saying everything he could think of, and asking, and then demanding, and then asking again. The exhorters gave out at two o’clock. The last of the singing stopped. The fires on the platforms burned down to beds of coals. He kept walking.

Toward morning he was so tired that he did not know which part of the ground he was on. He was walking with his eyes shut. He was asleep and walking. At the head of the rows, before the stand, the end of a split-log seat stuck out a foot past the others where somebody had cut it long, and he walked into it with his whole weight. It caught him above the knee and he went over it, and the bone of his thigh broke with a sound that people heard in their tents.

He screamed. He did not stop screaming.

The forty knew his voice, most of them; they had grown up with him. They came up out of their blankets and ran every way in the dark, falling over guy ropes and each other, until somebody got a pine knot lit at the coals and then another, and they found him lying in the leaves past the end of the seat with his leg bent where a leg does not bend. Two of them held his shoulders. One of the Hedge boys’ cousins, who had seen it done to a horse, said they would have to pull it straight, and they pulled it straight while he screamed into a folded blanket, and somebody pulled two rails off the fence by the wagons, and they bound the leg between the rails with strips torn from their own shirts, which were torn already. Then they sat around him in the leaves holding the pine knots, and held his hands, and wiped his face, and talked to him, and would not leave him.

The pine knots burned down and the sky over the creek went gray and then yellow, and the light came down through the beeches, and they were still sitting there with him when Brother Oakes came out of his tent with the tin horn in his hand and put it to his mouth and blew the day in.

It was full sunup. Two miles down the creek Nathan Hedge’s wife was taking chicken off the fire, and setting it at the back of the hearth to keep, and going to the door to look up the road.

IV. The Infare

The stand · the fourth morning

Brother Oakes came over and looked at the rails and the leg and the face of the boy, and knelt and put his hand on the boy’s forehead, which was wet and cold. Then he looked around at the forty, and at the sun.

You boys didn’t get to Hedge’s.

No, sir. They told him how it had happened.

He was quiet a while. Then he said, Well, that’s twice.

They waited, and he sat down on the end of the seat that had broken the boy’s leg, as if it were any seat, and told them.

Eight winters back, when I kept the school on Bledsoe’s Creek, I had a good many of you in it. You remember. In January I sent you all up the hollow for wood, and you went and cut and corded it like men, every one, but this one. This one found a big poplar that looked dead to him, and he thought, that’ll be dry, I’ll lie down and take my rest, and when they’re done I’ll climb up and break off what I need in a minute. And he spread his coat in the leaves and lay down and went to sleep and snored so you could hear him over the axes.

You found him on your way home, he said to the forty, and you kicked him awake, I heard about it, and you went on and left him. And he jumped up and went up that poplar half asleep, and it wasn’t dead. It was only bare. He took hold of a limb and pulled, and it was green and it bent and broke short and whipped back, and the end of it laid his eye open. And he came down with one hand over his eye and pulled green limbs off with the other, and tied them up, and came running down the hollow after you, and threw his green poplar on top of your good wood on the pile by my door.

Absalom, on the ground, put his hand up to the scar.

That was the night before the Gwin infare, the old man said. You recall. They had asked the whole school to the infare dinner, down at the Gwins’, eleven miles, and I told you we would have to start at daylight, and you could not walk eleven miles in January on nothing, so my wife would get up and make mush. And she got up in the dark and went to the pile for wood, and the top of the pile was green poplar, and she laid her fire with it. And she blew on that fire. Keziah blew on that fire till her face was red. And it smoked and it hissed and it would not take, and the sun came up over the ridge, and you all stood in my kitchen and looked at it, and it was too late to go.

That boy, he said, not unkindly, has kept you from your breakfast twice now.

Some of the forty laughed, and then looked at Absalom and stopped. But Absalom was laughing too, on his back in the leaves, with the tears running out of the corners of his eyes into his ears, and it hurt him to laugh, and he did it anyway.

Then the old man said the thing he said, and he said it to all of them, but he was looking at the boy.

There’s a time to rise, and it don’t wait. A young man, strong, that won’t get up when it’s time, and lies there, and puts it off, and then tries to do it all at once in the dark, he won’t ever find the way. Not in the woods and not on this ground. You can’t pray three days in one night any more than you can dry green wood by blowing on it.

He looked at his horn a while, turning it in his big burned hands. Then, because his voice would not carry, he did not sing it; he said it, the morning hymn his mother had taught him in Botetourt, the one he blew the horn for.

Awake, my soul, and with the sun
Thy daily stage of duty run;
Shake off dull sloth, and joyful rise
To pay thy morning sacrifice.

— Thomas Ken, “Awake, my soul, and with the sun,” stanza 1

Then he got up off the seat, and took the axe from beside the stand, and cut the long end of the log off flush with the others, so it would not catch anybody else in the dark.

V. Daylight

Station Camp Creek · the morning after

They kept Absalom on the ground one more night, in the wagon bed on a tick of cane leaves, because he could not be moved yet over the roads. The meeting broke up around him. The families struck their camps and went off home in their wagons, and the forty went off with them, every one stopping at the wagon to say something to him, and the fires on the platforms went out and were not built up again.

In the night he did not sleep much, because of the leg. He lay and looked up through the beeches and said his verse over, the one the old man had given him on the first evening, which he had hardly looked at in four days. He did not say anything else. He just stayed with it.

Before light he heard the old man come out of his tent, the only tent left standing on the ground, and cough, and walk down toward the stand in the dark. Absalom raised himself on his elbows in the wagon bed. Over the creek there was no color yet. Then there was a little, a gray, behind the trunks of the beeches on the far side, and then a little yellow, and he was watching it come.

When the horn blew, for nobody but the two of them and the horses, he was already awake.

Background

The verse

He who does not rouse himself when it is time to rise, who, though young and strong, is full of sloth, whose will and thought are weak, that lazy and idle man will never find the way to knowledge.Dhammapada 280, tr. F. Max Müller (1881)

In the commentary, five hundred young men of Sāvatthī are ordained, take a meditation subject from the Buddha and go into the forest. One gives up at once; the rest become arahants. On their way back a lay follower feeds them and invites them to a meal the next morning. The Buddha welcomes the returning monks warmly and says nothing to the one who quit, who resolves to become an arahant that very night so that the Buddha will speak to him. He walks up and down all night, falls asleep on his feet, trips over a stone seat at the end of the walkway and breaks his thigh, and the others spend the rest of the night tending him and miss the meal. The Buddha says the same monk once did this to them before, and tells the birth story of the Varuṇa tree (Jātaka 71): a lazy pupil sleeps through the wood-gathering, hurts his eye climbing a living tree he took for dead, and throws green wood on top of the others’ bundles, so that the next morning the maid cannot light the fire for their early gruel and they miss a feast in the village. Camp meetings, 1800–1802

The camp meeting came out of the Great Revival on the southern frontier. James McGready’s meeting at Gasper River in Logan County, Kentucky, in July 1800 is usually counted the first, and the Methodist John McGee held the first known Tennessee camp meeting that August at Drake’s Creek in Sumner County, as the Tennessee Encyclopedia recounts. The next August, at Cane Ridge in Bourbon County, Kentucky, somewhere between ten and thirty thousand people gathered. Families came by wagon and camped for several days, usually four, in late summer; at Cane Ridge Presbyterian, Methodist and Baptist preachers all took part, and observers wrote of people falling, shouting and jerking under the preaching. Methodists would go on holding camp meetings for decades after others gave them up.

The hymn Brother Oakes says is the morning hymn of Thomas Ken (1637–1711), the English bishop who wrote it for the scholars of Winchester College; its last verse is the doxology still sung everywhere as “Praise God, from whom all blessings flow.” The text here follows The Otterbein Hymnal (1890). An infare was the feast given at the groom’s family’s house after a wedding, an old custom of the upland South.
