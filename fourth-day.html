Walking the American WayCommentary 6.3 · Channa

Fourth Day

For from the good thou shalt learn good…
— Theognis 35

This I had by relation, at Enderby on the Merrimack.

I. The Wash-Way

Fen Enderby, Lincolnshire · October 1634

Mr. Josias Markby was the squire’s second son, and for nineteen years he had held the family living of Fen Enderby, in the parts of Holland. It was a fat one. It was worth a hundred and thirty pounds a year in tithe wool and tithe geese and the rent of the glebe marsh, and it came with a brick parsonage and an orchard walled against the east wind. He had a wife and a daughter in that house who loved him. John Chaney was his man. The two of them had been born in the one week in the one parish, the squire’s boy in the hall and the groom’s boy over the stable, and Chaney had saddled for him since they were both small.

In the autumn of 1634 the Archbishop’s visitors came through the fens asking which ministers wore the surplice, and which had read the King’s book of Sunday sports from the pulpit as they were ordered. Mr. Markby had done neither. He was cited, and did not go. In the second week of October a neighbor sent a boy over the dyke at dusk to say that a pursuivant of the High Commission was lying at an inn in Boston with a warrant in his coat.

He left that night and told nobody in the house. He came out to the stable in his gown, with a candle, and found Chaney awake already, because Chaney slept with one ear on the horses. The grey, John. And the cob for you. They rode south along the sea bank with the wind behind them, twenty-odd miles in the dark, and came at first light to the wash-way below Sutton, where the river runs out over the sands into the great bay, and where at dead ebb a man who has a guide may walk two miles across the bottom of the sea and come up in Norfolk.

The guide was there with his pole. The tide was out. Mr. Markby got down. He took off his gown and folded it, and took the seal ring from his finger and the keys of the church from his belt and laid them in the gown, and handed the bundle up to Chaney, and the grey’s rein after it.

Carry these to Mistress Markby. Tell her I am gone over, and will send.

I’ll come with you.

You will go back, John.

Chaney sat the cob and watched him walk out behind the guide, a man in a doublet with a stick, getting smaller on the wet sand, until either the light was too poor or he was too far. Then he turned the two horses and rode back into the wind the way he had come, and told the mistress standing in her kitchen. The grey would not feed after that week. It was dead before Martinmas.

Mr. Markby lay hid in London through the winter and took ship in the spring. His wife and the girl followed him in the summer of 1635, and Chaney came with them, with the gown in his own chest.

For the rest of his life, whenever the talk came anywhere near it, Chaney said the same thing. I rode with him that night. There was him and me and the horses. I have never looked to another man since, and I never will.

II. The Word by Itself

Enderby on the Merrimack · 1638 to 1648

They called the plantation Enderby, since most of them had come out of the fens together, and there was salt marsh enough at the river’s mouth to make them feel they had not come so very far. Thirty-odd families gathered a church there, with Mr. Markby for its pastor, and built a meetinghouse of sawn boards with a thatched roof and no bell. Chaney beat the drum for meeting. He beat the first drum at eight and the second at nine, every Lord’s day and every lecture day, and it was understood in the town that nobody else would be asked.

On the fourth day of each week Mr. Markby read Greek in his study with such boys of the town as were meant for the College at Cambridge and the ministry after it. He had one grammar and a thick little book of the lesser Greek poets, printed at the other Cambridge the year after he went over the sands and sent out to him by a friend. Its first leaf had carried a dedication to the Archbishop. He had cut that out and lit the fire with it. The boys stood in a row and told over their paradigms. Tupto, I strike. Tupteis, thou strikest. Tuptei, he striketh. Chaney carried in the wood and then sat down beside it on the floor inside the door, mending harness, since on a weekday morning the study had the only fire in the house, and nobody thought anything of it. He had no letters, English or any other.

First the etymologie, Mr. Markby told every new boy, which is the word by itself, in all its changes. Then the syntax, which is the word in company. A good many men get the first and die without the second. Once, to make them laugh, he told them that Goodman Chaney’s drum was Greek too, tympanon, the thing struck, and came out of the same tupto they were yawning over.

In 1638 the church called a second minister to be its Teacher. This was Mr. Thomas Sarson, lately of Emmanuel College, who was thirty years old and had the Hebrew as well as the Greek. For Ruling Elder it chose William Moulton, a cooper, a large, slow, careful man who had come over in 1637 and whom the whole town trusted with its quarrels. Mr. Markby leaned on the two of them more every year. He was past sixty. When he was asked a hard thing he had begun to say, Put it to Mr. Sarson, or, What does the Elder think?

Chaney could not bear it.

He said so at the well and at the mill and on the meetinghouse step with the drum still on his shoulder. Where were they the night he went over the sands? One was at his book and the other at his barrels. I was there, I and the horses. Now it is I am the Teacher, and I am the Ruling Elder, and a man must go through the pair of them like two gates to come at his own master. He mocked the way Mr. Sarson held up one finger when he preached. He said in the hearing of children that the Elder counted the hoops on every word before he let it out.

It was carried to Mr. Markby, who sent for him. John, they are good men, and they have done you no wrong. Leave it. Chaney stood and looked at the floor and said nothing for about as long as it takes to say the Lord’s Prayer. Then he went out to the woodpile, and the boy who was splitting there heard the whole of it over again.

He was sent for a second time, and it went the same way.

The third time Mr. Markby did not see him alone. It was a fourth day. The boys were there, Mr. Sarson and the Elder had come in about a boundary, and Chaney was on the floor by the wood with a breast-strap across his knees. The old man had the book of poets open at Theognis of Megara, whom the boys found easy. He turned in his chair.

John Chaney. In these two you have friends who are good men, the best you are like to meet with on this side of the grave. Make friends of such men, and follow such, and no others. Then he put it into English for him off the page, slowly, as he did for the boys.

…consort not with bad men, but ever cleave to the good: with them eat and drink, sit with them, and please them, of whom there is a large force. For from the good thou shalt learn good, but with the bad if thou shouldst mix, thou wilt lose even the mind thou hast.

— Theognis 31–36

Two of the boys who stood in the row that morning said in later life that they had got more good of those six lines than of all the rest of their Greek. Chaney got up, made his duty to the three of them, and carried out the ash bucket. Before noon he was heard at the well on the subject of gates.

When the brethren came to complain of it again, Mr. Markby heard them through. You will not teach John Chaney while I am alive, he said. After I am dead you will.

III. The Second Drum

Enderby · January to June 1649

He took to his bed at the turn of the year, in a hard frost. Stephen Anderby, a young man of the town who had been two years at the College and come home when his father died, sat up with him at night. Near the end Anderby asked him what the church was to do about Goodman Chaney.

Lay the heaviest thing on him.

Cast him out, sir?

No. Keep him in. Let him say whatever he will. Let no one answer him, nor reprove him, nor teach him, nor bid him good day, until he is another man. Tell Mr. Sarson I ask it.

He died on the eleventh of January. They kept a fire burning on the ground a day and a night before it could be opened. Chaney walked behind the bier at the widow’s elbow, as near as a man could get.

On the next Lord’s day, when the afternoon exercise was over, Mr. Sarson stayed the church. He had Anderby stand and say what he had been told, in the dead man’s words. Then he took up his copy of the Platform of discipline that the Synod at Cambridge had finished the summer before, and read from its chapter of censures the place that came nearest.

While the offender remains excommunicate, the church is to refrain from all member-like communion with him in spiritual things, and also from all familiar communion with him in civil things, further than the necessity of natural, or domestical, or civil relations do require; and are therefore to forbear to eat and drink with him, that he may be ashamed.

— Cambridge Platform XIV.5

Our pastor asked less than this, and more, Mr. Sarson said. John Chaney is not put out. He may sit where he sits. But from this hour no brother or sister of this church is to answer him. He called for hands, and the hands went up.

Chaney had stood at his own name. He stood a moment longer with his mouth working. Then his knees went, and he came down his whole length in the alley between the benches and lay there. When he could speak he spoke to the Elder, who was nearest. Do not undo me, sir. Three times he let me go free. Three times. The Elder looked at Mr. Sarson. Neither of them answered him, and that was the beginning of it.

He beat the first drum and the second drum the next Lord’s day as he always had, and the town came past him into the meetinghouse. For some weeks he went on talking at the well and the mill, of the two gates at first and then of other things, the frost, a lame ox, the price of nails, and the women drew their water and went home. Mistress Markby, whose servant he still was, set his meat on a bench in the lean-to and ate her own at the table. By March he had left off talking. He cut the wood for three houses and swept the meetinghouse and kept the drumheads from the damp, and he was on the step with the sticks in his hands at eight and again at nine.

The Greek went on, on fourth days, in the same room, under Mr. Sarson now. Nobody had told Chaney he was not to carry in the wood and sit by it, and he did.

On a fourth day in June the boys were in the passive voice, where boys always stuck. Tuptomai, I am struck. Tuptē, thou art struck. The Hale boy stopped there and stood with his mouth open. Mr. Sarson waited.

Tuptetai, said Chaney from the floor.

Nobody turned round. He went on in the same low voice, with his eyes on the strap in his lap, the way a man tells over his own cattle at the gate. Tuptometha. Tuptesthe. Tuptontai. Then he said, in English, to no one, Fifteen year I sat by it and thought it was for boys.

That is right, John, said Mr. Sarson, before he had thought.

The boys looked at Mr. Sarson, and Mr. Sarson looked at the book. He did not take it back. Go on from there, Hale, he said, and the boy went on.

The church book of Enderby shows no vote that took the censure off. On the next Lord’s day the Elder, going in at the second drum, said Good morrow, John, and those who came in behind him said it too. After the sermon Chaney was asked, as the order was, whether he had anything to say to the church. He stood up. They were the best men I ever met with, and I would not see it. Then he sat down again.

On the fourth day following there was a stool by the wood inside the study door. Nobody owned to having set it there. He sat on it.

Background

The verse

Do not have evil-doers for friends, do not have low people for friends: have virtuous people for friends, have for friends the best of men.Dhammapada 78, tr. F. Max Müller (1881)

In the commentary, Channa is the groom who rode out with Prince Siddhattha on the night he left the palace, and who carried the horse and the ornaments home from the river. Later, as a monk, he could not forgive the two chief disciples, Sāriputta and Moggallāna, for standing nearer the Buddha than he did, and he abused them. Three times he was admonished; each time he was silent a moment and then went on as before. The Buddha said that Channa could not be taught while he lived, and on his deathbed told Ānanda to lay on him the “supreme penalty”: he might say what he liked, and no monk was to speak to him, exhort him or correct him. When the sentence was read Channa fell down and begged not to be ruined. He went off, did his work, and became an arahat, at which the penalty lapsed of itself. Massachusetts Bay, 1634 to 1649

William Laud became Archbishop of Canterbury in 1633. That October the King’s Declaration of Sports was reissued, to be read from every pulpit, and from 1634 Laud’s vicar-general carried a visitation through the province. Ministers who would not conform were cited before the Court of High Commission, whose warrants were served by officers called pursuivants, and a good many slipped away at night and joined the migration to New England, which carried some twenty thousand people over between 1620 and 1640. The sands of the Wash could be crossed on foot at low water with a guide; William Camden wrote that when the tide was out “a man may pass over it as on dry land, but yet not without danger.” Fen Enderby, Enderby on the Merrimack and everyone in them are invented.

A New England church of the first generation had, where it could afford them, a pastor and a teacher, and a lay ruling elder beside them, and a town without a bell was called to meeting by a drum. The synod that met at Cambridge from 1646 to 1648 wrote the churches’ practice down as the Cambridge Platform; its fourteenth chapter, on censures, is quoted here from the Boston edition of 1855. The College at Cambridge was founded in 1636 so as not, in the words of New England’s First Fruits (1643), to leave “an illiterate ministry to the churches,” and the timetable printed in that pamphlet gives the fourth day of the week to Greek, with “Etymologie and Syntax” for the first-year men. Etymology then meant the forms of the single word. The verb the boys decline, typtō, “I strike,” was the stock paradigm of the old grammars, and the Greek word for a drum does come from it. The book of poets is Ralph Winterton’s Poetae minores Graeci (Cambridge, 1635), dedicated to Laud, which includes the maxims of Theognis. The English is the prose version of the Rev. J. Banks (1856).
