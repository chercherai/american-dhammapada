Walking the American WayCommentary 26.15 · Two Brahmins

Tow Strap

Unclamp, in a word, your intellectual and practical machinery, and let it run free.
— William James, “The Gospel of Relaxation” (1899)

This is the way we heard it, on the association beach at Lake Ellery.

I. Entry

Lake Ellery, Commerce Township, Oakland County, Michigan · Monday, June 16, 1986, 6:48 a.m.

Adult Beginners, Session One. Skill: enter the water safely and stand.

There were eleven of us, and none of us could swim, and all of us lived within a mile of the water. That was the thing nobody on the lake knew, or if they knew they were nice about it. We had moved out from Royal Oak and Livonia and Redford and the east side, some of us in 1971 and some last fall, for the schools and for the lake, and we had docks, and pontoon boats some of us, and children who swam like otters, and for years we had sat on the end of the dock in a folding chair with our feet out of the water and said we did not care for it much.

The Lake Ellery Property Owners Association put a notice in the spring newsletter, between the reminder about lawn fertilizer and the minutes of the April meeting, that Mrs. Wachowski would give a two-week course for adult beginners at the association beach, seven to eight in the morning, Red Cross method, free to members, and that the class would be confidential. Somebody on the board had underlined that word. Eleven of us called her. Two of us called her twice, to back out, and she said she would see us Monday.

So that Monday we came down the path from Heron Point Drive at a quarter to seven, one at a time, in robes and in T-shirts over our suits, trying not to see each other, and found each other anyway. Carol Dempsey, who taught third grade in the Walled Lake schools. Ray Fenner, a quality supervisor at Pontiac Motor, sixty-one, who had been in the Navy in Korea and had never told anybody on the ship. The two Hallorans, Pat and Marie, who were doing it together because they had a grandson coming in August. A dental hygienist named Linda. A Lutheran pastor’s wife. A man who sold insurance and a man who sold boats, which was the funniest thing about the eleven of us and the thing none of us mentioned. Janet Lusk, from the yellow colonial on the point, who had watched a cousin go under at Kensington when she was nine. Two others. And me, or we, whichever of us is telling it; we decided later we would tell it together, since we were all there.

The association beach is forty yards of trucked-in sand between the boat launch and the swimming dock, with a lifeguard chair nobody sits in before Memorial Day and a split-rail fence around it. On the Friday before, the association’s contractor had dumped twenty yards of new beach sand in a pile in the parking lot for the volunteers to spread, the way they did every June, and nobody had spread it yet.

Gary Lusk and Dennis Sorensen were standing on top of the pile.

II. Bobbing

6:52 a.m.

Skill: hold the breath and put the face in the water. Come up. Do it again.

Everybody on the lake knew about Gary and Dennis. They were both engineers at Ford, both in Truck Operations at Dearborn, both forty-four, both from Livonia, both on the association board, and they lived next door to each other on the point, and for two years they had been arguing about trucks.

Gary had been on the full-size Bronco since 1979, the big one, built on the F-150 frame at the Michigan Truck Plant in Wayne, with the 351 V-8. Dennis had been on the Bronco II since before it had a name, the little one off the Ranger, built down at Louisville, with the new fuel-injected 2.9 V-6 that came in this year. Each of them drove the one he worked on. Both trucks were tan, two-tone, tan over darker tan, and they sat side by side in the two driveways on the point like a cow and her calf, and the children on the lake called them Big Tan and Little Tan, and so did everybody else eventually, and so, by the end, did Gary and Dennis.

The argument had started at the Fourth of July picnic in 1984 over which one would pull a pontoon trailer up the launch, and it had gone on through two Christmases and an Easter brunch and the April board meeting, where it had to be ruled out of order. Dennis said weight was nothing, it was gearing and traction, and a short wheelbase on soft ground was worth a hundred pounds of torque. Gary said a V-8 was a V-8. Their wives had stopped going to things together. Janet Lusk had told one of us in March that she lay awake some nights listening to the two of them at it across the side yards, low, in the dark, like dogs at a fence.

That morning, it turned out, they had met at the pile by accident, each of them come down early to start spreading sand before work as a favor to the board, each with his own truck and his own utility trailer and his own shovel, and each had looked at the other and seen what was going to happen.

All right, Gary said. We heard him from the dock. All right. We’ll settle it.

They took Gary’s trailer, an eight-foot steel one, and backed it down the launch ramp and off the side of the concrete into the soft sand at the edge of the water, where the beach goes wet and dark. Then they shoveled it full from the pile, both of them, in their work slacks and good shirts, without a word, a wheelbarrow at a time, until the springs were flat and the tires had gone into the wet sand to the rims. Then they unhitched it. The idea, as near as we could tell, was that whoever could pull it out of there and up onto the ramp would win.

We were in the water by then, the eleven of us, standing in a row up to our waists along the swimming-dock rope, holding the rope with both hands. Mrs. Wachowski had told us on the phone to get in and get used to it if we came early. Nobody had told us to put our faces in. We stood there holding the rope with our chins up and watched.

Dennis went first. He hooked a nylon tow strap from the Bronco II’s hitch to the trailer tongue, a yellow one, two inches wide, still in its plastic bag from Kmart. He got in and put it in four-wheel low and took up the slack and gave it gas. The little truck dug. All four wheels threw sand. The trailer did not move one inch. He gave it more, and the engine went up high and thin, and the tires went down into the beach, and Gary stood with his arms crossed, and then the strap went.

It went with a crack like a .22, and the free end came back and hit the Bronco II’s tailgate and left a stripe in the tan paint you could still see in September.

Gary laughed. That was the worst thing that happened that morning, maybe. It was not a nice laugh. Dennis got out and looked at his tailgate and did not say anything.

Then Gary hooked up Big Tan, with his own strap, a heavier one, red, and a pair of shackles. He did not go easy. He backed up to get a run at it, and hit the end of the strap at maybe ten miles an hour, and the big truck jerked and the trailer jerked and rocked and settled back down into the wet sand deeper than before, and Gary backed up and hit it again, and again, and on the fourth time the hitch ball on the trailer tongue tore loose of its nut and the strap and the shackle came whipping back over the sand and took out a post of the split-rail fence.

Then both of them were standing in the sand with nothing in their hands, yelling.

We will not put down what they said. Some of it was about trucks and some of it was about Ford, which they both loved, and some of it was not about either, it was about the side yard and the property line and something at a Christmas party, and some of it was about a man’s wife, and Janet Lusk was standing in the lake beside us holding the rope.

The trailer sat where it was with its wheels in the beach and its load of sand on it, exactly where they had put it, and had never moved.

III. Front Float

7:01 a.m.

Skill: take a breath, put the face in, let the legs come up. Five seconds. Recover to a stand.

Mrs. Wachowski came down the path at seven exactly, as she always would, every morning of those two weeks, with a canvas bag over her shoulder and a whistle on a shoelace around her neck. She was sixty-eight. She had taught girls’ physical education at Pontiac Central for thirty-four years and given Red Cross swimming lessons every summer since 1946, at Elizabeth Lake and Cass Lake and Sylvan and the Pontiac Y, and she had taught, she said once, something over four thousand people to float, and she was proudest of the ones who were over forty.

By then Gary and Dennis had both driven off to Dearborn, separately, and the beach was empty except for the trailer and the strap and the post lying on its side.

She looked at all of it. She looked at us in our row holding the rope. She put her bag down on the lifeguard chair and took off her sandals and walked into the water in her suit to her waist, and stood in front of us.

Well, she said. What happened here?

So we told her. All of us at once, and then one at a time. Big Tan and Little Tan. The two straps. The hitch ball. The fence. Somebody started to say what they had yelled, and she held up her hand.

She did not say anything for a while. She looked at the trailer.

Those are tow straps, she said. Anybody can break a tow strap. You saw two men do it before seven o’clock in the morning without even trying. That’s not what you came down here for. She put her hand flat on the water in front of her and pressed down on it, a little. You came down here to break the other kind. The one that’s been holding you up on the end of the dock for forty years. The one that wants to win, and the one that’s scared. It’s the same strap, mostly. Those two this morning had the first kind wound so tight around them they couldn’t move a trailer of sand.

Then she went back to the lifeguard chair and got a paperback out of her canvas bag, an old clothbound copy with a cracked spine and a coffee ring on the cover, and came back into the water with it held up out of the wet, and read to us. She said it was William James, the psychologist, who gave these talks to schoolteachers in Cambridge, Massachusetts, in the eighteen-nineties, and that somebody had given her the book in 1952 at a phys-ed conference in Lansing and she had been reading it to beginners ever since, because a man who could write that well about not trying so hard was worth listening to.

If we only check a cowardly impulse in time, for example, or if we only don’t strike the blow or rip out with the complaining or insulting word that we shall regret as long as we live, our feelings themselves will presently be the calmer and better, with no particular guidance from us on their own account.

— William James, “The Gospel of Relaxation,” Talks to Teachers on Psychology (1899)

Now, she said, and put the book back on the chair. Breathe in. Faces in. I’ve got you.

IV. Back Float

7:20 a.m.

Skill: lie back on the water with the ears under. Arms out. Let it hold you.

It took the whole hour, and we did not all get it that first day. But we will say what happened, because it is the reason we tell this.

She took us one at a time, with her hand flat under the small of the back. Lie back. Ears in. Look at the sky. She said the same thing to every one of us, in the same voice, not loud. She said the water would hold us up if we would let it, and that the only thing in the lake that could sink us was the part of us that was holding on. She said a line from the book without looking at it, as if it were a line from a song she had sung so often it had worn smooth, that a person who never gives himself up wholly to the chair he sits in, but keeps his muscles half contracted for a rise, cannot be anything but anxious. Give yourself up to the chair, she said. This is the chair. And she took her hand away.

Ray Fenner went first. He lay back stiff as a plank and sank and came up coughing and lay back again, and the second time something went out of his shoulders and he floated, sixty-one years old, with his eyes wide open, looking at the sky over Commerce Township, and he started to laugh, and the laughing did not sink him.

Then the Hallorans, together, holding hands, which she allowed. Then Carol. Then the boat salesman. Then the pastor’s wife, who prayed out loud the first time and not the second. One after another, along the rope, the eleven of us lay back on Lake Ellery, and it held us.

Janet Lusk was last. She stood a long while with her hands on the rope, looking at the trailer up on the beach with her husband’s red strap still shackled to it, and at the post on its side, and Mrs. Wachowski waited and did not hurry her and did not say anything at all. Then Janet let go of the rope. She lay back. Her hair went out around her head on the water. Mrs. Wachowski took her hand away, and Janet floated, and her face did something none of us had seen it do in two years on the point, and she did not cry, but it was close.

We lay there, all of us, with our ears under, so that the whole world was the sound of our own breathing and the far small knock of somebody’s outboard on the other side of the lake, and the sky over us was white and then blue, and none of us was holding on to anything.

•••

That evening, about six, some of us were out on our docks and saw Gary and Dennis come down to the association beach, separately, a few minutes apart, each in old clothes with a shovel. They did not say anything we could hear. They shoveled the sand off the trailer and spread it on the beach, a wheelbarrow at a time, both of them, until the trailer was empty and the beach was new, and then they each took a side of the tongue and walked it up out of the wet sand onto the ramp by hand, which it turns out you can do with a trailer if there is nothing in it, and Dennis held the light while Gary put the ball back on. Then Gary picked up the post and set it back in its hole and tamped it with his heel, and they went home up the path to the point, one ahead and one behind.

The yellow strap lay in the sand by the lifeguard chair in two pieces all that summer. Nobody threw it away. By August the frayed ends had gone white in the sun, and the children used one piece to mark the finish line for races in the shallows, and the other piece somebody tied to the swimming-dock rope, about where the eleven of us had stood, and it floated there on the water every morning at seven, and it held nothing, and the waves lifted it and set it down.

Background

The verse

Him I call indeed a Brahmana who has cut the strap and the thong, the chain with all that pertains to it, who has burst the bar, and is awakened.Dhammapada 398, tr. F. Max Müller (1881)

Two brahmins, one with an ox called Little Tawny and the other with an ox called Big Tawny, argue over whose ox is the stronger. Tired of arguing, they load a cart with sand on the bank of the Aciravatī river and yoke the oxen to it. Some monks come down to bathe and watch: the brahmins whip the oxen, the cart does not stir an inch, and the straps and thongs break. The monks tell the Buddha, who says that anyone can cut those outward straps; a monk must cut the inner strap of anger and the thong of craving. Hearing the verse, the five hundred monks are freed. Oakland County, 1986

Oakland County, north and west of Detroit, is dotted with hundreds of small glacial lakes, and after the Second World War the townships around them filled with subdivisions of commuters to the auto companies, many of them organized into lake and property-owner associations with private beaches. In 1986 Ford built two Broncos. The full-size Bronco, in its third generation (1980–1986) based on the F-150, came from the Michigan Truck Plant in Wayne, Michigan, and could be had with a 5.8-liter V-8. The compact Bronco II, derived from the Ranger pickup and built alongside it at the Louisville Assembly Plant in Kentucky, was sold from the 1984 model year; for 1986 it got a fuel-injected 2.9-liter V-6.

William James delivered his Talks to Teachers on Psychology to schoolteachers in Cambridge and elsewhere in the 1890s and published them in 1899 with three talks to students, among them “The Gospel of Relaxation.” There he argues that American “over-tension” is a habit, that feeling follows action as much as action follows feeling, and that a person who never gives up wholly to the chair they sit in cannot be at rest. The American Red Cross, which hired Wilbert E. Longfellow in 1914 to organize its volunteer life-saving corps and went on to build a national water-safety program, taught most of the country’s twentieth-century learn-to-swim classes, adults among them, through local volunteer instructors like Mrs. Wachowski.
