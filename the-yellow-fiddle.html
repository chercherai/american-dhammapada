I. The Warm Rock

Sylamore Creek, Stone County, Arkansas · 1946

The monks came over from Kentucky in April of 1946, twenty-two of them in two borrowed trucks, to a worn-out hill farm on Sylamore Creek that a widow in Little Rock had left to the Church. The abbey in Kentucky was full past bursting with men home from the war, and the abbot was sending them out wherever somebody gave him land. There was a dogtrot house on the place, and a barn with a tin roof, and a spring, and about forty acres that would grow anything but money. They put up wallboard cells four feet wide in the hayloft and slept there in their habits on straw ticks. They rose at two in the morning for the Night Office. They did not speak.

Brother Isidore had been born four miles up the same creek in 1884, as Lafe Sutterfield. He had farmed, and fiddled some when he was young, and buried a wife, and in 1926 he had gone to Kentucky and asked to be let in as a lay brother, and they had let him. Twenty years he had worn the brown habit and the beard and milked other people's cows. When the abbot looked for a man who knew the Arkansas hills, Isidore was the one, and so he came home. At the new place he was the cowman, because he knew cows, and the infirmarian, because there was nobody else.

Brother Paschal was the youngest of the choir novices, in white. His name in the world had been Francis Kemper. His father was a surgeon on Lafayette Square in St. Louis, and the Jesuits had taught him, and he had gone from Saint Louis University into the Marines and landed on Guadalcanal in August of 1942 with the First Division. He came home the next year weighing a hundred and eighteen pounds, with the malaria in his blood. He was twenty-six. He had a long, quiet face and very good manners, and the lay brothers liked him, which the lay brothers did not always do with the young men from the cities.

In the afternoon work that first summer he was sent to cut cedar off the bluff pasture above the creek. When the bell rang for the break, the others sat down in the shade. Paschal went out on a shelf of grey limestone at the edge of the bluff, where the sun lay all day, and sat with his hands in his sleeves, and went still.

Isidore saw it once from the fence row. He had seen men pray all his life, at brush arbors and at the abbey, men with their lips going and men with their eyes squeezed shut. This was not like that. It was like the boy had gone down a well and pulled the lid over. The fence lizards that lived in the cracks of the bluff came out one at a time onto the warm rock around him, the way they will when nothing is moving, and one of them climbed his sleeve and sat on the back of his hand with its sides going in and out, and he did not stir, and it did not leave.

After that the lay brothers had a sign for him among themselves, a flat hand with the fingers flickering. The Lizard. They did not mean any harm by it.

II. Chills and Fever

The infirmary · June 1946 to March 1947

The first time it came back on him was in June. Isidore found him at Vespers holding the edge of the choir stall with both hands, his teeth going like a sewing machine, and walked him across the yard to the dogtrot house, where the infirmary was two rooms and a washstand. Isidore knew the look of it. Every child on Sylamore Creek in his day had had the chills and fever, every other summer, and his own mother had kept a quinine bottle on the shelf by the salt. First the shaking, so hard the cot walked on the floor. Then the fever, a hundred and four, a hundred and five, and the boy talking to somebody named Gunny who was not there. Then the sweat, and the sheets wringing wet, and then nothing, a day or two of weakness like a wrung rag, and then it came again.

The doctor from Mountain View came out in his Ford and gave atabrine, which turned Paschal's skin yellow as a squash. In two weeks he was back in choir. In three he was back on the rock.

But it was not the same, and he told Isidore so, because the infirmarian could speak to the sick and the sick could answer him. Isidore was changing the bed.

It goes, Brother. When the fever comes, it goes. I sit out there and it isn't there.

It'll come back.

It did come back. It took a month.

The second time was in August, and the third at the end of September, when the sumac was red on the bluffs. Each time Isidore sat up with him, and each time it went the same way: the shaking, the fever, the sweat, the weakness, the slow walk back to choir, and then a long while on the rock waiting, like a man at a spring that has stopped, and then one afternoon the lizards coming out again. The fourth time was at Christmas. The fifth was in January, in the snow. By then the doctor had stopped saying the boy would grow out of it. Some of them did, he said, and some of them had it ten and twelve times. There was nothing in his bag to stop it coming back.

After the fifth time Paschal lay in the infirmary looking at the ceiling for most of a week, and one night when the lamp was down he said, What happens to a man who keeps falling back? Six times, seven times. You'd think God would get tired of fetching him.

Isidore was sitting in the chair by the stove. He had been asleep, or nearly.

That's the fever talking, he said. I've had it. It lies to you. It told me once my wife was in the yard calling me.

I'm a burden on this house.

You're the least burden in it. You ought to see Brother Alberic eat.

Paschal did not laugh, and Isidore did not like that he did not. The next morning he went to Father Prior, which a lay brother did not do lightly, and stood in the door of the little office with his cap in his hands and said what he had heard. The prior was a big, slow Belgian who had been at the Kentucky house since before the first war. He listened, and wrote to the doctor, and wrote to the boy's mother in St. Louis, and moved Paschal out of the hayloft into the room off the infirmary, where Isidore could hear him in the night through the board wall. Isidore put his own straw tick on the floor across the door of the next room. For three weeks he did not sleep more than a few hours together, and he did not mind. He had sat up with cows.

The sixth time was in February. When it was over, Paschal went back out to the rock in his coat, in the cold, and sat a long time, and came back and did not say anything. Isidore watched his face and did not like what he saw, and could not have said what it was.

Then the seventh time came, in March, and it was short and light, and when it had passed Paschal got up and ate his bread and went out to the bluff in the first warm sun of the year, and came back with his face changed.

It came, he told Isidore. Right away this time. It was all there.

Isidore was glad, and believed him, and that night for the first time in weeks he went back up to the brothers' dormitory and slept the whole way to the bell.

III. The Barn

Before day · March 1947

The lay brothers milked at half past three, after their own prayers, while the choir monks were still in the Night Office. It was raining. Isidore had the lantern hung on the nail and his head in the flank of a red cow when the cows down the line all stood up at once in their stanchions and turned their heads the other way.

There was a man in the barn door. Isidore had not heard a car on the road or a step in the yard. The man wore a good grey overcoat and a hat, and the rain had not touched either one, and the barn cat had gone flat to the floor under the feed box and stayed there.

You're the old man that sits up with him, the man said. The Marine. Kemper. He means to die this morning. You'd better go and stop him. He won't listen to a word from me.

He said it in a reasonable voice, the way a neighbor tells you your gate is open. Isidore had grown up on this creek, where people knew certain things and did not say them to outsiders, and he looked at the man's dry hat and the cows' turned heads and he knew who was standing in the door.

You don't care one way or another if he lives, Isidore said. You want to know where he goes.

The man smiled at him.

He's not yours, Isidore said, and knocked over the milk getting up, and went past him out into the rain.

He ran. He was sixty-two years old and had not run since the year his wife died. He crossed the yard in the dark with his habit gathered up in his fists, went through the dogtrot to the infirmary door, and it was already over. Brother Paschal had taken his own life.

Isidore knelt down on the floor. He said the prayers for the dying, though it was past the time for them, because he could not think what else to say, and he said them all the way through. Then he went and rang the bell at the wrong hour, and kept ringing it, until the prior came across the yard with the oils.

•••

In those years the law of the Church said that a man who killed himself deliberately was not to have Christian burial. The prior sat a long time in his office with the book. Then he came out and said, by signs and then in words, so that there would be no mistake, that Brother Paschal had been very sick for a long while and had not been himself, and that he would be buried with his brothers.

They buried him on the slope above the creek the next afternoon, the way they bury their own, in his white cowl, with no coffin, his hood drawn over his face, the brothers taking turns with the shovels. His mother came down from St. Louis on the train to Batesville and a neighbor drove her out in a truck. She was a small woman in a black coat, and she stood at the grave without crying and then afterward could not stop. Isidore was not allowed to speak to her, and he did anyway, out by the truck.

He was sick, ma'am. It was the sickness. He was as good a boy as I ever knew.

She held on to his sleeve. Did he say anything?

Isidore told her the truth, which was that he had been asleep upstairs, and had thought the boy was better. She did not let go of his sleeve for a long time.

IV. The Smoke

The week after

Bud Treat, hauling cedar posts on the Mountain View road on the Monday, saw a smoke go up the Sylamore valley against the wind with no fire under it. He told it at the store. It was a dark smoke, he said, like a brush pile of green cedar, and it went along the bluffs low and slow, and into the mouths of the little hollows one after another, and came back out of each one, and went on. Mrs. Lackey saw it go along her fence row and over her spring box and come back. Two of the novices saw it from the woodpile on the Tuesday. It went down to the creek and along the gravel bar and turned in the water like a dog casting for a scent, and came up the slope through the burying ground and stood a while there over the new dirt, and went on up into the cedars on the bluff.

Isidore was splitting kindling. The novices did not speak, but one of them caught his sleeve and pointed.

He stood and watched it a long time with the hatchet in his hand. It went out along the limestone shelf at the top of the bluff where the sun lay, and turned there, this way and that, and went down into the cracks, and came up.

He was allowed to speak to the novices for their instruction, and he decided this was instruction.

That's the one that came to the barn, he said. He's hunting.

Hunting what? said the younger one, forgetting.

Brother Paschal. Where he's gone. He's been through the burying ground and the creek and Mrs. Lackey's springhouse, and now he's up on the rock.

The smoke went down into the hayloft through the gap under the eaves, where the cells were, and came out the other end, and went on east.

Will he find him?

No, Isidore said, and went back to the kindling.

V. The Fiddle

The county road · Easter week

In Easter week Isidore was setting a post where the abbey lane meets the county road, because the cows had been out. It was a fine afternoon with the redbud out along the ridges. He heard the fiddle a good while before he saw the man.

He knew most of the tunes in Stone County and had played half of them, before he went to Kentucky, at dances his mother prayed against, and he did not know this one. It was quick and sweet and it went down into the low notes like water going over a lip of rock, and the cows in the near pasture all lifted their heads. Then the man came walking up the county road playing it. He was young, and handsome as a picture, in a suit the color of new butter, and the fiddle under his chin was yellow. Not varnished yellow. The wood itself was yellow all through, the bright hard yellow of hedge apple, bois d'arc, which the old people said was too hard to make anything of but fence posts and bows.

He stopped across the post hole from Isidore and took the fiddle down from his chin. His face had gone sad and puzzled, like a child's that has lost something in the grass.

Old man, he said. I've been all over. I've been up every hollow on this creek and down the White River to Batesville, and over the ridges, and up the sky as far as a man can get and down the other way as far as it goes. I've been in the graveyard and in the creek and in the loft and in the rock. I looked everywhere he ever set his foot and everywhere he didn't. Where has he gone?

Isidore leaned on the post-hole digger. He said it the way the Catholic book has it, though he had heard it first another way, as a boy, at a brush arbor on Turkey Creek.

Whither shall I go from thy spirit? or whither shall I flee from thy face? If I ascend into heaven, thou art there: if I descend into hell, thou art present. If I take my wings early in the morning, and dwell in the uttermost parts of the sea: Even there also shall thy hand lead me: and thy right hand shall hold me.

— Psalm 138:7–10 (Douay-Rheims)

Everyplace you looked, Isidore said, somebody was there before you.

The young man stood there in the road. His face went slack, the way a face does when there is no more use in trying. His arms came down, and the fiddle slid out of them into the grass by the ditch, and the bow after it.

Isidore bent to pick them up, because he could not stand to see a fiddle lie in the wet. When he straightened up the road was empty both ways, as far as the bend, and there was no dust on it.

What he had in his hands was a split limb of hedge apple about as long as his arm, and a switch, and that was all. He used the limb to prop the gate.

VI. The Lantern

Sylamore Creek · 1947 and after

That evening, after Compline, the two novices who had seen the smoke came and stood by the woodpile where Isidore was sitting, and waited with their hands in their sleeves. He knew what they wanted. They wanted to know what he knew, and he did not know very much. He knew that the boy had been sick and had suffered, and that the sickness had told him lies, and that the old man had not been awake when he was needed, and would carry that. He knew who had come to the barn door, and he knew that a hundred like him could hunt this creek until the Judgment and not find what they were looking for. He said it to them as Saint Paul says it, because Saint Paul says it better.

For I am sure that neither death, nor life, nor angels, nor principalities, nor powers, nor things present, nor things to come, nor might, Nor height, nor depth, nor any other creature, shall be able to separate us from the love of God, which is in Christ Jesus our Lord.

— Romans 8:38–39 (Douay-Rheims)

The younger novice was crying. Isidore let him, and after a while sent them both to bed.

•••

The government spray men came through the county that summer with their hand pumps and sprayed DDT on the inside walls of the houses, the dogtrot house too, and in a few years there was hardly any chills and fever left in Arkansas. The abbey did not last. It was too poor and too far from anything, and in 1953 the monks went back to Kentucky and left the land to the cedars. They took Brother Paschal with them, in a pine box this time, and he lies at the big house now among the others, under the same plain cross as all of them.

Isidore lived to be ninety and was infirmarian until the last two years of it. The young monks at the Kentucky house, where there were always sick, said that he had one rule he would not bend for any abbot, and that was that no brother with a fever would lie alone at night. When they were sick he came in with a straw tick over his shoulder and a lantern, and put the tick down across the door, and sat up in the chair by the bed with a basin of cool water on his knees, an old man in brown with a white beard, the lantern turned down low so it would not wake them, as long as it took.

The verse

Of the people who possess these virtues, who live without thoughtlessness, and who are emancipated through true knowledge, Mara, the tempter, never finds the way.Dhammapada 57, tr. F. Max Müller (1881)

In the commentary, the elder Godhika lives on the Black Rock at Isigili, near Rājagaha. A long illness makes him fall away from deep meditation six times; on the seventh, fearing for his future, he takes his own life. Māra, the tempter, comes to the Buddha in disguise and urges him to stop the monk, but only because he wants to keep Godhika inside his own realm, and the Buddha recognizes him. Afterward Māra searches every direction for Godhika's consciousness as a column of smoke and a mass of darkness, then comes as a young man with a yellow lute of vilva wood to ask where Godhika has gone. The Buddha says he has gone beyond Māra's reach, and Māra drops his lute and vanishes. Read the full commentary story (it follows Saṃyutta Nikāya 4.23 almost word for word), or about Māra. In the Buddhist tradition, Godhika's case is an exception that the texts treat with great care; neither the commentary nor this retelling holds it up as an example. Here the story's center is the searcher who cannot find him, and the old brother who stays with the sick.

The Arkansas Ozarks, 1946–47

After the Second World War, the Trappist abbey of Our Lady of Gethsemani in Kentucky filled with so many new monks, many of them veterans, that it sent out foundations across the country, among them Conyers, Georgia (1944), Huntsville, Utah (1947) and Mepkin, South Carolina (1949). The Sylamore Creek foundation in this story is invented. The details of Trappist life are drawn from accounts of the time: rising at two in the morning, silence kept with a sign language, choir monks in white and lay brothers in brown with beards, and burial in the habit without a coffin. Under the 1917 Code of Canon Law, those who deliberately took their own lives were to be denied church burial; superiors and pastors often judged that illness had taken away the person's freedom, and the current Code no longer contains the rule.

Malaria was a fact of life in Arkansas well into the twentieth century. According to the Encyclopedia of Arkansas, the state reported more malaria deaths than any other through most of the 1920s and 1930s, and a federal campaign sprayed hundreds of thousands of homes with DDT between 1947 and 1953. In the Pacific, the Guadalcanal campaign left many thousands of American servicemen with malaria; relapsing vivax malaria brought many of them back to fever again and again, ten relapses being not unusual, and a drug that could clear the dormant parasite was not yet in general use. Ozark folk belief long called the fiddle the devil's instrument, and in Southern folklore the devil is a stranger you meet on the road carrying a fiddle of his own.
