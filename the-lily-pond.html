Walking the American WayCommentary 11.9 · Mahā Dhana

The Lily Pond

I am become like a pelican in the wilderness, and like an owl that is in the desert.
— Psalm 102:6

This is what I heard, on Asylum Hill.

I. Trebles

Asylum Hill, Hartford · 1898

Hiram Holbrook made steel tubing for bicycle frames in a brick works on Capitol Avenue, and in the spring of 1897, when every clerk in Hartford rode to the office on two wheels and the craze looked as if it would never end, he sold the works for a sum the Courant printed on its front page and never went down to Capitol Avenue again. He was forty-four. He bought eleven acres on the slope west of Prospect Avenue, with a carriage house and a stand of elms. At the bottom of the lawn there was a hollow, and his wife had it dug out into a pond, planted with white water lilies, and stocked with goldfish from a dealer in Boston.

Then he and Abigail sat down in the library to decide what their son should learn.

There was very little, they agreed, that the boy would need. There was money in that house for three lifetimes. He would not have to sell anything or make anything or answer to anyone. Let him have what his father had never had time for. Abigail, who came from Litchfield people and had the Litchfield sort of conscience, made one condition. On his tenth birthday Dana signed a printed card at her desk, promising, God helping him, never to touch intoxicating drink, and she put the card in her Bible at the Book of Proverbs and that was the end of his moral instruction.

After that he learned to sing.

He had a voice. Everybody said so. Walter Ince said so, and Walter Ince did not say things. He had come over that autumn from Lichfield, in England, where he had been a cathedral chorister and then a pupil of the organist, to be organist and choirmaster at St. Barnabas on Farmington Avenue. He was twenty-two and thin as a music stand. At the first rehearsal he walked down the front row of trebles in their Eton collars with his head on one side, the way a man listens to a watch, and stopped at the Holbrook boy and held up his hand, and the whole choir stopped with it.

Again, he said. You alone.

Dana sang the Mendelssohn solo, O for the wings of a dove, at Evensong four Easters running. Women in the parish wept into their gloves. Mr. Ince never praised it. He said Breathe and Lower and Again, and once, after a Christmas when the boy had held the last note longer than was written, That was for you. Next time make it for them.

He had piano lessons twice a week from a German on Asylum Street, and a banjo from a man who played on the excursion boats down the river. He was not sent to learn bookkeeping, or Latin past the second year, or anything about steel. When his voice broke at fourteen he cried for two days, and Mr. Ince took him into the vestry and played a low C on the harmonium and told him to match it. It will come back lower, he said. Wait. It came back a baritone, warm and easy, and Dana took it to Yale in 1906 and sang in the Glee Club and in the late quartets at Mory's, where the other boys drank beer out of silver cups and he drank ginger ale, because of the card in the Bible, and was the only one who could still find the pitch at one in the morning.

Celia Burnham's grandfather had founded a fire insurance company, and her people had arrived at the same conclusion Dana's had, by the same road. Celia learned to dance. She learned the waltz and the two-step from a dancing master on Pearl Street before she could do long division, and later ballet from a Russian in New York she went down to by train, and what was called aesthetic dancing, in a Greek tunic, barefoot on the lawn of the Golf Club for a pageant. She held her head as though there were a book on it. She could cross a ballroom without seeming to touch the floor.

They were married at St. Barnabas in June of 1912. Mr. Ince played them out with the Widor, too loud, which everyone forgave him. Hiram Holbrook died in 1915. Abigail died in October of 1918, of the influenza, in three days. Celia's mother and father followed inside of a year. When the lawyers were done there was twice the money in the one house on the slope, and nobody in it who had ever been taught to count it.

II. Prospect Street

Downtown Hartford · 1920

Dana went down to the Hartford Club three times a day, the way his father had. At ten for the mail and the New York papers. At one for lunch. At five for the hour before dinner, when the men came over from the insurance companies and somebody always asked him to sit down at the piano. Fenn drove him in the Pierce-Arrow, down Farmington Avenue and through the city to Prospect Street, and waited at the curb with the motor ticking, across from the new Travelers Tower, which was the tallest thing in New England and which Dana had watched go up from the club window one floor at a time.

In January of 1920 the country went dry. Connecticut had never agreed to it. The state senate had voted the amendment down. It came anyway, and with it came a new kind of man on the sidewalks of downtown Hartford, men who lived by their wits and had found that their wits were suddenly worth a great deal.

Four of them took to waiting on the iron fence across from the club. A Kerryman named Duffy, who had worked the boats out of New London. A piano player who had lost his job at a hotel bar when there were no more hotel bars. Two others whose names changed. They watched which car came at ten, and at one, and at five, and who got out of it, and who touched his hat to him.

On a cold bright morning in March they were ready. They had flasks in their coat pockets, and salted peanuts in paper twists, and horehound candy, and a paper of hot sausage from the cart at the corner, and a bunch of scallions that Duffy bit the ends off of and chewed like a horse. When the Pierce-Arrow pulled up they started in on all of it, loudly, with a great show of pleasure, passing the flasks, and when Dana stepped out onto the curb Duffy lifted his and called across the street.

Long life to you, Mr. Holbrook! A hundred years! And may the four of us eat and drink on you the whole of it!

Dana laughed. He liked to be liked. He stood on the curb with his gloves in his hand.

Fenn, he said, what are they drinking?

Something, sir.

Is it good?

Fenn had been in France. He looked at the four men on the fence for a while before he answered.

Mr. Holbrook, there isn't anything in this world like it.

Well, then, said Dana. I ought to know what it is.

Fenn crossed the street and came back with a little in the cap of Duffy's flask. Dana drank it on the curb with his back to the club windows. It was Canadian, and good, and it went down into him like the first warm day. He sent Fenn back for a little more. That evening he did not go home for dinner until after nine, and Celia, who had been taught never to ask a man where he had been, did not ask.

The card was still in Abigail's Bible in the library, at Proverbs. Nobody had opened the Bible since the funeral.

III. The Ready Tin

Hartford and New Haven · 1920–1931

It took Duffy about a month to see that it had become a habit, and after that the four of them were never far away. Then they were eight. Then there were more of them than Dana could have named, though he knew them all by their faces and always had a word for each. There was a back room on Front Street behind an Italian grocer's, and a room over a garage on Allyn Street, and later a house in the Farmington Valley with the shades down. Wherever he was, he got into the way of pulling a roll of bills out of his coat and setting it on the card table in front of him, and then another roll, and peeling them off as the evening went.

Fenn, take this and get flowers for the table. Take this and get the good cigars, not those. Look at this one, he can make the dice do anything he tells them. Listen to this man play! Have you ever heard anything like that? Give him a thousand. This one can do the Charleston, look at him go. Give him two.

Late, he would sit down at whatever piano there was and sing. He still had the voice. He sang the old Glee Club songs and the new ones off the records, and near the end of every night, because they asked for it, the Kipling song they used to do at Mory's, about the gentlemen who had come down in the world and gone into the ranks as common soldiers. The whole room would come in on the line they all knew, roaring it, pounding the tables.

And the world was more than kin while he held the ready tin,
But to-day the Sergeant’s something less than kind.

— Rudyard Kipling, “Gentlemen-Rankers” (1892)

Dana sang it laughing, with his head thrown back, and did not hear a word of it.

He put money into a musical revue that tried out at the Shubert in New Haven and closed there. He put money into a speedboat, and a racing stable in Kentucky he never saw, and a man who said he could get whiskey across the border in hollow telephone poles. He paid for weddings and funerals of people whose last names he never learned. He bought a band its instruments. It went out of him the way water goes out of a basin with the stopper pulled, smoothly, with a little turning motion at the end, and he did not look down at it until the spring of 1926, when Duffy, who by then was something like a secretary, came to him at the club and shut the door.

Mr. Holbrook, I'm sorry to tell you. It's gone. All of yours.

Dana thought about that for a while with his drink in his hand.

Has my wife no money?

She has, sir. A good deal.

Well, then, said Dana. Fetch that.

Celia signed the papers at the dining-room table where she had been taught to sit up straight. She had been taught to follow. She had followed in the waltz and in the two-step and in the long marriage, beautifully, and she followed now, and signed where the lawyer's finger was, and her grandfather's fire insurance money went the same way as the bicycle money, onto card tables, into bands and boats and telephone poles. By the time the market broke in 1929 there was not much left of it for the market to take.

IV. Everything

West Hartford · 1931–1940

Then he sold things.

He sold the farm in Simsbury, where the shade tobacco grew under white cloth in the summers like a field of tents. He sold the cottage at Fenwick with Celia's rose garden. He sold the Pierce-Arrow and then the Packard he had bought to replace it, and let Fenn go with two weeks' wages and a gold watch, which Fenn tried to give back. He sold the Gorham silver, and the Haviland china a few pieces at a time to a dealer on Pratt Street, all of it but one cup with a gold rim and a small chip, which Celia kept back without saying so. He sold his grandmother's quilts and the down comforters and Celia's sealskin coat and the chesterfield from the library and the library, shelf by shelf. Abigail's Bible went in a box of books for a dollar, and the card went with it, still at Proverbs.

The Steinway went last. Two men came with a dolly and took off the legs in the front hall and carried it out on its side like a coffin. Dana stood in the empty music room and hummed a note and listened to it come back off the bare walls.

In September of 1938 the hurricane came up the river and took down the elms along the drive, nine of them, and there was no money to have them cut. They lay across the drive all that winter with their roots in the air. The goldfish in the lily pond had grown as long as a man's hand and nobody fed them but the herons that came up from the river in the fall and stood at the edge on one leg and fed themselves.

In the spring of 1940 he sold the house. A builder named Gorski bought it, house and lawn and pond, and let them stay on until the papers passed, and then on a Monday in May his men came and carried their trunks to the curb and changed the locks. In the carriage house, behind the harness pegs, one of them found a boy’s lemonwood archery bow with a broken limb and a quiver with two arrows left in it, and asked Dana if he wanted them, and Dana said no.

They took a room on Sigourney Street, back on Asylum Hill, six blocks from St. Barnabas. It was in what Hartford called a perfect six, three floors of yellow brick with two bow fronts and six families, and their room was the one behind the kitchen of the family on the third floor, so that every time they went out or came in they went through somebody else’s kitchen and said good evening. They lived against other people’s walls. They heard other people’s radios and fights and children through every one of them.

By the war Dana was singing for money. He stood at the corner of Main and Asylum with his hat off, or in front of G. Fox’s at Christmas when the windows were lit, and sang carols and the old Glee Club songs in a baritone that was still better than anything else on Main Street, and Celia stood beside him in a cloth coat holding out the Haviland cup. People put dimes in it. Some of them remembered who he was. Those did not stop.

After a while they were eating what people left. The kitchen door of the Bond Hotel. The back of a cafeteria on Pearl Street. And on Thursday nights the alley behind the parish house of St. Barnabas, where the women of the Auxiliary had their supper before choir practice and whatever was left went out the kitchen door in the boys’ hands.

V. The Kitchen Door

St. Barnabas, Farmington Avenue · October 1948

Practice let out at half past eight. The trebles came clattering down the parish house stairs with their cassocks over their arms and went straight to the kitchen, because the Auxiliary ladies gave them the pans to carry out, and it was the part of Thursday they liked best. Creamed chicken in a roasting pan. A basket of rolls under a dish towel. A coffee pot with the grounds still in it. Out in the alley, under the light over the kitchen door, there were four or five men waiting, and a woman, and the boys went down the line and spooned it into whatever was held out to them. Pails. Coffee cans. A pie tin. A china cup with a gold rim.

Walter Ince came out the vestry door with his music case. He was seventy-two. He had been at St. Barnabas for fifty years that autumn and the vestry had given him a dinner and a silver salver and a pension he had not asked for. He was still thin, and he still held his head on one side.

He stopped on the vestry step and looked at the alley.

Kenneth Lusk, who was twelve and the head chorister and stood at the end of the front row on the Decani side, was coming back with the empty chicken pan, and he saw Mr. Ince standing in the light from the kitchen window, looking at the old man with the china cup. And he saw Mr. Ince smile.

It was not a large smile. Kenneth knew all of Mr. Ince’s faces, the way boys learn the faces of the one person whose opinion they cannot do without, and he had seen this one only a few times. It was the face Mr. Ince made when a boy came in a beat early, at the very top of a phrase, so that everybody heard it. It was not unkind. It was not surprised.

Kenneth took the pan in and came back out. Mr. Ince was still on the step.

Sir, Kenneth said, why did you smile?

Mr. Ince did not answer right away. He came down the step and walked Kenneth along the side of the church toward the avenue, where Kenneth’s mother would be waiting in the Plymouth, and at the corner he stopped.

Do you know who that is?

No, sir.

That is Dana Holbrook. He stood where you stand. Decani, the end of the front row. He sang the Mendelssohn four Easters running, better than you will, and you will sing it well. He looked back toward the alley. He had more money than anyone in this parish. His wife had nearly as much. They had the house on the hill past Prospect, with the pond.

Kenneth waited. He had learned that if you waited Mr. Ince would go on, and if you said anything he would not.

If he had put his hand to anything at all when he was twenty-five, Mr. Ince said, anything, his father’s trade or anyone’s, there would not be a richer man in Hartford today. If he had started at forty-five, he would be the second. If he had started at fifty, when they still had the roof over them, he would be the third. And she would have had all of it with him.

He shifted the music case to his other hand.

And if he had given it to God. At twenty-five, Kenneth, he would have been a saint. I mean that. I heard what was in him. And Celia would have been the next thing to one, because she goes where she is led. At forty-five, a holy man, and she a good woman. At fifty, at the very least, a man at peace, and she would have had her peace. He was given every one of those, one after another, and he let every one of them go by. Now he has neither the one nor the other.

Then Mr. Ince said the psalm, the way he pointed the psalms for the boys on Thursdays, quietly, with the small lift at the half-verse where the star is printed in the Prayer Book.

I am become like a pelican in the wilderness, and like an owl that is in the desert.

For I have eaten ashes as it were bread, and mingled my drink with weeping;

And that, because of thine indignation and wrath; for thou hast taken me up, and cast me down.

My days are gone like a shadow, and I am withered like grass.

— Psalm 102:6, 9–11

Kenneth looked back down the alley. The old man had finished the chicken and was holding the china cup for his wife while she ate the roll, and he had his other hand on the small of her back, as if they were about to go out onto a floor together.

He still has his voice, Kenneth said. I’ve heard him by G. Fox’s.

Yes, said Mr. Ince. That is what was left to him. It is the only thing anyone ever taught him.

Kenneth’s mother tapped the horn. Mr. Ince put his hand on the boy’s head for a moment, which he had never done before, and went back up the side of the church toward the vestry, and Kenneth got into the Plymouth and did not say anything all the way home to Elizabeth Street.

That night he did his Latin. He did it every night after that, all through school, and could not have told anyone why, and nobody asked.

VI. The Pond

West Hartford · November 1948

On the first Sunday in November, with the dimes from the week, they took the bus out Farmington Avenue. Celia had wanted to for a long time and Dana had not, and then one morning he put on his hat and said, All right.

The elms were gone. Mr. Gorski had put a road up the middle of the lawn where the drive had been, and paved it, and named it Holbrook Road, and on either side of it there were eleven Cape Cod houses with dormers and picture windows and young maples staked in the front yards, and in the driveways the cars of men who had been in the Pacific and the Bulge and now sold insurance downtown. The big house had been cut up into apartments. Somebody had painted the shutters green.

At the bottom of the road, where the lawn had gone down into the hollow, there was the pond. Gorski had drained it in the summer to fill it for two more lots, and had not got to the filling. The bulldozer stood at the edge with frost on its seat. The lily roots were heaped on the bank, black and fibrous, the size of a man’s arm. The bed of the pond had dried and cracked into plates like a broken platter, and in the low middle there was a skin of brown water an inch deep that had already started to freeze at the rim.

A heron was standing in it.

It was a great blue, tall as a child, and it stood in the middle of the dead pond on one leg with its neck drawn down into its shoulders, the way the herons had stood at the edge of the lilies every autumn of their marriage, when the goldfish were fat and gold and slow. It did not fly when they came down the bank. It turned its head and looked at them with one yellow eye and turned it back to the water.

A boy from one of the new houses was poking along the bank with a stick. He had pulled something out of the mud and was looking at it, and when he saw them he held it up. It was an arrow, the shaft black with rot, the feathers gone, the little brass point gone green.

Hey, mister. Look what was in the pond.

Dana looked at it a long time. Then he said it was a fine one and the boy should keep it, and the boy ran up the hill with it to show somebody.

Celia stood on the bank with her hands in the pockets of the cloth coat. Dana stood next to her. After a while he began, very quietly, to sing. It was not one of the Glee Club songs. It was a psalm to the chant he had learned at ten, on a Thursday, with Walter Ince walking down the front row with his head on one side, and he had not sung it in forty years and had every word.

I have considered the days of old, and the years that are past.

I call to remembrance my song, and in the night I commune with mine own heart, and search out my spirit.

— Psalm 77:5–6

His voice was still good. It carried across the cracked mud to the bulldozer and came back off its blade. A woman in one of the picture windows stopped with a dish in her hand and listened, and did not know what it was.

The heron did not look up. It put its bill down into the brown water and brought it up with nothing, and waited, and put it down again.

Background

The verses

Men who have not observed proper discipline, and have not gained treasure in their youth, perish like old herons in a lake without fish.Dhammapada 155, tr. F. Max Müller (1881)
Men who have not observed proper discipline, and have not gained treasure in their youth, lie, like broken bows, sighing after the past.Dhammapada 156, tr. F. Max Müller (1881)

In the commentary, Mahā Dhana ("Great Wealth") is the son of a rich family in Bārāṇasī whose parents, thinking he would never need anything else, have him taught only singing and music; his wife, from an equally rich house, is taught only dancing. When both sets of parents die, the couple hold a double fortune. Rogues who watch the road he takes three times a day to the king's palace sit drinking and eating where he can see them, and toast him; he asks his attendant what they are drinking, is told nothing in the world compares to it, and takes a little, then more. Surrounded by hangers-on, he spends his own fortune and then his wife's, sells his fields, parks, carriages, dishes and coverlets, and finally the family house, from which the new owners put him out. In old age he and his wife live against other people's walls and he begs with a broken bowl, eating leftovers handed out by novices at a rest-house. The Buddha, seeing him there, smiles, and tells Ānanda that had Mahā Dhana applied himself in youth, middle age or old age he would have been the first, second or third richest man in the city, and had he gone forth he would have reached the paths of holiness, his wife a step behind him; instead he has lost both, like a heron in a dried-up pond. Hartford, 1897–1948

At the end of the nineteenth century Hartford was a manufacturing city as well as an insurance one: the Pope Manufacturing Company made Columbia bicycles there from 1878, and owned its own steel-tubing plants in the city, before the bicycle trade gave way to the automobile. The Hartford Club, founded in 1873, has met since 1908 in its clubhouse on Prospect Street, across from the Travelers Tower, which was the tallest building in New England from its completion in 1919 until 1964 (Travelers Tower). When national Prohibition came in January 1920, Connecticut was one of only two states that had refused to ratify it; its senate voted the amendment down, enforcement in the state was thin, and liquor came ashore from the "rum row" of ships anchored off the coast (Connecticut Digital Newspaper Project). The "perfect six," a three-story apartment house with two bow fronts and six flats, was a Hartford type built in large numbers on Asylum Hill and elsewhere early in the century (Historic Buildings of Connecticut).

After the Second World War, West Hartford filled up with new houses for returning veterans and their families; its population grew by more than fifteen thousand in the two decades after 1940, and the war-workers' housing built there in 1942–43 became the subject of a long fight over who could live in it (Life in West Hartford). The psalms in the story are from the Psalter of the American Book of Common Prayer of 1928, which was essentially Miles Coverdale's sixteenth-century translation (the 1928 Psalter); choirs sang it to Anglican chant, with an asterisk marking the half-verse. The Kipling song is "Gentlemen-Rankers" from Barrack-Room Ballads (1892), sung at Harvard and Yale in the early 1900s and adapted there into the Whiffenpoof Song (Gentleman ranker).
