Best Construction

…but defend him, speak well of him, and put the best construction on everything.
— Luther's Small Catechism, the Eighth Commandment

The way I got it, on Larkspur Circle.

I. The Clipboard

Larkspur Circle, south Lincoln, Nebraska · 1997

Larkspur Circle was nine houses around a bulb of concrete, split-entries and two-stories put up in 1978, and every front yard had a silver maple or a green ash that had gone in as a whip tied to a stake and now stood higher than the roof. The Larkspur Hills Homeowners Association had covenants, twenty-two pages of them, on file with the Lancaster County Register of Deeds. Since 1994 it had also had a Compliance Committee, which was Gordon Ulmer.

He was fifty-six and an internal auditor for one of the insurance companies downtown. Saturday mornings he walked the circle and the two streets that fed it with a clipboard, and he was never wrong. People found that the hard part. Article VI said trash containers were to be stored out of view from the street, and you could see the Novaks' can beside their garage. Article IX allowed one basketball goal to a lot, on a permanent black pole, and the Hansens had the kind on wheels. Fences were to be stained cedar tone. The Sorensens' was nearer redwood, and Gordon carried a paint chip to hold against it. He noted how a man edged his walk, and which way the Beckers' boat sat on its trailer, and that Denny Kratky mowed with his shirt off. In 1996 he wrote up the Larsons for an eighteen-inch satellite dish, and Larson came to the annual meeting with a printout showing that the federal government had that very fall made it unlawful for an association to forbid a dish under a meter across. Gordon read the printout through twice. He did not forgive the Larsons or the FCC.

Article XI prohibited clotheslines and the outdoor drying of laundry. Gordon would tell whoever was nearest how this one kept his cans and how that one hung out her wash.

I am not saying a thing that isn't in the document, he would say, and that was so.

Marv Hagedorn lived at the back of the bulb, at number 7. He was fifty-three and had Hagedorn Heating & Air, two vans and a nephew, and for a good many years he had taken the seventh-grade Sunday school at Good Shepherd Lutheran. Between the vans and the church he had been in most of the basements on Larkspur Circle and heard most of its children say the commandments. He did not talk a great deal. Gordon had written him up twice under Article VIII, commercial vehicle with signage parked overnight, and Marv had moved the van both times.

II. Rifle Shots

October 25 to 29

It rained all that Friday and Saturday. Sometime Saturday evening, while the Kansas game was on the radio from Lawrence, the rain went over to snow. It was thirty-three degrees and the leaves were still on the trees. By midnight the power was out, and people on the circle stood at their front windows in the dark and listened. A limb would let go with a crack like a deer rifle, and then came the long tearing and the thump. Then another would go, two yards over. It kept on until daylight. By Sunday noon there were thirteen inches on the ground, twice the most Lincoln had ever had in an October, and not a whole tree was left on Larkspur Circle. The Beckers' ash lay across their Suburban.

The lights stayed off eight days out there. School was called off, and people managed.

Denny Kratky had a generator from his deer camp. Marv went down the circle basement by basement with a flashlight in his teeth and wired a cord and plug onto every furnace, so that each could run an hour off Denny's machine, by turns, around the clock. Orange cords lay across three lawns and the common sidewalk. Marv would take nothing for it, and while he was down there he looked each furnace over, since that was his habit. The Hansens cooked for whoever came on a camp stove on their front stoop. Everybody dragged brush to the curb until the curb was a hedge six feet high the whole way round the bulb. The Beckers nailed a blue tarp over the hole in their roof. On Tuesday the sun came out, and Carol Novak, with three children home and no dryer, strung a rope between her deck posts and hung up everything they owned, down to the underwear.

On Wednesday morning each of them found a yellow sheet folded into the storm door. Gordon's printer was down, so he had done them by hand in block capitals. Brush at curb more than twenty-four hours ahead of a scheduled collection, Article VI. Unapproved temporary roof covering, Article IV. Electrical cords across common sidewalk. Open-flame cooking forward of the building line. The Novaks had Article XI, and under REMARKS he had put undergarments visible from street. Marv had Article VIII again, six nights.

There were eleven notices. Every one of them was correct.

III. What Does This Mean

The Novaks' basement · Wednesday night

The Novaks had the generator from seven to eight. Marv was on his knees at their furnace with the access panel off, and Kyle Novak, who was thirteen, sat on the basement stairs in his coat with a flashlight and his confirmation folder, because the basement was where the heat would be. Carol came down with her yellow sheet. Denny Kratky and Paul Becker came down behind her with theirs, and the three of them stood at Marv's back and all talked at once. Gordon had done this. Gordon had said that. In the middle of a disaster, Marv. Underwear.

Marv heard them out and went on with what he was doing. When they had run down he spoke without turning around.

Kyle. What have you got for memory work?

The Eighth.

Stand up and say it.

Kyle had sat under Marv in the seventh grade and knew there was no use arguing. He stood up on the stairs and said it to the furnace.

Thou shalt not bear false witness against thy neighbor.

What does this mean? We should fear and love God that we may not deceitfully belie, betray, slander, nor defame our neighbor, but defend him, speak well of him, and put the best construction on everything.

— Luther's Small Catechism

Pastor give you the long one too?

He had. It was four stapled sheets with a paragraph marked in pen.

Read what he marked.

Kyle held the flashlight on it.

For there is a great difference between these two things, judging sin and knowing sin. You may indeed know it, but you are not to judge it. I can indeed see and hear that my neighbor sins, but I have no command to report it to others. Now, if I rush in, judging and passing sentence, I fall into a sin which is greater than his.

— Luther's Large Catechism

Marv set the panel back and sat on his heels.

Is he wrong? About any one of them?

Nobody said he was.

Somebody has to carry the clipboard. I look for what's wrong for a living. I have shut the heat off on a widow in January because her exchanger was cracked, and she stood there and cried, and I shut it off. A man who does his job and tells you to do yours hasn't done you any harm.

He wiped his hands down his coveralls.

But a fellow can get so he likes it. I've had them work for me. They'd come up out of a basement pleased. That one goes looking, and he tells it around, and he never gets one bit better at anything, and I'll tell you why. It's a cracked exchanger. The bad air comes up through his own registers a little at a time, the whole while he's got his eye on your yard. It hasn't any smell. He's the last to know.

He let that stand a moment.

Now. Has any of you said a word of this to Gordon? Or only to me?

Denny looked at his yellow sheet. Carol looked at her son, who had heard her on the subject of Gordon Ulmer all afternoon and was now reading the furnace label with great attention.

Marv plugged in the cord. The blower came up, and the first warm air in that house since Saturday night came out of the register over their heads, and all five of them stood under it and said nothing.

Go on, Kyle, Marv said. The Ninth.

IV. The Mirror

Number 4 · Thursday, November 6

The lights came back on the third. Gordon's clean-and-check fell on the first Thursday of November, as it had for eleven years, and he saw no reason to move it. His own silver maple had split to the ground the first night and still lay across his front walk. He had been busy.

On the basement stairs he mentioned that it had been six nights with the van, and Marv said he knew, he had read it.

Gordon stayed down to watch. He liked to see a thing done in order, and Marv did it in order: the burners out and brushed, the flue, the filter, each reading written on the ticket as he took it.

You write it all down, Gordon said. From him that was praise.

I write it down so whoever comes next year knows where to look. I don't know another reason to.

Marv went into the heat exchanger last, with the flashlight and the little round mirror on its wand. He was in there some time. Then he backed out and held the mirror and the light up to Gordon.

Look for yourself. Left side, second cell, up top. Get the light under it.

Gordon got down on one knee on his own basement floor. It took him a while to find the angle. Then it was there in the glass, a dark line as long as a finger with rust along both lips.

How long?

Two winters. Could be three.

You were here every one of those years.

I was. It opens hot and shuts cold. You catch it the year you catch it.

Marv turned off the gas cock. He took a red tag from his shirt pocket and filled in every line of it, the date, the address, the defect, his license number, and wired it to the valve. Gordon read it through twice. He was still on one knee, with the mirror in his hand, looking up into his own furnace, when Marv went out to the van to call in the order.

It was to freeze hard that night. By dark there were four electric heaters on Gordon Ulmer's front step, each with its owner's name on a strip of masking tape, and nobody had rung the bell. The one from the Novaks had an orange cord coiled on top of it, in case he came up short.

Background

The verse

If a man looks after the faults of others, and is always inclined to be offended, his own passions will grow, and he is far from the destruction of passions.Dhammapada 253, tr. F. Max Müller (1881)

The commentary story is one of the shortest. A monk whose name means “Fault-Finder” goes about remarking on the others: this one puts on his under-robe so, that one his upper robe so. The monks report him to the Buddha. The Buddha first makes a distinction. One who keeps strictly to his duty and admonishes others to do the same is no fault-finder. But one who looks for flaws because he is minded to find them, and goes about talking of them, reaches none of the higher attainments; what the tradition calls the āsavas, the inflowing pollutants, only increase in him. Lincoln, October 1997

On the night of October 25–26, 1997, heavy wet snow fell on eastern Nebraska while the trees were still in full leaf. Lincoln measured 13.2 inches, double its previous record for the whole month of October. Limbs came down all night across the city; at the height of the storm more than 30,000 of Lincoln's 105,000 homes were without power, public schools and the university closed, and the last customers were not reconnected until November 3. Larkspur Circle, its association and its people are invented. The rule Mr. Larson brought to the meeting is real: under the Telecommunications Act of 1996 the Federal Communications Commission barred homeowner associations from prohibiting satellite dishes a meter or less across, effective that October. A cracked heat exchanger in a gas furnace can let combustion gases, including carbon monoxide, into the air a house breathes, and a serviceman who finds one shuts the furnace off and tags it.

Martin Luther wrote both of his catechisms in 1529, the Small Catechism for households and children and the Large Catechism for pastors and teachers. The longer passage is from the Large Catechism in the translation of F. Bente and W. H. T. Dau (1921).
