The Third Chair

Only the poor man counts his flock.
— Seneca, Letter 33, tr. Gummere (quoting Ovid)

C’est de même que je l’ai entendu conter, above the Androscoggin.

I. The Tuesday Table

Pontook Falls, New Hampshire · 2012–2017

Chief Lise Pelletier ran the Tuesday table at the VFW post on Main Street because the men would not come to anything called a group. She had tried calling it a group. Two men came, and one of them was the minister.

She had driven trucks in the Marines in the first Gulf war and come home to a town of two thousand people that had lived for a hundred years on the pulp mill in Berlin and had not yet worked out how to live without it. The mill shut in 2006. By 2012 the Pontook Falls police department was the chief, three officers and a cruiser with a quarter of a million miles on it. Mostly what they did was drive men home. She was forty-five. She had read Seneca since a lieutenant in Saudi Arabia left a paperback in a tent, and she now owned the three red volumes of the old Loeb edition, bought used in Hanover, the spines held together with packing tape.

On Tuesdays at seven she read one letter aloud, slowly, the way you would read a will. Then she asked one question and said nothing else. Some nights the men talked until ten. Some nights nobody said a word. They drank their coffee and went home, and she counted that a good night too.

Before anyone sat down, somebody set a chair for her at the end of the table. Nobody remembered who started it. Nobody else ever sat in it. The winter night she was out at a fatal on Route 16 and never came, eleven men sat around the empty chair for an hour and talked about the letter anyway.

Ray Lacasse and Kyle Brennan came to the table the same Tuesday, in November of 2012, and on paper they had nothing to say to each other. Ray was forty-eight. He had been a millwright at the pulp mill for twenty-two years and had plowed driveways and fixed snow machines since. At nineteen he had been at the Beirut airport the morning the barracks went down, and he had dug for two days, and that was all anybody at the post had ever got out of him about it. Kyle was twenty-three, two years out of Sangin, and had just been hired as a corrections officer at the new federal prison in Berlin. He talked fast. He had read more by twenty-three than Ray would read in his life, and he knew it, and he could not help letting you know it.

But they were the only two men at the table who did not drink, and after the second Tuesday they walked out to the lot together, and after that they were seldom seen apart. Kyle was at Ray’s kitchen table most Sundays. They ran Ray’s traps on the Upper Ammonoosuc and sat in an ice shack on the reservoir with nothing to say for six hours at a stretch. When Ray’s wife Monique died in the spring of 2015, Kyle slept on the couch for a month. When Ray’s son Danny died on a bathroom floor in Manchester in November of 2016, one of four hundred and eighty-five people the state lost to drugs that year, Kyle drove down and got him and drove him home.

Five years after their first Tuesday, they stayed after the table, in the fall of 2017, and asked the chief what the work was.

She folded her reading glasses. There’s two ways to do this. You can learn it. All of it, the Latin, the Greek if you want, every letter he wrote. Or you can do what it says. Both of those are good. Most men try both and do half of each.

I’m fifty-three, Ray said. I read a page and by the bottom the top’s gone. I’ll do the other one.

I’ll learn it, Kyle said.

She tore a card off the stack of three-by-fives she kept in her shirt pocket for parking tickets and wrote on it in square capitals and gave it to Ray. It said DO THE NEXT THING. Kyle she gave nothing, because he was already reaching for the red book.

II. The Hired Man

North Haverhill · 2017–2021

Monique’s cousin Gordon Pike had forty-four Holsteins in a tie-stall barn on the river flats below Route 10, a bad back, and nobody to milk. His children had gone to Concord and Charlotte and were not coming back. Fourteen-eighty a hundredweight, he told Ray, standing in the milk house that December with his hands under his armpits. That was the summer before last. I lost money on every can of it. Half the farms in this valley sold out. I could pay you in a room and whatever’s left.

Ray sold the house in Pontook Falls for what it would bring. He took nothing much. His barn coat came out of the donation box at the post and had somebody else’s name, GAUTHIER, stitched over the pocket. He moved into the old hired man’s rooms over the milk house, two rooms and a hot plate, and at fifty-three he learned to milk.

Four-thirty in the morning and four-thirty in the afternoon. Dip, strip, wipe, attach. The pipeline overhead carried the milk through glass to the tank, and you could see it go by, white, over your head. He learned each cow by her name and her temper. He learned to read a heifer’s tail and to pull a calf. He did not read anything. He was too tired to read, and it turned out he did not need to. He said very little. When people asked how he was, he said Milking, and that was the answer.

The spring after he came, a boy from Pontook Falls named Tyler Roy showed up in the dooryard with a duffel bag and one of the chief’s three-by-five cards. The chief had arrested him twice. This card said YOU ARE NOT IN CHARGE OF THE WEATHER. Ray read it and put it in his coat and showed Tyler where the boots were.

After Tyler there were others. The chief sent some. The drug court at the county courthouse two miles up Route 10 sent some, once word got around. Each of them came with a card in the chief’s square printing, and each card said one thing, and Ray never asked what it meant. He stood beside them in the parlor at four-thirty and did the next thing, and they did it beside him. Some of them left in a week. Some stayed a year. Gordon watched from his kitchen window and said he had never seen so many grown men so careful with a cow.

They stayed clean, a good many of them. Not all. Ray went to the funerals of the ones who did not, in Gauthier’s coat, and stood at the back, and drove home in time for the evening milking.

III. The Watch

The Millyard, Manchester · 2018–2021

Kyle quit the prison in 2018 and moved to Manchester, to a third-floor studio in one of the old Amoskeag mill buildings on the river, brick walls and maple floors still dark with a hundred years of loom oil. He bought two good cameras and a microphone on an arm and began to talk.

He was good at it. He would have told you so himself, but it was also true. He had read every one of the hundred and twenty-four letters twice, and Epictetus, and the Meditations in Greek with a dictionary propped open beside him. He could give you the three disciplines, the four passions, the difference between an impression and an assent. He could give them to a nineteen-year-old lance corporal in Jacksonville at two in the morning in words the boy could hold on to. He called the channel The Watch. He signed off every episode the same way: Stand your watch. When the pandemic closed the gyms and the bars in 2020 and young men all over the country sat alone in their rooms with their phones, they found him by the hundred thousand.

By the summer of 2021 he had five hundred and twenty thousand subscribers. He had eighteen chapters that met in VFW halls, church basements and the back rooms of breweries, from Bangor to Albany. Every one of them set out a chair at the end of the table that nobody sat in. Kyle had told them about the Tuesday table, and they liked the story. He sold challenge coins stamped MEMENTO MORI. He kept his commonplace cards in a steel ammunition can, four thousand three-by-fives in his own small printing, sorted by author, and on camera he would sometimes reach into the can without looking and come up with the right one.

He answered every email himself. Three men that he knew of had put down a gun because of something he said. He did not forget that, and he did not let himself make too much of it. But he knew the subscriber count every morning before he knew the weather.

That August the eighteen chapters held their first Muster, a weekend at a function hall in Gorham, six miles from the post on Main Street, because that was where it began. Four hundred men came. On the Saturday afternoon four young men in clean jeans and barn boots came up to Kyle by the coffee urns. They had driven over from Haverhill. They had been to see the chief that morning to thank her, and the old men at the post, and now they had come to see him.

Ray says hello, Tyler Roy said. He said to tell you.

Ray! How is he? Kyle meant it. What’s he got you working on up there?

Milking, mostly.

Kyle laughed. No, what are you reading? Has he got you on the Enchiridion? Give me the first line. Some things are within our power. He waited, smiling. Come on. The dichotomy of control. Anybody.

The four of them looked at each other. Tyler took a card out of his shirt pocket, worn soft as cloth, and looked at it, and put it back.

Cows don’t care, one of the others said.

Kyle laughed again, and nobody laughed with him. They shook his hand and drove back to Haverhill for the evening milking. Kyle stood by the urns with his coffee going cold and thought about Ray, who could not get to the bottom of a page, who had never in five years of Tuesdays said one sentence that was not about the weather or a truck, who had taken a dead man’s coat out of a donation box and gone off to the river flats and gathered strays. And they called him their teacher. Next year, Kyle thought, I’ll have him up. On the stream. He can tell everybody what he knows.

IV. The Third Chair

Gorham · June 2022

Ray came over for the second Muster on a Saturday in June, the first day he had taken off the farm in four and a half years. Tyler and the others were milking. He parked in the function hall lot at ten in the morning in Gauthier’s coat, though it was already warm. He found Kyle on the stage running cables and gave him the coat and his feed-store cap.

Watch these. I’m going up to see the chief.

He drove up to Pontook Falls in Gordon’s truck and sat in the chief’s office for half an hour. Then he went down Main Street to the post, where the old men were at the bar drinking coffee at eleven in the morning, the Korea men, the Vietnam men, the ones from his own war. He shook every hand. Then he drove back to Gorham.

Kyle had the stage ready. Two chairs, the same make and the same height. He had checked. He was not going to have it said he had put Ray in a lower chair. Then, without thinking, the way you put your keys in the same pocket, he set a third chair at the end of the table, and stood looking at it, and left it.

In her office that morning the chief had listened to Ray tell her, without any edge in it, that Kyle wanted to ask him some questions on the stream that afternoon. After he left she sat for a while with her hands flat on the desk. She knew Kyle. She knew what he would do in front of forty thousand people, and she knew he would not mean any harm by it. She also knew there are things a man can do in front of forty thousand people that he cannot take back, and that the one they are done to is not always the one who pays for them.

At two-thirty she got in the cruiser.

The hall was full. The stream was live, and the number in the corner of Kyle’s laptop said forty-one thousand and climbing. Kyle had just finished introducing Ray (the man who sat across from me at the Tuesday table for five years, my oldest friend in this) when the side door opened and the chief came in in uniform with her hat in her hand. She walked up the side aisle, climbed the three steps to the stage, and sat down in the third chair.

Chief, Kyle said.

Go ahead, she said. I’m just on my rounds.

V. That’s Right

The same afternoon

But it was she who asked the questions, and Kyle let her, because it was her table.

She asked him what is in our power and what is not. He gave it to her in Greek and then in English. She asked him the four virtues, and he gave them. She asked him the three disciplines, the passions and their kinds, the reserve clause, the view from above, what Seneca meant by a day’s accounts. He answered every one of them correctly, without a card, in good plain sentences, and the hall clapped, and in the corner of the laptop the chat ran faster than anyone could read it.

Then she said, Kyle. When were you last afraid?

He began to tell her what fear is. It is a judgment about a future evil, he said; the evil lies in the judgment, not in the thing. He went on in that way, well, for most of a minute.

When, she said.

He opened his mouth and nothing came. He was afraid at that moment and could not say so. Four hundred men watched him not say it.

She turned to Ray. When were you last afraid?

Tuesday, Ray said. Two in the morning. A heifer calving backwards. I thought she’d die and the calf with her. Then I got my arm in up to here. He touched his shoulder. You can’t be scared and have your arm in a cow. Not both at once. You have to pick.

That’s right, the chief said.

In the milk house in North Haverhill, Tyler Roy had his phone propped on the bulk tank so he could watch while he washed up. He said afterward, and the three others backed him, and so did Gordon Pike in his kitchen, that when she said it all forty-four cows in the barn lifted their heads at once and bawled, and the swallows went out of the rafters and through the big door in one rush. In the chat, the same two words went by thousands of times, faster than the screen could hold them.

She asked Kyle who he was angry at. He gave her Seneca on anger, a brief madness, and the three ways to meet it, and named some officers he had served under.

She asked Ray.

The fella who sold Danny the stuff, Ray said. I was. He’s up at Berlin now, the federal. Kyle helped me get on his visiting list, before he quit there. You remember. Kyle nodded. He had driven him up the first time. I go up the first Saturday of the month. He’s twenty-nine. They’ve got him learning to weld.

Are you angry at him?

Ray thought about it honestly, the way he thought about everything. Not that I can find.

That’s right, the chief said. And in North Haverhill the cows bawled again.

She asked Kyle what he wanted. He said he wanted to want nothing outside his own will, and quoted, and was right.

She asked Ray.

Rain Wednesday, Ray said. Gordon’s got second cut coming.

That’s right. The cows.

She asked Kyle who he was. He was a Marine, he said, a student, a teacher of young men, a man still trying. He spoke for a long time. All of it was true.

She asked Ray.

Hired man, Ray said.

That’s right, she said, the fourth time. And this time it was not only the Pike barn. Every farm family on both sides of the Connecticut that day, in Haverhill and Bath and over the river in Newbury and Bradford, will tell you that at about a quarter to four on that Saturday afternoon every cow in the valley called out at once, in the barns and in the pastures, and the dogs answered from the dooryards. People came out onto their porches and looked up and down the river. They saw nothing and went back in.

VI. Other Men’s Cows

The same afternoon · and July

In the front rows the chapter leaders had gone quiet in a way that was not respect. A man from the Albany chapter said it out loud, and the chat said it louder. Kyle knew all of it, every letter, every line, and the chief had not said That’s right to him once. She had said it four times to an old man in a borrowed shirt who could not name the three disciplines.

What’s the trouble? the chief said into the microphone, and the man from Albany told her.

She heard him out. Then she said, Ray says he’s the hired man. He’s wrong about that. Kyle’s the hired man. He’s like a fella you pay to stand at the gate and count another man’s cows through. He’ll get the number right every time, and he won’t get a glass of milk out of it. Ray milks his own.

She took the first red volume out of her jacket, where it had been all along, and opened it at a page she had marked with a parking ticket, and read it the way she read on Tuesdays.

For a man, however, whose progress is definite, to chase after choice extracts and to prop his weakness by the best known and the briefest sayings and to depend upon his memory, is disgraceful; it is time for him to lean on himself. He should make such maxims and not memorize them. For it is disgraceful even for an old man, or one who has sighted old age, to have a note-book knowledge. “This is what Zeno said.” But what have you yourself said? “This is the opinion of Cleanthes.” But what is your own opinion? How long shall you march under another man’s orders?

— Seneca, Letter 33.7, tr. Gummere

She turned the page.

But it is one thing to remember, another to know. Remembering is merely safeguarding something entrusted to the memory; knowing, however, means making everything your own; it means not depending upon the copy and not all the time glancing back at the master.

— Seneca, Letter 33.8, tr. Gummere

Then she closed the book, put on her hat, and went down the three steps and out the side door. The hall sat and listened to the cruiser pull out of the lot. Kyle did not sign off. After a while somebody in the booth ended the stream.

•••

In July Kyle drove to North Haverhill with the ammunition can of cards on the passenger seat. He did not bring it in. He slept in Tyler’s spare room over the milk house. At four-fifteen Ray knocked, and they walked down to the barn in the dark with the river fog lying on the flats.

The cows came in off the night pasture and went each to her own stanchion, without being told. Kyle stood by the door and counted them under his breath as they came, the way he had counted men off a helicopter.

Forty-four, Ray said, handing him the dip cup. You can stop.

Kyle took the cup. The first cow shifted her weight and let her breath out long and warm against his arm, and over their heads, in the glass line, the milk began to run toward the tank.

Background

The verses

The thoughtless man, even if he can recite a large portion (of the law), but is not a doer of it, has no share in the priesthood, but is like a cowherd counting the cows of others.Dhammapada 19, tr. F. Max Müller (1881)
The follower of the law, even if he can recite only a small portion (of the law), but, having forsaken passion and hatred and foolishness, possesses true knowledge and serenity of mind, he, caring for nothing in this world or that to come, has indeed a share in the priesthood.Dhammapada 20, tr. F. Max Müller (1881)

In the commentary, two friends from Sāvatthī go forth together. After five years, one, who has gone forth late in life, takes up the duty of meditation, goes off to the forest in rag robes and becomes an arahant with many pupils. The other takes up the duty of study, learns the Three Baskets by heart and teaches five hundred monks in eighteen communities. When the forest elder’s pupils send greetings, the scholar scoffs that the old man does not know a four-line verse, and resolves to question him. Foreseeing that this would ruin the scholar, the Buddha comes as if on his rounds and sits in the seat always kept ready for him. He asks both monks questions. The scholar answers everything about the meditative absorptions, but nothing about the paths; the elder answers every question, and the Buddha praises him four times while the gods applaud. To the scholar’s offended pupils he says their teacher is like a man who tends cows for wages, while the elder enjoys the products of the cow as his own. Read the full commentary story.

New Hampshire, 2012–2022

Berlin, in the North Country, was a paper town for more than a century, and much of it still speaks New England French. Fraser Papers closed the Berlin pulp mill in 2006, and the town later took a federal prison, opened in 2012. New Hampshire’s per-capita overdose death rate was among the highest in the country in these years: 485 people died of drugs in 2016 and 487 in 2017. The state’s dairies were going under at the same time. In 2016, as drought set in and milk fell to $14.80 a hundredweight, 19 of the state’s 120 dairy farms closed. Haverhill, on the Connecticut River, is the Grafton County seat; the county offices and courthouse stand on Route 10 just north of North Haverhill. Pontook Falls, the Pike farm and every person in the story are invented.

Seneca wrote his 124 moral letters to Lucilius at the end of his life. Richard Mott Gummere’s translation of them appeared in the Loeb Classical Library, whose Latin volumes are bound in red, beginning in 1917. Stoicism has had a long modern revival: the founders of cognitive therapy traced their method back to the Stoics, and the Navy pilot James Stockdale credited Epictetus with carrying him through years as a prisoner in Hanoi. That made it a philosophy many American servicemen and veterans took up, and in the 2010s and 2020s it found a large online audience. Ray dug at the Beirut barracks, where 241 American servicemen were killed in October 1983. Kyle’s studio is in the Millyard, the brick mill buildings of the Amoskeag Manufacturing Company, which went bankrupt in 1935.
