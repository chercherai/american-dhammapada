What Shall I Cry

Favour is deceitful, and beauty is vain: the woman that feareth the Lord, she shall be praised.
— Proverbs 31:30

Así lo oí contar, on the bay below the Castillo.

I. Hot Lard

San Agustín de la Florida · Lent 1736

In the spring of 1736 there were six English ships in the harbor at once, which was against the King's law and everybody knew it, and the men off those ships said that the most beautiful woman between Charles Town and Havana lived in a stone house south of the plaza in San Agustín, and that her name was Gloria.

She was the younger sister of Maestro Pedro Leal, the surgeon of the garrison, who had set half the broken legs in the town and closed most of its knife wounds. Their father had been a soldier and their mother a soldier's widow who took in washing, and when Gloria was fifteen she stopped taking in washing. By twenty-five she owned the stone house, and a woman named Juana who had been bought for her in Havana, and a set of pearls. An English captain had once paid her an onza of gold for a single night, sixteen pieces of eight, and told it all the way up the coast. Officers of the garrison paid her in silver when the situado came from Mexico, and in promises when it did not, which was most years. The town's wives crossed the street when they saw her coming. The town's husbands did not.

That Lent she was living in the house of a merchant's son named Salgado. It was an arrangement. His wife, Doña Inés, was a quiet woman who went to the friars' church twice a day and kept every fast in the calendar, and who had said to her husband, it was said, that if he must have somebody, let it be somebody she could see, and let it be only until Easter, and let her pray in peace.

Gloria lived in that house for three weeks and could not stand it. She could not stand how quiet Doña Inés was. She could not stand being forgiven before she had done anything. One morning in the kitchen, with the lard spitting in the iron pan for the fish, Doña Inés came in to give an order to the cook and looked at Gloria with no anger at all, and Gloria picked up the pan and threw it.

The cook screamed. The lard went over Doña Inés's face and her neck and her arms, and ran down, and she stood there and wiped her eyes with her sleeve. There was not a mark on her. Not a blister. The cook would swear to it until she died, and so would the kitchen boy, and the lard had been hot enough to fry a mackerel. Doña Inés looked at Gloria for a moment more, and said, Go home, child, before somebody hurts you, and went out.

Gloria went home. She did not sleep for two nights. On the third day she found out that the Father Guardian of the Franciscans was to dine at the Salgado house after the noon Mass, and she put on a plain dress and no pearls and went there and knelt on the floor of the dining room in front of everybody and asked Doña Inés to forgive her. She said it to Doña Inés. She looked at the friar.

Fray Lucas Ortega had been born in San Agustín, the son of a sergeant, and except for his years of study in Havana he had never lived anywhere else. He was fifty that year and had a soldier's shoulders and a voice that did not carry far, so that people leaned toward him. He had finished his meal. He put down his spoon.

She forgave you before you came, he said. You know that. That's what you couldn't stand.

Be not overcome by evil: but overcome evil by good.

— Romans 12:21

Gloria stayed on her knees a long while after that, and nobody at the table moved, and when she got up the people who were there said that something in her face had gone still, like a pond when the wind drops, and it did not come back. She asked Fray Lucas if he and his friars would eat at her house the next day. He said they would.

They did, and after that they came every day. Eight of them, at noon, in their grey habits, from the friary at the south end of town and from the Christian Indian towns outside the lines where some of them lived. Gloria stood at the door and filled their bowls herself. The situado had not come that year, and the soldiers' families were living on corn and fish and whatever their gardens gave, but there was white bread at Gloria's from English flour off the English ships, and butter, and milk, and eggs, and oranges from her own trees. Take the butter, Father. Take more milk. Take another loaf for the walk. What she put in one friar's bowl would have fed three. She spent two pieces of eight a day on it. People said she was buying her way to heaven with smuggled flour. People said a great many things.

What they did not say, because they did not know it, was that the officers had stopped coming. The English captains came to the door that summer as they always had, with their hats in their hands and their silver in their pockets, and Juana sent them away. Gloria went to the friars' church in the mornings and sat at the back, near the door, where Doña Inés sat. The two of them did not speak, in church or out of it. But once in May, coming out into the glare of the plaza after Mass, Doña Inés put her hand for a moment on Gloria's arm, and the women who saw it talked about nothing else for a week.

II. Three Leagues

The coast road north · summer

Fray Bernardo was old and fat and talked, and one day in June, after he had eaten at Gloria's table, he walked three leagues up the coast road to a mission town on the inland water, where a young friar named Gil kept the church for a few dozen Christian families who had come down from Guale and the islands when the English and their Creek allies burned the old missions, before Gil was born.

Fray Gil was twenty-four and from Galicia and had been in Florida less than a year. He had never been in San Agustín except to land there. He gave Fray Bernardo water from the well and a hammock under the shed, and after the evening prayer the old man lay in the hammock and talked.

Where did you eat today, Father?

At Gloria Leal's.

Is it good there?

Good? Fray Bernardo laughed in the dark. There's no telling it. White bread. Butter like in Spain. She stands in the door and fills your bowl till it runs over and then she puts in more. One bowl of hers would keep you a week up here. But I'll tell you, as good as the food is, she's better to look at. And he told it, the way old men tell things, lying in a hammock in the dark with their hands on their bellies. Her hair. Her throat. The way she stood in the door with the light behind her. How the English captain had paid an onza. Fray Gil said nothing at all.

He had never seen her. He would say later that this was the strangest part of it, that it went into him all the same, the way a fever goes into a man who has only walked past the house. He lay on his mat all night listening to the frogs and the old man snoring and could not stop seeing a woman he had never seen.

In the morning he said, carefully, as if it were nothing, that he had business at the friary and would walk back to town with Fray Bernardo, and was there any order to how the eight friars came to her table, and who sat where.

No order, said Fray Bernardo. First come. Go early, son, and you'll sit at the head, and she'll fill your bowl first.

Fray Gil did not wait for him. He took his bowl and his staff and started down the coast road alone in the dark before the next dawn, and walked three leagues, and was standing at the door of the stone house south of the plaza with his bowl in his hands when the sun came up out of the sea.

He had come in by the land gate in the Cubo line while it was still grey, past the sentry, who knew a friar when he saw one and did not ask. He had crossed the plaza with the fishermen going down to the boats and the bakers' smoke going up, and the Castillo standing over the bay at the north end of the town, grey stone made of shells, with the first light on it. He had not noticed any of it. He had been walking all night toward a face, and when he got to the door he found that he was afraid to knock, and stood there with the bowl in his hands until Juana opened it to throw out the wash water and nearly threw it on him.

III. The Fever

The stone house · that day

Gloria had fallen sick the afternoon before, just after Fray Bernardo left her door. It began with a headache and a pain in her back, and by night she was burning. She took off her pearls and her rings and her earrings and gave them to Juana to put away, and lay down in her room with the shutters closed.

At noon Juana came in and told her that the friars were at the door, eight of them, and a young one first.

Gloria could not get up. She lay with her eyes closed and gave her orders from the bed. Let them in. Give them seats in the cool room. Give them the broth first, and bread. When it's time for the rice, fill their bowls, and fill them right, Juana, the way I do it. Don't be stingy because I'm not there to see.

So Juana and the girl from the Indian town who worked for wages brought the friars in and sat them on the benches and gave them broth and bread and then the rice with milk and cinnamon, and filled their bowls the right way. Then Juana went back to the room.

Carry me out, said Gloria. I want to greet them.

Señora, you can't stand.

Then carry me.

So they carried her, Juana under her arms and the girl at her feet, in her shift with a shawl around her, and set her on a stool in the doorway of the cool room. She put her hands together and bowed her head to the friars. She was shaking all over with the fever. Her hair was down. There was nothing on her, not a ring, not a ribbon.

Fray Gil was at the head of the bench, where he had wanted to be. He looked at her. He thought: if she is like this sick, with nothing on her, shaking, what must she be when she is well, and dressed, and has the pearls on?

That was all. But he could not eat after that. He sat with his bowl in his hands and the rice going cold and heard nothing anybody said. When the other friars rose he rose with them and took his full bowl and walked out of the house and down to the friary at the south end of town, where the masons were laying the first courses of the new stone walls, and went into the cell they had given him in the old wooden building, and put his bowl on the shelf with a cloth over it, and spread his habit on the floor, and lay down on it with his face to the wall.

Another friar came at evening and tried to get him to eat. Fray Gil did not answer him.

Gloria Leal died that night, before the bells for Matins, with her brother holding her wrist.

In the morning Maestro Pedro went up to the Castillo and told the governor, because everyone of any account in the town told the governor such things, and the governor sent his adjutant down to the friary to tell Fray Lucas that the surgeon's sister was dead, she who had fed his friars, and to ask whether she should not be buried in the friars' church, where she had already paid for a grave before the altar of Our Lady.

Fray Lucas was a long time answering.

Don't bury her, he said. Ask His Excellency for this. Lay her outside the Cubo line, on the open ground, on a bier, where everyone can see. Put a guard on her day and night, so the dogs don't get her, and the buzzards. Don't let anyone touch her. I'll tell him when.

The adjutant stared at him. Maestro Pedro, when he heard, sat down on the friary steps and put his face in his hands. But he did not ask for anything different, and in the end it was done the way Fray Lucas said.

IV. Four Days

Outside the Cubo line · June 1736

The Cubo line ran from the Castillo across the neck of land to the San Sebastián River, an earth wall faced with palm logs, with a palisade along the top and a ditch in front and the slope planted thick with Spanish bayonet so that no man could climb it without leaving pieces of himself. North of it the soldiers kept the ground cleared for a musket shot, so an enemy would have nowhere to hide. That was where they laid her, on a plank bier on two trestles, in her best dress, with her hands folded and her hair combed by her brother.

The first day she looked asleep. People came out through the gate and stood at a distance and looked at her and went back and said she looked asleep.

The second day the color went out of her. The third day there was a smell that the sea wind took toward the town in the afternoons, and the buzzards came, black ones and red-headed ones, and sat on the palisade in a row and waited, and the two soldiers on guard threw stones at them, and the buzzards moved down a few stakes and sat again. On the fourth day she had swelled. Her face was gone. From her mouth and her nose and her eyes and her ears and the other openings of the body there came the white moving things that come, and her whole body under the good dress was like a cracked pot of boiled rice.

On that day the governor had the drum go through the town.

The crier was Domingo de la Cruz, a free Black man past sixty who had cried every proclamation and every auction in San Agustín for thirty years, the prize cargoes the privateers brought in and the goods of dead men sold in the plaza to pay their debts. Everybody knew his voice. He went down every street of the town with a soldier beating the drum in front of him and stopped at every corner and cried it. By order of His Excellency the governor. Let everyone go out to see Gloria Leal. Everyone, except those who are guarding houses. Anyone who does not go will pay a fine of eight reales. And the governor sent to the friary to ask that the Father Guardian and his friars go too.

We'll go, said Fray Lucas.

Fray Gil had been lying on his habit for four days. He had not eaten. He had not spoken. The rice in the bowl on his shelf had rotted, and in the wet heat of that June a grey-green fur had grown up over the cloth that covered it. His brothers came to the door of the cell and said, Fray Gil. The Father Guardian is going out to see Gloria Leal. Will you come?

At her name he was on his feet. I'll come, he said.

He took the bowl down off the shelf and pulled off the cloth and threw the rice out the window to the hens, and went to the cistern and scrubbed the bowl with sand until it was clean, and put it in his sack, and went out to the gate with the others.

V. The Crier

The cleared ground · the fourth day

The whole town was there. The friars stood together on one side of the bier, Fray Lucas at their head. On the other side stood everybody else, each in their place, the way they stood at processions. The officers of the garrison and their wives in front, and the merchants, and Maestro Pedro with his hat in his hands. The soldiers. The women of the confraternities in their black. The free Black militiamen and their families. People from the Christian Indian towns outside the lines, who had come down because the drum said everyone. Juana, who had combed her mistress's hair every morning for eleven years. At the back, standing a little apart where the road went down to the harbor, a few English sailors who did not know what was happening and had come to see.

Nobody went closer than ten paces. Many of them held cloths to their faces, and some of them held oranges cut in half.

Fray Lucas looked a long time at the bier. Then he turned to the governor's adjutant.

Who is this woman?

The adjutant thought he had misheard. Father, it's Gloria Leal. The surgeon's sister.

This is Gloria?

Yes, Father.

Then let her be sold, said Fray Lucas. Have your crier cry it the way he cries the goods of dead men in the plaza. Whoever will give an onza of gold for her, let him take her.

The adjutant looked at Domingo de la Cruz, and Domingo looked at the adjutant, and then Domingo stepped up beside the bier with the drum behind him and lifted his head and cried it, in the voice everybody knew. An onza of gold! Who gives an onza of gold for Gloria Leal?

Nobody said anything. Nobody so much as cleared his throat.

They won't take her, Father, said the adjutant.

Then bring the price down.

Eight pieces of eight! cried Domingo. Who gives eight pieces of eight for Gloria Leal? Nobody. Four! Two! One piece of eight! The drum beat after each one. The buzzards on the palisade lifted at the sound and came down again. Four reales! Two reales! One real for Gloria Leal! Nobody. Half a real! A copper! Who gives one copper for her? Nobody moved. Some of the English sailors were laughing, uneasily, the way men laugh in church. A child began to cry and was hushed.

For nothing! cried Domingo. Take her for nothing! Whoever wants her, take her away for nothing!

Nobody said anything.

Maestro Pedro had not moved through any of it. He stood in the front with the officers, his hat in his hands, looking at the ground between his boots. He had held his sister's wrist while she died and combed her hair for the bier, and he did not look at the bier now, and he did not look away from it either. Once, when Domingo cried one real, his lips moved, and the officer beside him said afterward that he had been saying the price along with the crier, under his breath, the way a man counts.

Father, said the adjutant, nobody will take her. Not even as a gift.

Fray Lucas turned to his friars.

Look, he said. That is what a woman is worth to the multitude. In this town an English captain paid an onza of gold for one night of her, and told it all the way to Charles Town, and the officers of the garrison paid what they had, and you have all heard what Fray Bernardo says at table. Three months ago half the men here would have sold their boots for her. Now nobody will carry her away for nothing. That beauty is gone where beauty goes. Look at this body. Look at it, brothers. Don't look away.

Domingo de la Cruz stood beside the bier with his hands at his sides. He had cried prices for thirty years, and he had never once in all that time been unable to sell a thing. He did not know what to do. He turned to the Father Guardian and asked him, quite simply, the way he would have asked the governor, Father, what shall I cry now?

Fray Lucas told him. Domingo lifted his head and cried it out over the cleared ground, in the same voice he had used for the prices, to the friars and the officers and the soldiers' wives and the Indian families and the English sailors at the back, and the buzzards rose all together off the palisade and went up into the white sky.

The voice of one, saying: Cry. And I said: What shall I cry? All flesh is grass, and all the glory thereof as the flower of the field.

— Isaias 40:6

Fray Gil was standing at the end of the line of friars, with his clean bowl in his sack. He had been looking at the thing on the bier since he came through the gate. He went on looking. The people near him said afterward that he did not weep and did not pray out loud and did not fall down. He only stood there with his hands open at his sides while the fever went out of him, the way the color goes out of a coal, and then he put his hands in his sleeves like any friar at any burial and bowed his head. Many others on that ground saw something that morning that they did not forget, and some of them changed their lives over it, and the town talked about it for years. But Fray Lucas, walking back through the gate, put his hand on the young man's shoulder and left it there, and did not say anything.

VI. Coquina

The friary · that summer

They buried her that evening in the grave she had paid for, before the altar of Our Lady in the friars' church, with the shutters open and the doors open and a great deal of incense. Juana walked behind the coffin. So did Doña Inés Salgado, in black, and Maestro Pedro, and Domingo de la Cruz, and all eight of the friars who had eaten at her table, and some of the wives who had crossed the street. Fray Lucas said the Mass himself. When it was over he stood at the edge of the open grave with the holy water and said one thing more over it, quietly, the way he said everything, so that the people near him had to lean in.

Favour is deceitful, and beauty is vain: the woman that feareth the Lord, she shall be praised.

— Proverbs 31:30

Then they closed the grave and laid the flagstone over it.

•••

Fray Gil did not go back up the coast that summer. He asked to stay and work on the new friary, and Fray Lucas let him. Every morning before light he went across the bay in the boat to the King's Quarry on Anastasia Island with the masons and the hired men, and cut coquina out of the pit, and loaded it, and rowed it back.

Coquina is soft when it first comes out of the ground, so soft it can be cut with an axe, and it is no good for building until it has lain out in the sun and the rain for a year or more and hardened. So the stone Fray Gil cut that summer was not the stone he laid. He stacked his blocks to dry at the edge of the quarry, and in the afternoons he carried other blocks, cut before he ever came to Florida, up from the boats to the masons. If you look at coquina closely it is not one thing. It is shells. It is thousands upon thousands of little shells, the small bright clams that live in the wash of the surf along that whole coast, pink and white and yellow and violet, every one of which was once alive and once beautiful, broken and packed together and pressed down under the sea until they were stone.

At noon the masons sat in the shade of the half-built walls and ate, and Fray Gil sat with them with his bowl on his knees and ate whatever was in it, corn or fish or nothing, and when he had finished he washed the bowl and set it upside down on a block of coquina to dry in the sun. Then he went back to work, and lifted the next stone onto the last one, with its thousands of shells in it, and the mason set it level, and Fray Gil went down to the boat for another.

The verse

Look at this dressed-up lump, covered with wounds, joined together, sickly, full of many thoughts, which has no strength, no hold!Dhammapada 147, tr. F. Max Müller (1881)

In the commentary, Sirimā is a celebrated courtesan of Rājagaha and the sister of the physician Jīvaka. After wronging the devout laywoman Uttarā, she comes to ask her pardon while the Buddha is eating at Uttarā's house, hears him teach that anger is overcome by kindness and evil by good, and becomes a disciple. From then on she feeds eight monks every day, lavishly. One monk praises her food and her beauty so warmly that another monk falls in love with her without ever having seen her, and contrives to be among the eight the next morning. She is ill that day and is carried out trembling to greet the monks; he goes home unable to eat, and she dies that evening. The Buddha asks King Bimbisāra not to burn her body but to leave it under guard in the charnel ground. On the fourth day, with the body swollen and crawling with maggots, the king has a drum beaten through the city and fines anyone who stays away, and at the Buddha's word offers her body for a thousand coins, then less and less, down to an eighth of a coin, then for nothing, and nobody will take her. The Buddha speaks the verse on the body, and the lovesick monk reaches the first stage of awakening. Read the full commentary story.

San Agustín, 1736

In the first half of the eighteenth century, St. Augustine was a poor garrison town that could barely feed itself. The royal subsidy from Mexico, the situado, came irregularly and sometimes not for years. Carolina forces had burned the town in 1702, sparing only the Castillo de San Marcos, and English and Creek raids destroyed most of the interior Franciscan missions; their surviving Christian Native families resettled in small towns close to the city. Trade with the English was illegal but went on openly, and according to the Florida Museum one observer in 1736 counted six English merchant ships in the harbor at once.

The Franciscans' friary at the south end of town, burned in 1702, began to be rebuilt in stone in 1735; the building survives as the St. Francis Barracks. Its stone, like the Castillo's, was coquina, a rock made of shell fragments, quarried on Anastasia Island across the bay. Coquina is soft when first cut and has to dry for a year or more before it can bear weight. The town's northern edge was the Cubo Line, an earthwork of earth and palm logs with a palisade and a moat, running from the Castillo to the San Sebastián River.
