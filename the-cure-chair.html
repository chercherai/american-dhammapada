Walking the American WayCommentary 15.7 · Tissa

The Cure Chair

And the true philosophers, Simmias, are always occupied in the practice of dying, wherefore also to them least of all men is death terrible.
— Plato, Phaedo

As it was told on the porches, on Helen Hill.

I. Four Months

Pelletier Cottage, Helen Hill, Saranac Lake, New York · June 1925

Mrs. Pelletier's was a tall brown house on Helen Hill with three porches stacked on the front of it, one to a floor, and twelve cure chairs on the porches. Fourteen people boarded there that year at twenty-two dollars a week with trays. They had come up from offices and schoolrooms and one from a ribbon counter in Utica, each with a spot on an X-ray plate, to lie out of doors eight hours a day in every weather the Adirondacks had and wait for the lung to heal. It was called curing. A person was on the cure, or cheating the cure, and the whole of medicine was in the difference.

Miss Agnes Thayer had the front room on the first floor. She was fifty-four. She had taught Greek and Latin for twenty-two years at a girls' school downstate, and she had been in Saranac Lake for nine, which made her what the village called a chronic. Every afternoon when rest hour was over and the four o'clock temperatures were written down, she read aloud from her chair on the bottom porch until the supper trays came. She read Plato, in Jowett's English, a volume at a time. Whoever was allowed out of bed came down in coats and blankets to hear it, and those who were not lay on the porches above and got what came up through the floor.

She did not lecture. If someone asked what a thing meant, she read it again more slowly. To each new boarder she said one thing, the first week, and did not repeat it.

You are going to lie still for a year. You had better learn to be there while you do it.

On the fifth of June she finished a page of the Republic, shut the book on her finger, and told them that Dr. Ames gave her about four months.

I tell you so that you needn't have it from Mrs. Pelletier. We shall have time for the Phaedo, I think.

Then the trays came.

II. The North End

June to September

Nobody ate. The old hands, the ones with three and four winters in, went quiet and were suddenly particular about their own hours, since they knew to the week what four months was. The new ones cried where they sat. The girl from Utica cried so that she brought on a cough, and had to be put to bed. For days afterward they stood about in twos and threes on the stair landings, where they were not supposed to stand at all, and asked each other what was to be done.

A great deal was done. There was a list on the hall table of who would sit with her, hour by hour, so that she should never be on the porch alone. The women sewed balsam pillows, the kind the village sold to tourists, until she had eleven. There were flowers from the florist every Saturday. A subscription went round for a shelf of the classics in her name at the Free Library, and a man on the second floor who had been a sign painter lettered the plate for it. Her porch was full from the minute she was wheeled out, and they talked to her, kindly and without stopping, and when she read now they sat so close that the ones upstairs heard nothing. Even rest hour was whispered through. It tired her a good deal, and she thanked them.

Walter Tice did none of it.

He was twenty-nine and had been a clerk in an insurance office in Hartford, and he had been at Mrs. Pelletier's since November without curing a single day. His four o'clock ran a hundred and two-tenths as regularly as a clock. He played pinochle through rest hour on the second-floor porch. He walked down to the Pontiac on Broadway for the pictures when he was meant to be flat, and smoked, and knew a taxi man who could get Canadian whisky, and was cousining with a girl at a cottage on Franklin Avenue. The one hour of the day he never missed was Miss Thayer's. He liked it better than the pictures. He had come to believe that lying near her while she read was doing him the same good as doing what she said.

On the night of the fifth of June he lay awake on the sleeping porch and thought it through.

If she is gone in four months and I am still running a hundred at four, then all I ever had of her was the pleasure of listening. Whatever she has, I had better get it while she is here to tell me whether I've got it.

In the morning he gave his cigarettes to the tray boy. He had his chair moved up to the north end of the top porch, which nobody wanted, since it got no sun after one and looked at nothing but Baker Mountain. He took his trays there. He kept every hour on the card, eight in the chair and the two of rest hour dead flat without a book, and the milk and the raw eggs between meals, and when Dr. Ames after a month allowed him ten minutes' walking he walked ten minutes by his watch, up Helen Street and back, alone. He stopped talking. He answered the nurse and the doctor and otherwise said nothing to anyone, at first because talk made him cough and afterward because he found he had no use for it.

And he did the other half, which was hers. He lay there and tried to be there. His mind went down to Broadway forty times an hour, and to Hartford, and to the girl on Franklin Avenue, and each time he fetched it back to the chair and the blanket and the mountain. For weeks it was like calling a dog that will not come. Then, some afternoons, it stayed.

He did not go down for the reading. At half past four he could hear, two floors below, that she had begun. He could not hear the words.

Tice, what has got into you? they asked him on the stairs. She's dying. You haven't been down once.

He nodded to them and went on up.

III. No Feeling

The bottom porch · October

On the seventh of October Christy Mathewson died in his house up on Park Avenue, and for a week there was not a cut flower to be had in the village at any price. Pelletier Cottage found chrysanthemums for her anyway.

It was the sign painter and two of the women who said it to her at last, and they meant it kindly.

Mr. Tice has no feeling for you at all, Miss Thayer. Four months, and he has not come down those stairs. He hasn't signed the list. He never so much as put his name to the shelf.

She asked whether Mr. Tice still kept his hours. They said he kept nothing else.

Ask him to come down tomorrow at half past four. If his chart allows.

He came down in his overcoat with his blanket over his arm. She was propped on a back-rest with the eleven pillows ranged along the porch rail behind her and the chrysanthemums in a pail, and she had gone the color of the pages she read from. All fourteen were there, and they made room for him grudgingly.

Mr. Tice. I am told you have kept away from me since June. Why is that?

It was the first he had spoken to any of them in four months, and his voice was not used to it.

You said four months. I'd been here seven and never cured a day. I liked your hour, and I took that for the same thing as doing what you told me. So I thought I had four months to find out whether I could do it, while there was still somebody to ask.

And can you?

My four o'clock has been normal for nineteen days. That isn't what I mean.

No, Miss Thayer said. I know what you mean. Well done, Mr. Tice.

She looked along the rail at the pillows.

You have all been very good to me. I have more pillows than I have a head for. But I should like one thing understood while I am able to say it. Mr. Tice is the only one of you who has paid me any attention.

The book was open on her knees at a place near the end. She had been keeping it for them.

When he had done speaking, Crito said: And have you any commands for us, Socrates—anything to say about your children, or any other matter in which we can serve you?

Nothing particular, Crito, he replied: only, as I have always told you, take care of yourselves; that is a service which you may be ever rendering to me and mine and to all of us, whether you promise to do so or not.

— Plato, Phaedo, tr. Benjamin Jowett

Nobody on the porch said anything. The girl from Utica took her name off the list that evening and went to bed at eight.

IV. Four O'Clock

The same porch, and the top one

She kept him back when the trays came and the others had gone in.

What is it like, up at your end?

He tried to tell her and could not. He said it was like the house after everybody has gone to the pictures. He said that was wrong, too.

It is in here, she said, and turned back sixty pages without having to look for it, and read it to him alone, with the light going.

But when returning into herself she reflects, then she passes into the other world, the region of purity, and eternity, and immortality, and unchangeableness, which are her kindred, and with them she ever lives, when she is by herself and is not let or hindered; then she ceases from her erring ways, and being in communion with the unchanging is unchanging. And this state of the soul is called wisdom?

— Plato, Phaedo, tr. Benjamin Jowett

He had sat down on the porch step to listen. When she stopped he found that it had stayed, the thing that stayed some afternoons, and that it was not going to go. He had been afraid since a doctor in Hartford held a plate up to a window and showed him the spot. He had been afraid of her dying, all summer, two floors over her head. He looked for the fear now where he was used to finding it, and there was the porch, and the pail of chrysanthemums, and a woman shutting a book.

Go up, Mr. Tice, she said. You will miss your hour.

He climbed the two flights at the pace Dr. Ames allowed. At the north end he got into the chair and wrapped himself the way the nurse had taught him the first week and he had never troubled to do, the blanket under and over, left side, right side, feet, until nothing was out but his face. Baker Mountain had the last of the light on it. He lay there done up like something laid out for burial, in the cold, by himself, and he had never in his life been so comfortable.

Background

The verse

He who has tasted the sweetness of solitude and tranquillity, is free from fear and free from sin, while he tastes the sweetness of drinking in the law.Dhammapada 205, tr. F. Max Müller (1881)

In the commentary, the Buddha tells the monks at Vesālī that in four months he will pass away. Seven hundred of them are shaken. Those who have not reached the goal cannot hold back their tears, and they go about in little groups asking one another what they are to do. One monk, the elder Tissa, reasons that he is not yet free and had better become so while his teacher is alive. He keeps to himself and stops speaking with anyone. The others ask him why and get no answer, and they report to the Buddha that Tissa has no affection for him. The Buddha sends for Tissa, hears his reason, and says, “Well done.” Then he tells the rest that only one who acts like Tissa truly loves him: those who honor him with perfumes and garlands do not honor him, and those who practice what he taught do. He speaks the verse, and Tissa reaches the goal. Read the full commentary story.

Saranac Lake, 1925

Before antibiotics the best treatment anyone had for tuberculosis of the lungs was rest, food and cold clean air, kept up for months or years. Edward Livingston Trudeau, a consumptive doctor who had recovered in the Adirondacks, opened his sanitarium at Saranac Lake in the 1880s, and the village grew into the country's best-known cure town. Most patients boarded in private cure cottages, houses built or rebuilt with open porches where they lay out in every season on wide reclining cure chairs made in the village. Many of the cottages stood on Helen Hill. A patient's day was ruled by the thermometer and the clock: hours in the chair, a silent rest period after dinner, exercise allowed by the minute once the afternoon fever was gone. Romances between patients were called cousining. Pelletier Cottage and its people are invented.

The pitcher Christy Mathewson, one of the most admired ballplayers of his day, came to Saranac Lake with tuberculosis and died there on October 7, 1925, the day the World Series opened; both teams wore black armbands. The book Miss Thayer reads is Plato's Phaedo, the account of Socrates' last day in prison, in Benjamin Jowett's translation. Socrates spends the day arguing that a philosopher's whole life is “the practice of dying,” and when his friend Crito asks at the end what they can do for him, he gives the answer quoted in the story.
