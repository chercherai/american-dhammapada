Thirteen Pleats

…a past thought is not perceived, a future thought is not perceived, and the present thought is not perceived.
— Diamond Sutra 18

Yue si ngo man, on King Street.

I. The Window

Chinatown-International District, Seattle · March 2018

For thirty years Leung Wai-man came back from the produce market at ten minutes to seven, walking up King Street from the alley behind the restaurant with a box of gai lan on his shoulder, and for thirty years Chan Mei-ling was at the window.

It was the upstairs window, the one over the red sign. Lucky Pearl Seafood and Dim Sum had the ground floor and the kitchen in back, and above it were three rooms where they had lived since 1988, when they bought the building with a loan from the bank on Jackson Street and money from four of his cousins and every dollar she had saved working the cart at a dim sum house in Vancouver. She stood at the window at ten minutes to seven every morning in her housecoat with a cup of hot water with lemon in it, and he came up the street with the box, and when he got under the sign he looked up and smiled, and lifted two fingers off the box and wiggled them at her, which was a thing he had done since the first week, and which their son Kevin, when he was little, thought was the funniest thing in the world.

Wai-man was sixty-three. He had come from Hong Kong in 1975, a dim sum cook since he was fourteen, with burn scars on both forearms from the steamers that went white in the winter. He made har gow with thirteen pleats, and in the Chinatown-International District people said there were only three men left in Seattle who could still make them that way, and the other two were his students. Mei-ling was sixty. She had come from a village in Taishan by way of a garment factory in Kwun Tong and the dim sum house in Vancouver, and she ran the front of Lucky Pearl the way a ship’s captain runs a deck, every table and every check and every credit card, and she knew the names of the grandchildren of people who had been coming in since the Kingdome was still standing. On the shelf behind the register, between the fortune cat and the calendar from the herbal shop, there was a white porcelain Guanyin with an incense pot and a plate of oranges, and she changed the oranges every Monday, and lit the incense every morning before she unlocked the door. That was most of her religion. She thought of herself as a practical woman.

In February, Wai-man’s old friend Lau, who had cooked at the Lucky Pearl until his knees went, started going to Dharma talks at a little Buddhist temple on the second floor of a building on Jackson Street, over a travel agency. It had been a dentist’s office. Now there was a Buddha in it, and folding chairs, and an old monk from Hong Kong whom everybody called Shifu, who had come to Seattle in 1986 and taught there on Sunday afternoons in Cantonese and Wednesday nights in English, in both languages very slowly. Lau talked Wai-man into coming. Wai-man went to be polite. He went the next Sunday because Lau asked him again. He went the third Sunday on his own.

On the third Sunday Shifu was teaching the Diamond Sutra, as he had been, Lau said, for eleven years, one chapter at a time, around and around. And before he began he told an old story, the way he told one every week, about a famous monk in the Tang dynasty named Deshan, who was the greatest scholar of the Diamond Sutra in all of China and walked south with his commentaries on his back to show the meditation monks how ignorant they were. On the road he got hungry and stopped at a tea stall to buy dim sum from an old woman. She saw the books on his back and asked what they were. He told her. Well, then, she said, I’ll ask you one question from your sutra. If you answer it, I’ll give you the dim sum for nothing. If you can’t, go somewhere else to eat. The sutra says the past mind can’t be got hold of, the present mind can’t be got hold of, the future mind can’t be got hold of. So which mind, Venerable, are you going to refresh? Because dim sum, Shifu said, if you write it out, means to touch the heart. To dot the mind.

The room laughed. Shifu did not. He said Deshan could not answer her, and went on hungry, and soon afterward burned every one of his commentaries. Then he opened the sutra and read from the sixth chapter, slowly, in Cantonese and then in English, and Wai-man sat on his folding chair in his good jacket and heard it.

By those who know the teaching of the Law, as like unto a raft, all qualities indeed must be abandoned; much more no-qualities.

— Diamond Sutra 6

Nobody ever knew exactly what happened to him in that room. Lau said he just sat there afterward while they folded up the chairs, with his hands on his knees, looking at the floor as if he had dropped something on it and then decided to leave it there. When Lau asked if he was all right he said, Yes. Yes. Very all right. Then he got up and went down the stairs.

On Monday morning at ten minutes to seven Mei-ling was at the window with her hot water and lemon, and Wai-man came up King Street from the alley with the box of gai lan on his shoulder, and walked under the sign, and did not look up. He went in through the side door. She stood at the window with her cup for a while, looking at the empty sidewalk where he had been.

Never mind, she thought. At staff meal I’ll find out.

II. Take It

Lucky Pearl · that day

Staff meal at the Lucky Pearl was at three-thirty, in the dead hour between lunch and dinner, at the big round table in the back with the lazy Susan. The cooks and the waiters and the dishwashers ate, and Wai-man and Mei-ling ate with them, and every day for thirty years Wai-man had served her first. He would turn the lazy Susan and pick out the best piece of steamed fish, the cheek, with his own chopsticks and put it in her bowl, and say Sik faan, eat, and only then would anybody else start.

That Monday he sat down and ate his rice. He ate the gai lan and the tofu and he did not touch the fish or the soy-sauce chicken or the pork. He did not turn the lazy Susan. He did not say sik faan. He ate slowly, with great attention, as if rice were something he had just heard about and wanted to understand, and when his bowl was empty he set his chopsticks across it and sat there, and the whole table went quiet around him and nobody looked at Mei-ling.

He’s angry, she thought. Something I did. Something with the money.

That night after closing, when the cooks had gone and the chairs were up on the tables and the only light in the dining room was the red glow from the sign coming in through the front window, he asked her to come and sit down. She sat across the round table from him. He put a manila envelope on the lazy Susan and turned it, slowly, until it stopped in front of her.

Inside were the deed to the building, the bank books, the papers for the restaurant license, the title to the van, and a key ring with every key he owned on it.

Mei-ling, he said, everything in this building is yours. Everything we have. Take it.

She looked at the envelope for a long time. People who are angry, she thought, do not give you the building. People who are angry do not give you the van. She knew him. She had known him for thirty-eight years, every way a person can know another person, and she had never seen his face like this. It was not angry. It was not sad. It was like the street at five in the morning before the delivery trucks, when it has been washed by the rain and there is nobody on it yet.

And you? she said finally. What about you?

I’m finished with business, he said. From today. I’ll go and help at the temple. Shifu needs somebody in the kitchen. I’ll get a room. Don’t worry about me.

She sat there with her hands flat on the envelope. Through the front window the sign buzzed and the rain came down King Street in the red light. She thought about thirty years of standing at that window. She thought about every dollar in the bank books, which she had counted herself, one at a time, out of the register, for thirty years.

Leung Wai-man, she said. You chewed it all up and now you’re spitting it out, and you want me to pick it up off the floor and eat it?

He looked at her.

Who picks up what somebody else spat out? she said. Not me. If you’re leaving it, I’m leaving it. Let me leave home too.

She meant the old words, the words for it. Chut ga. To leave home. To shave your head and put on the grey robe and go.

He did not argue. That was the thing she remembered afterward, and told the nuns, and laughed about: thirty-eight years that man had argued with her about the price of shrimp and the color of the menus and which way to hang the toilet paper, and that night he did not say one word against it. He only looked at her with that washed face and said, Good. Very good, as if she had told him the soup was right.

•••

It took her four months. She did it the way she did everything, with a list. She gave the Lucky Pearl to the people who had made it, Ah Fong the head dim sum cook, who had been with them twenty-two years, and Tina Huynh, who ran the floor on weekends, for one dollar each and a promise to keep the name and the har gow at thirteen pleats. The building she put in Kevin’s name, because he was their son and somebody had to have it, though he worked for Amazon in South Lake Union and did not want a building in Chinatown any more than he wanted a restaurant, and he flew back from a conference in Las Vegas and sat in the upstairs kitchen and cried and said they had both lost their minds. She held his hand and told him maybe so. She gave her jade to her sister in Richmond and her good coat to the woman who sold newspapers on Maynard Avenue and her housecoat to the Goodwill.

The Guanyin behind the register she left where it was. Ah Fong asked if she wanted to take it. She said no. She said it had been looking at the door for thirty years and it would know who came in.

III. Leaving Home

Interstate 5 and the foothills · July 2018

Wai-man drove her up himself, in the van, which was now Ah Fong’s van and which Ah Fong lent them for the day. In the back were the offerings: two fifty-pound sacks of jasmine rice, a case of peanut oil, a case of soy sauce, ten pounds of dried shiitake, a box of the good oolong, a check, and a suitcase with almost nothing in it.

The women’s monastery was two hours north and east, past the tulip fields and the dairies and up a gravel road into the foothills, on a hundred acres of second-growth fir that a retired couple from Bellevue had given to the Dharma in the 1990s. There were nine nuns there and an abbess from Malaysia, a long wooden hall, a kitchen, a meditation hall with a woodstove, cabins in the trees, and a bell. Wai-man carried the rice in from the van on his shoulder, one sack at a time, the way he had carried the gai lan up King Street, and set it down in the kitchen, and bowed to the abbess three times, forehead to the floor, and drove back to Seattle alone in the long July light.

They shaved her head the next week. She had worn her hair to her shoulders since 1975. An old nun from Penang did it with clippers and then a razor, in the yard, while the others chanted, and Mei-ling sat on the stool with her eyes closed and felt the air on her scalp for the first time in her life, cold and very clean, as if somebody had opened a window in her head. They gave her the grey robe of a novice and a Dharma name, Fa Yu, and the next spring she went to Taiwan with two of the other nuns and stood for six weeks in a great ordination hall with three hundred women from all over the world to take the full precepts, and when she came back to the foothills she was a bhikshuni, and the younger nuns called her Shifu, and she still answered to Mei-ling when the abbess forgot.

IV. The Steamer

The monastery in the foothills · 2018–2019

She asked to stay in the country. The abbess had meant to send her down to the temple in Seattle after her novice year, to help in the city where they needed a nun who spoke Cantonese, but Fa Yu asked to stay, and stayed. She wanted, she said, to be quiet for a while. She had not been quiet since she was eleven years old.

She did not know how to be quiet. She sat in the meditation hall at four-thirty in the morning with the woodstove ticking and her legs on fire and her mind running the front of the Lucky Pearl, table nine wants more tea, table four has been waiting for the lo mai gai, the fish man is short again, Kevin hasn’t called. It went on like that for months. The abbess told her not to fight it. The abbess told her to watch it the way you would watch a busy dining room from the kitchen door, everybody coming and going, and none of it, the abbess said, is yours.

They put her in the kitchen, naturally, because she was a restaurant woman. She cooked for ten. She made the vegetarian dim sum for the festival days, mushroom and bamboo shoot in a rice-flour wrapper, chive dumplings, turnip cake, because Wai-man had taught her the pleats in 1981 and she had never told anybody she knew how. The nuns from Malaysia and Taiwan and Vietnam and the one young American from Ohio stood in the kitchen door to watch her fold them.

It happened in the kitchen. She said so herself, years later, to a woman who asked her, and so it is known.

It was a morning in February, before dawn, with rain on the roof, and she had a stack of bamboo steamers going on the wok for the Lunar New Year, four high. She lifted the lid off the top one to see if the dumplings were done, and the steam came up all at once in a white cloud into her face and past her into the cold kitchen, rising, spreading, thinning, and was gone. She stood with the lid in her hand. She lifted the second steamer off and the steam came up and was gone. And she saw that the Lucky Pearl was like that, and Taishan, and the factory in Kwun Tong, and the window, and Wai-man coming up King Street with the box on his shoulder and lifting two fingers off it, and Kevin crying in the upstairs kitchen, and the woman she had been at the window with her hot water and lemon, and the woman standing here now with the lid in her hand, and whatever woman would stand anywhere tomorrow. Not one of them could be got hold of. The past one could not be got hold of. The next one could not. This one, here, could not. She opened her hand, she said, the way you let go of a steamer lid that is too hot, without deciding to, and there was nothing in it, and nothing was missing.

The dumplings were overdone. The nuns ate them anyway. The old nun from Penang looked at Fa Yu across the table while they ate, a long look, and after the meal took her hand in both of hers in the yard, in the rain, and held it for a while, and did not say anything.

After that, the abbess said, Fa Yu could explain anything in the sutras to anybody, in Cantonese or English or the Taishanese of her village, to the old women who came up from Seattle on the temple bus and to the young American from Ohio, so plainly that people went away thinking they had always known it. She did not seem to have studied for it. When the abbess asked her where she had learned to read the Diamond Sutra like that, Fa Yu said, In a kitchen.

V. Questions

The temple on Jackson Street · October 2019

In the fall of 2019 she asked the abbess to send her down to Seattle after all. The abbess asked why, now, when she had asked to stay. Fa Yu said that the old women who came up on the bus could not come up anymore, a lot of them, their knees, their sons wouldn’t drive them, and that there were people on King Street who had eaten at her tables for thirty years and would listen to the Dharma from her who would never listen to it from a stranger. She said there was nothing left for her to do in the country. The abbess looked at her a long time and said yes.

Wai-man heard on a Thursday that she had come back and was staying in the room behind the shrine at the temple on Jackson Street. He had been living in a rented room on Maynard Avenue for a year and a half, cooking in the temple kitchen every day but Monday, keeping the eight precepts, sitting with Shifu in the evenings. He had not seen her since July of 2018. He did not know why she had come back. He thought, Maybe it was too hard. Maybe she misses the restaurant. Maybe she wants to come home. And he thought that he could not ask her that. It would be a terrible thing to ask a nun.

He went on Friday afternoon. She was sitting on a cushion by the window of the shrine room, in her grey robe, with her shaved head, reading. The rain was coming down Jackson Street outside and the streetcar went by below with its bell. He stood in the door. She looked up and saw him, and her face did not change, except that it was very glad, in a way that did not seem to have anything to do with him in particular, the way the street is glad in the morning.

He came in, and knelt, and bowed to her three times, forehead to the carpet, the way a layman bows to a monastic. Leung Wai-man, who had given her the cheek of the fish for thirty years. Then he sat down on the floor to one side, respectfully, a little way off, with his hands on his knees.

He could not ask her if she was unhappy. So he asked her about the Dharma.

He asked her about the first fruit, about the one who enters the stream, the way the Diamond Sutra asks it in the ninth chapter: what is it that the stream-enterer has entered? She answered him right away, plainly, in Cantonese, with no sutra words in it at all, the way she used to tell a new waiter where the forks went. He asked her about the second fruit, the one who returns once. She answered. He asked about the third, the one who does not return, which was what Shifu had told him, though not in so many words, about himself, and she answered that too, and looked at him with something that was almost the old smile when she did. He asked about the fourth, the arhat, the one who has done what was to be done. She answered him. He asked about the bodhisattva who could go into nirvana and does not, who stays and comes back for everybody, and she answered, and glanced, without seeming to, toward the window and King Street below it, where the red sign of the Lucky Pearl was coming on in the dusk.

He asked what was on the other side of nirvana.

She laughed. It was her old laugh, the one from the front of the restaurant, the one that could get a table of eight to move over for a table of two.

Wai-man, she said. You’ve gone too far. That question has no handle on it. You can’t pick it up. If you want to know, go ask Shifu.

VI. Well Said

The same evening

He bowed to her three times again, and got up, and went down the hall to Shifu’s room, where the old man was sitting at a card table eating oranges, and sat down, and told him all of it. The questions, the answers, every one, as near as he could remember them. Then the last question, and what she said.

Shifu peeled an orange in one long piece and laid the peel on the table in a spiral.

What she told you is well said, he said. Every word. If you had asked me, I would have told you the same, and not so well. He put a section of orange in his mouth and ate it. That one is my daughter in the Dharma, Mr. Leung. She went to the country a restaurant woman and came back the old lady at the tea stall. Deshan would have gone hungry all the way home.

Then he took down the sutra from the shelf behind him, the old English one with the cracked binding that he read on Wednesday nights, and opened it near the middle, and read to him, slowly, in English, the way he did, as if he were reading the words for the first time.

And why? Because, O Subhûti, a past thought is not perceived, a future thought is not perceived, and the present thought is not perceived.

— Diamond Sutra 18

She has nothing behind her, Shifu said. Nothing ahead of her. Nothing in her hand. She holds on to nothing, and nothing holds on to her. That is a person I would bow to. He closed the book. You bowed to her?

Three times.

Good. Very good.

The people sitting outside the door in the shrine room, waiting for the Friday chanting, heard most of it, and some of them told it afterward on King Street, and a few of them said that something turned over in them, hearing it, the way a page turns in a draft. Lau was one of them. He said he went home that night and did not turn on the television.

•••

Fa Yu taught at the temple on Jackson Street on Saturday mornings after that, in Cantonese, and on Tuesday evenings in English, and the old women of Chinatown came, and some of their daughters, and a few of their granddaughters who did not speak Cantonese and sat in the back with their phones turned face down on their knees. She taught the Diamond Sutra one chapter at a time. Before she began she always told a story. Most weeks it was a story about a restaurant.

Wai-man cooked in the temple kitchen. He made the dim sum for the festival days, vegetarian, thirteen pleats. He never asked her a question again that he could have answered himself.

On Saturday mornings at ten minutes to nine she walked from the temple to the teaching, the long way, down Jackson Street and up King, under the red sign of the Lucky Pearl, past the front window where Ah Fong’s nephew was setting out the teapots and the Guanyin stood on the shelf behind the register with its oranges and its morning incense, looking at the door. She did not look up at the window over the sign. There was nobody there. Sometimes it was raining and sometimes it was not, and the rain ran down the glass of the upstairs window in long threads that caught the red light of the sign, and joined, and ran on down, and were gone off the bottom of the glass, and more came.

Background

The verse

Him I call indeed a Brahmana who calls nothing his own, whether it be before, behind, or between, who is poor, and free from the love of the world.Dhammapada 421, tr. F. Max Müller (1881)

In the commentary, the merchant Visākha hears the Buddha teach and attains the third path, non-return. That day he walks past his wife Dhammadinnā at the window without his usual smile, eats in silence, and then tells her that all the wealth in the house is hers. She asks what he will do; he says he is finished with worldly affairs. "Who will accept the saliva you have rejected?" she answers, and asks to go forth herself. He escorts her to the nuns with rich offerings. She goes to live in the country and soon reaches full awakening, then returns to Rājagaha. Visākha, not daring to ask whether she has come back discontented, questions her on the paths; she answers each, until his last question, when she tells him he has gone too far and should ask the Buddha. The Buddha says her answers were well said, that he would have answered the same, and speaks the verse. Their conversation is preserved as the Cūḷavedalla Sutta, which ends with Visākha asking what lies on the other side of unbinding. Seattle, 2018–2019

Seattle's Chinese community is older than the city's fire of 1889 and survived its expulsion of Chinese residents in 1886. The neighborhood around King and Jackson Streets was cut through by Interstate 5 in the 1960s and crowded by the Kingdome from 1976 until its demolition in 2000; Hing Hay Park, with a pavilion built in Taipei, opened on King Street in 1975, and a new Chinatown Gate was dedicated at the foot of King Street in 2008. Cantonese dim sum houses and small storefront temples are part of its life. The Diamond Sutra, the Perfection of Wisdom text whose Dunhuang copy of 868 is broadly considered the oldest dated printed book, is read and lectured on in Chinese Buddhist temples everywhere; its ninth chapter takes up the four fruits of the path, and its eighteenth says that past, present and future thought cannot be grasped. Shifu's story of Deshan and the old woman with the rice cakes is a classic of Chan: the word for her refreshments, dianxin, Cantonese dim sum, can be read as "touching" or "dotting" the heart-mind.

Full ordination for women died out in the Theravāda countries in the Middle Ages, but the Dharmaguptaka bhikṣuṇī lineage continues in East Asian Buddhism, and women from many countries, including the United States, travel to Taiwan for ordination programs lasting six to eight weeks. A Chinese Buddhist who leaves home (chujia, Cantonese chut ga) shaves the head, receives a Dharma name and the novice precepts, and later the full precepts; laypeople greet monastics with three full bows. The women's monastery, the temple on Jackson Street and the Lucky Pearl are fictional.
