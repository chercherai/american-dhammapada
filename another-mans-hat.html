Another Man’s Hat

Vale más un Dios te lo pague que un talegón de dinero.
— dicho of New Mexico (Espinosa, no. 591)

Así me lo platicaron, at the ford below San Miguel.

I. The Wagons

San Miguel del Vado, New Mexico · July 1839

The Americans stopped on the east bank that summer and unloaded. The governor had tired of counting varas of calico, and he now took five hundred pesos for every wagon that came in, large or small, full or half full. So on the flat across the river from San Miguel the traders pulled the sheets off their loads and made two wagons out of three. The empty ones stood about with their tongues on the ground until somebody bought them for the iron.

Don Ygnacio Baca rode across to them before the first bale was down. He had his English from a Kentucky trapper who had wintered in his father's house, and he wrote a fair hand in two languages. For twenty years he had done what the Americans called passing goods. He rode up to Santa Fe with each caravan and wrote out its manifests for the customhouse. He knew which inspector liked brandy and which had a daughter marrying, and when he was done the duties were arranged. Of what the law asked, a part went to the officers, a part stayed with the trader, and something reached the treasury. Each trader paid him a fee for this and thanked him. He had a saying for it, as he had for most things. Vale más un mal arreglo que un buen pleito. A poor arrangement is worth more than a good lawsuit.

It had made him the rich man of the valley. He had sheep out on shares with thirty families and a house with glass in two of its windows. This year, for the first time, he had four wagons of his own in the train, bought in Missouri with silver.

Two men at the river took nothing from the Americans.

One was the corporal of the guard, Tomás Padilla. The government kept soldiers at San Miguel to see that no wagon went around the customhouse, and the soldiers could be had for a twist of tobacco, all but the corporal. The Americans called him the deaf one. He walked down the line with a tally stick and counted wagons and bales, and what he counted went up to Santa Fe by a rider the same night. He and Ygnacio had herded goats together on the mesa as boys. That July he counted Ygnacio's four wagons as he counted anybody's. His count came to nine bales more than the manifest Ygnacio afterward wrote for himself, and Ygnacio paid on the nine. They had not spoken since.

The other was the old man who kept the ford. He was called tío Chano, and he was Tomás's godfather. His mother had been a Pecos woman, from the pueblo up the river whose last families had gone over the mountains to Jemez the year before. After every rise of the river he waded out with a pole and felt for where the gravel had gone, and he drove willow stakes in two rows to show it. A wagon that kept between the stakes crossed with dry axles. A wagon whose driver knew better went into the quicksand to the hubs, and the old man said nothing and went to borrow oxen. He would not take a party over at the upper bend by night, where goods went around the guard, for any money. He took nothing at the ford by day. People said the Pecos had never wet him above the knee, in flood or out of it. It was a thing people said.

II. The Year

The same village · September

Since the last feast of San Miguel, Don Ygnacio had been mayordomo of the función. For one year the saint's day was his to pay for, and he had paid as nobody on the Pecos had paid before him.

There were six dozen candles of white wax from Chihuahua, where other years had burned tallow. There was a keg and a half of powder from the Americans, for the salutes at vespers and at the Elevation. There was a cloak for the saint cut from a bolt of blue Missouri silk, and two fiddlers and a guitar hired down from Santa Fe, and the padre's fee for vespers, a sung Mass, a sermon and the procession. In the last week he had forty wethers killed and two beeves, and the women of nine households were baking in the ovens behind his house. He reckoned the year at a thousand pesos, which was the duty on two wagons.

He knew what he did it for. San Miguel stood over the altar with a sword in one hand and a balance in the other, and every child in the valley knew what was weighed in that balance, and when. A man who had made his living by arrangement did well to have someone standing at the scale. Hasta para ir al cielo se necesita abogado, he said. Even to get into heaven you need an advocate.

Two days before the feast, Tomás Padilla walked into the corral where the sheep were hanging. He had his hat in his hand, as he had always carried it in that house.

Compadre, he said. Do you ever do one good thing?

Ygnacio had not spoken to him since July. He swept his arm at the forty carcasses, the smoke of the ovens, the keg under its hide.

This.

What is it for?

So the saint will remember my house when he lifts the balance.

Who told you he would?

The padre. And my father, who was mayordomo three times.

The padre has not stood where that weighing is done, Tomás said. Your father had not, and you have not. I know one man who has been across. Come down to the river.

Ygnacio went. He could not have said why. The corporal had counted his bales in front of forty Americans, and now he walked into his corral as if nothing lay between them, and a man wants to see how far such a person will go.

III. The Ford

The Pecos · the same evening

The old man was in the water to his shins, working a stake back and forth to seat it. He heard Tomás out without turning round. Then he waded to the bank and looked at the mayordomo's boots, and afterward at his face.

Is it as he says?

It is, tío.

Then listen. You have fed this valley for a week. Keep that. A hungry man's thanks is worth having. He wiped his hands down his shirt. The rest of it, the wax and the powder and the fiddles, forty sheep killed so that people will say forty, the whole year of it, does not come to the fourth part of one thing. Take your hat off one time to a man who goes straight. Your own hat.

Ygnacio knew to a finger's width what respect weighed. He wrote other men's letters for them. To an equal you left a fourth of the page blank down the left side, and to the governor you left half. He had never asked himself what you left for a man who drove stakes.

What straight man? he said. On this river?

The old man looked at his godson, who was looking at the water.

That one wrote forty when you wrote thirty-one. He lost a friend by it, and he wrote it. You have dealt in derechos for twenty years, Ygnacio, and you have arranged every one. He used the word for the duties, which is also the word for men who are straight. Here is one that is owed to los derechos. I have looked, and there is no upper bend to carry it around by.

He drove the stake once more with the heel of his hand.

You know the saying. Hace carabana con sombrero ajeno. He makes his bow with another man's hat. That has been your función. The hat was the governor's, and the Americans', and thirty families' of shepherds'. San Miguel can see whose hat it is.

Then he said the other one, which the women of that country say every day of their lives.

Vale más un Dios te lo pague que un talegón de dinero.

— dicho of New Mexico

A God-repay-you is worth more than a great sack of money. Ygnacio had heard it ten thousand times. He had always taken it for a thing the poor said because they had no sack.

His hat was a good one, low in the crown and covered with oilcloth, with a band of tinsel cord as thick as his thumb. He had taken it off to the Host, to the governor, to the bishop the year he came up from Durango, and to women. He took it off now. The evening wind found the place on his head where the hair was going, and he turned to the corporal.

Dios te lo pague, Tomás, he said. For the nine bales.

Tomás looked up from the water. Of the two of them, he was the one who did not know what to do with his hands.

And you, tío. Dios se lo pague. Twenty years you have brought my goods over dry.

Now one is paid, the old man said, and he waded back out to his stake.

It was done in a moment, and nobody saw it but a boy watering mules. Ygnacio said afterward that it was the first duty he ever paid in full, and that he came up the bank lighter than he went down, which was not how duties had worked before.

IV. San Miguel

September 29, and after

The feast was kept, since everything was bought. The saint went out in blue silk through the corn stubble and along the ditch with his balance swinging. The powder was burned, the fiddles played until the candles were stubs, and the whole valley ate mutton for three days. The mayordomo walked behind the saint bareheaded, as everyone did. It was noticed that he carried his hat in his hand through the dancing too.

The last of the Americans started home in the first week of October, and Ygnacio's four wagons went with them, back to Missouri for next year's goods. He rode down to see them over. In the middle of the ford the old man stood in the current with a stake in his hand, and on the far bank the corporal was counting. Ygnacio took off his hat in midstream and held it against his chest until his horse had climbed out on the other side.

The teamsters behind him were Missouri boys who did not know the country. They saw the rich man of the place uncover in the middle of the river, and they supposed it was the custom, and one after another they took off their hats to the old man in the water and the corporal on the bank.

So it became the custom. For some years every wagon that crossed at San Miguel went over between the stakes with its driver bareheaded, and men who had made the crossing told others in Independence that you did it for the saint.

Background

The verse

Whatever a man sacrifice in this world as an offering or as an oblation for a whole year in order to gain merit, the whole of it is not worth a quarter (a farthing); reverence shown to the righteous is better.Dhammapada 108, tr. F. Max Müller (1881)

In the commentary, the elder Sāriputta asks a Brahmin who has been his friend whether he ever does a single good deed, and the friend answers that he offers sacrificial slaughter. It was then the custom, the commentary says, to make such sacrifices at immense expense. Sāriputta brings him to the Buddha, who asks whether he has been correctly reported and then tells him that a year of it is not worth a fourth part of what a man gains who gives alms to ordinary people with a believing heart, or who pays reverence to those who walk straight. San Miguel del Vado, 1839

San Miguel del Vado, "Saint Michael of the Ford," was settled in 1794 on the Pecos River by genízaro, mestizo and Pecos Pueblo families, and its church was built in 1805. After 1821 it was the first New Mexican town the caravans of the Santa Fe Trail reached. The Mexican government stationed troops there against smuggling, and traders repacked their loads in the village before going on to the customhouse in Santa Fe. The last families of Pecos Pueblo, upriver, left for Jemez in 1838. The Missouri trader Josiah Gregg, who made the trip eight times in the 1830s, describes all of this in Commerce of the Prairies (1844): the guards who could be had "for a trifling douceur," the interpreters who made a business of "passing goods" and arranging the duties, the custom of splitting the legal tariff among officers, merchants and government, and the few years in which Governor Manuel Armijo charged a flat five hundred dollars on every wagon, large or small, so that traders took to the largest wagons they could find. Gregg also records that fords were marked out with stakes, and that in a New Mexican letter respect was measured by the width of the left margin, a fourth of the page for an equal and nearly half for a superior. By 1839 New Mexican merchants were sending their own wagons to Missouri.

The yearly feast of a village's patron saint, the función, was in the charge of a mayordomo who served for a year and bore its costs; in New Mexico towns such as Bernalillo a new family still becomes the mayordomos of the fiesta each year. Don Ygnacio, Tomás Padilla and tío Chano are invented, and so is the custom of uncovering in the ford. The four dichos are real. They are quoted as Aurelio M. Espinosa took them down in Santa Fe and Albuquerque and printed them in "New-Mexican Spanish Folk-Lore: Proverbs" (Journal of American Folk-Lore, 1913), where they are numbers 162, 386, 382 and 591. A proverb in New Mexico, Espinosa wrote, "is considered the final word on any subject."
