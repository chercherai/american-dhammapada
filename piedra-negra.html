Piedra Negra

You can do so very frequently, since the nature of the punishment incurred always corresponds to that of the fault committed.
— Allan Kardec, The Spirits’ Book, 399

Así lo oí yo, in Piedra Negra.

I. The Rock

The south coast of Puerto Rico · 1994

Celestino Vega lived by himself in a wooden house with a zinc roof on the hill above the cane, beside the black rock the barrio was named for. The rock was as big as a bus and older than anybody’s story about it. Below it the cane ran down to the sea in every direction, or what was left of the cane, because the central had closed the year before and nobody was cutting it now, and the fields were going back to guinea grass and acacia, and in the dry months they burned.

He had cut cane for the central for thirty-one years. He was sixty-two. He had a machete scar across the back of his left hand and a way of standing with his weight on one hip, from all those years of bending, and he wore a white guayabera every day of his life that was not a working day. Since the central closed, every day was one of those.

On Thursday nights he sat at the white table in the Centro Espiritista Luz y Verdad, down in the town, and went over.

That was how people in Piedra Negra said it. He closed his eyes and put his scarred hand flat on the white cloth beside the glass of water, and his breathing changed, and he went over to where the dead are, and walked among them, and came back and told the people at the table what he had seen. He went to the ones in the light and asked them what they had done in life to be there. A woman who had given water from her well to anybody who knocked. A man who had raised his dead brother’s six children and never once said so. He went down into the dark too, which the other mediums would not do, among the ones who were suffering, the ones who did not yet know they were dead, and asked them what they had done. A man who beat his wife every Saturday for twenty years. A man who sold a neighbor’s land out from under him with a forged paper. A woman who had left a baby. And he came back and told it all at the table, plainly, the way you tell what you saw at the market, with names when the dead gave him permission, and people went home and changed how they lived.

He took nothing for it. That was the rule of the centro and of every proper centro since Kardec, that a gift given free is to be given free, and he kept it harder than anybody. People left things anyway. They left flowers on his porch, and coffee in paper bags, and envelopes with ten and twenty dollars in them at the centro for the soup kitchen and the funeral fund and the light bill, and on Thursday nights the line for the white table went out the door and down the steps and along the sidewalk past the panadería.

He had been brought along, as they say, by Doña Monserrate Ayala, the presidenta of Luz y Verdad. She was eighty-four years old. When she was a girl she had embroidered handkerchiefs at home for the American trade by the dozen, by lamplight, for a few cents apiece, and she still had the eyes for it, small and black and missing nothing. She had seen Celestino at fifteen, the year his fits started, when the priest wanted to send him to the hospital in Río Piedras, and she had said, That boy is not sick. That boy has faculties. And she had sat with him at the white table every Thursday for most of fifty years.

She talked less than anybody in the centro. She opened the session with the prayer and closed it with the prayer, and in between she watched, and if something went wrong at the table she put her hand on the cloth, and it stopped going wrong.

II. The Botánica

Calle Comercio · February

There were three men in the back of Botánica Los Tres Reyes after closing, among the plaster saints and the bundles of herbs and the bottles of bath oil with names on them like Abre Camino and Vence Batalla and Dinero Rápido, and they were adding up what they had lost.

Wiso Medina owned the botánica. Papo Rentas had a little centro of his own in the back of his house where the spirits charged forty dollars for a cleansing and a hundred and fifty for a work against an enemy. The third was a man from the next town who called himself Hermano Ezequiel and sold prayers by the week. Business had been bad for a year. Not everyone who sold herbs and candles on that coast was a thief; most of them were not; there was a woman with a botánica two streets over who sent half her customers up the hill to Luz y Verdad and told them to keep their money. But these three had been living on the fears of the people of that coast for a long time, and the fears had gone up the hill.

It’s him, said Wiso. It’s all him. He goes over there and comes back and tells them who’s in the light and who’s in the dark and why, and they believe him. He tells them for nothing. And then they don’t need us.

So what do we do?

He’s sixty-two years old, said Wiso. He lives alone up on the hill.

Nobody said anything for a while. Then Hermano Ezequiel said, That’s the way, and they all three said it, one after the other, and it was decided.

They went around to the people who still came to them, and to the men who owed them, and they raised a thousand dollars. They took it in an envelope to the basketball court behind the caserío Las Gardenias, where the boys who were on the needle sat on the bleachers in the afternoon waiting for the evening, and they found one called Chiqui, who had four others with him.

The old man at Piedra Negra, Wiso said. The medium. Go up there and finish him.

The envelope was very thick. Chiqui counted it twice, and said yes, and the other four said yes, and they went.

III. The Keyhole

Piedra Negra · February and March

They came up the hill the first time on a Tuesday night with a car jack handle and two lengths of pipe and a baseball bat, and surrounded the house, one at each corner and Chiqui at the door, and Chiqui put his shoulder to the door and broke it in.

The house was empty. The bed had been slept in. The coffee pot on the stove was still warm. The windows were shut and barred from inside, as they always were, and the back door was bolted from inside, and the front door had been locked from inside with the key still in it until Chiqui broke it.

Doña Lula Pabón, who lived below on the cane road and was up at that hour with her sugar, told it afterward to anybody who would listen. She had seen the light go off in his window and the boys come up around the house in the dark. And she had seen, at the front door, at the height of a man’s waist, something come out of the keyhole like a thread of smoke from a cigar, white in the moonlight, and stretch out long and thin along the porch rail, and go down the steps and away across the cane toward the town. She said it was him. Half the barrio believed her and the other half said she should check her sugar again. Celestino, when he was asked, said only that he had spent the night in town.

They came back three weeks later, on a Saturday, and surrounded the house again. This time they did not break in the door. They waited until they could hear him inside, moving around, the radio on, a novela, and then all five of them went in at once, through the door and through the back and through the window over the sink.

He was not in the room. But one of the five, a boy called Tato, who was standing by the bed, would say years later in prison, where people say a great many things, that he had looked up, and seen one of the zinc sheets of the roof lift on its nails at one corner like the page of a book, and something white go up out of the gap into the night, very fast, the size and shape of a man in a white shirt. The sheet came down again. The radio went on playing. The novela was the one about the twins.

The barrio talked about nothing else. Two times. Two months. People came up the hill to look at the keyhole and to look at the roof. Children dared each other to put an eye to the keyhole, and when they did they could see straight through the house, through the front room and the kitchen and out the window over the sink, to the black rock behind and the light on the cane beyond it, and nothing else at all.

IV. The Third Month

Piedra Negra · April

In April he did not leave.

He knew they were coming. Everybody in Piedra Negra knew they were coming; the boys from Las Gardenias had been spending Wiso’s money all over the south coast and talking while they spent it. Celestino went to the centro that Thursday as usual and sat at the white table and went over and came back and told what he had seen, and afterward in the doorway Doña Monse took hold of his sleeve.

Celestino. Stay in town tonight. Stay at my house.

He put his hand over hers on his sleeve, the scarred one.

No, Doña Monse. This one I owe. I’ve known it since I was a boy. I didn’t know what for, but I knew it was owed. I can’t keep going out through the roof.

She looked at him a long time with her small black eyes, and let go.

He went home and made coffee, and sat on the porch in his white guayabera with the cup on the rail and the light on behind him, and watched the cane road. Doña Lula, who was up with her sugar, saw him there. She said afterward he sat very straight, the way he sat at the white table, with his hands open on his knees.

They came at one in the morning. They did not have to break anything this time. He stood up when he saw them on the steps and put the cup down.

What they did to him took a long time, and there were five of them, and they had been paid. They used the pipes and the jack handle and the bat and their boots, and when they were done they kept on, because there is a point where men doing a thing like that stop being able to stop. People in the barrio said afterward that they broke every bone in him, that they broke the bones small, as small as grains of rice, and maybe that was the barrio talking and maybe it was not. When they had finished they thought he was dead. They dragged him around behind the black rock into the guinea grass, where he could not be seen from the road, and left him there, and went down the hill and drove away in Chiqui’s cousin’s car.

Doña Lula heard it. She called the police from the phone in her kitchen. Nobody came until morning. That was the year the government had sent the police and the National Guard into the caseríos with rifles and helicopters and fences, and on the south coast at one in the morning there was nobody left to send up a hill for an old man.

V. The Glass of Water

Centro Espiritista Luz y Verdad · the same night

There was a session at Luz y Verdad that night after all, a late one, because Doña Monse had not gone home. She had sat down at the head of the white table at eleven with the light off in the front room and two of the older mediums with her, and said the prayer, and waited.

At half past one the door opened and Celestino came in.

All three of them would swear to it as long as they lived, and two of them swore to it to the police. He was in his white guayabera. It was raining outside, the first rain in a month, and he was dry. He walked straight, without the hitch at the hip. He came up the room to the head of the table and bent and kissed Doña Monse’s hand, which he had never once done in fifty years.

Doña Monse. I’m going.

You’re going, Celestino?

Yes.

Where to?

To Piedra Negra.

She looked at him the way she had in the doorway, and nodded once.

Then give us the session before you go, she said. Because I am not going to have another one like you to look at.

He sat down in his place at the white table and put his hand flat on the cloth beside the glass of water.

What happened then the two old mediums told to the police and in the centro and to their grandchildren, and they told it the same way every time. The water in the glass rose. It stood up above the rim of the glass the height of a finger, round and clear, like a lens, and did not spill, and there was a light in it that did not come from anywhere in the room. The white cloth moved as if a wind were under it. And Celestino went over, with his eyes open this time, and did not come back for two hours, and the whole of that time he talked. He told them where everybody was. Their mothers. The children they had lost. The men they had hated. Who was in the light and why, and who was in the dark and why, and how long the dark would last for each of them, and that it would not last forever for any of them, not one. He talked as if he were walking through a big house with every door open, telling them what was in each room.

At half past three he stopped. The water went back down into the glass. He stood up, and kissed Doña Monse’s hand again, and went out into the rain and down the steps, and the rain did not wet his shirt, and he walked away up the street toward the cane.

Doña Monse sat at the head of the table until it was light. Then she went home and put on her good black dress.

They found him behind the rock at seven in the morning, in the guinea grass, in the rain. The forensic doctor said he had been dead since about one.

VI. The Cafétín

The old cane road · May

It went all over the island. It was in the San Juan papers with a photograph of the rock. Medium beaten to death at Piedra Negra.

Teniente Edgardo Rosa of the district police had been at the white table at Luz y Verdad exactly once, three years before, after his son Eddie was shot outside a club in Ponce. Celestino had gone over and come back and told him where Eddie was and why, and some of it was a thing about Eddie that the lieutenant had not known and did not want to know, and some of it was a message, in words only Eddie used. The lieutenant had never gone back. He had never forgotten it either. When he heard about Piedra Negra he put two men in plain clothes into the cafétíns along the cane road with orders to play dominoes and drink Medalla and listen.

It took three weeks. The boys from Las Gardenias were in a cafétín called El Guayacán on a Friday night, spending the last of Wiso’s money, and Chiqui shoved Tato off his stool, and Tato got up off the floor with his lip bleeding.

What did you hit me for?

Because you’re a thief and a liar, said Chiqui. You hit the old man first and you said it was me.

You don’t know who hit him first. You were behind the bat.

I hit him first. Me. With the jack handle.

You hit air with the jack handle. I hit him.

And then all five of them were shouting it, standing up, knocking over the beer, I hit him, it was me, I hit him first, the way boys will fight over who scored, and the two men at the domino table in the corner looked at each other and put down their tiles.

The lieutenant had the five of them brought into his own office at the precinct. He asked them one question each. Was it you that killed the old man at Piedra Negra? Each of them said yes, because by then each of them was proud of it. Then he asked who paid. They told him that too.

What happened after that was never in El Vocero.

The five boys were never booked. Wiso Medina and Papo Rentas and Hermano Ezequiel were picked up by men in a car with no markings on a Tuesday night, and the botánica on Calle Comercio burned down on Wednesday, with the plaster saints in it and the bottles of Abre Camino and Dinero Rápido exploding one after another in the heat like firecrackers, and the fire department took forty minutes to come. Their wives went to the precinct and were told there was no record.

The people who lived along the old cane road said, and still say, that on that Tuesday night there was a fire in the dead cane at the colonia they called Las Ochenta, out past the last of the irrigation ditches, where nobody had cut for two years. Cane fires were nothing that dry season. But this one had trucks around it before it started, and men standing in the light of it with rifles, and people say that eight men were put into the dry irrigation ditch there, waist deep, and the dead cane trash was piled over them and lit. And that the next week a contractor came with a tractor and a disc harrow and ran it over that whole field, over and over, back and forth, in the middle of the dry season, when there was nothing to plant.

Nobody was ever charged. The newspapers said eight men from the south coast had disappeared, and that some people believed they had gone to New York.

Doña Monse went to see Chiqui’s mother in Las Gardenias, through the fence and past the Guard at the gate, and sat with her in her kitchen for an afternoon. She went to see the others too, one at a time, all eight. Nobody knew what she said to them. When people in the centro asked her about the fire, all she would do was read them one answer out of the book on the white table, and close it.

Is murder a crime in the sight of God?

Yes, a great crime; for he who takes the life of his fellow-man cuts short an expiation or a mission; hence the heinousness of his offence.

— Allan Kardec, The Spirits’ Book, 746

VII. The Debt

Luz y Verdad · a Thursday in June

The centro was full every Thursday that summer, and none of the mediums could do what he had done, and everybody knew it, and at the end of the sessions people stayed and talked.

He didn’t deserve it, they said. A man like that. That death. He never hurt anybody in his life.

Doña Monse was putting away the white cloth. She folded it in half, and in half again.

If you look at this life only, she said, no. He didn’t deserve it.

They waited. She did not often say more than that, and they did not expect her to. But she put the cloth down on the table and sat down and put her hand flat on it, the way Celestino used to, and closed her eyes. And then she told them, plainly, the way you tell what you saw at the market.

A long time ago, she said, before any of them were born, in the year the Americans came, there was a young man up in the coffee mountains above Yauco who took care of his father and mother with his own two hands. They were both blind. He picked coffee on shares and worked his own little plot and came home every night and cooked for them and washed their clothes and led them out into the sun in the morning and back in at night. His parents said, Son, you are killing yourself, let us find you a wife. And he said, As long as you live, I will take care of you myself. But they kept on at him, and in the end they found him a wife and brought her home.

For a few weeks she was good to the old people. Then she could not stand the sight of them. She began, when he was out in the coffee, to throw ashes and coffee husks and the starchy water from the rice all over the floor of the house, and when he came home she would say, Look what your blind old people have done. Look at the filth. I cannot live in the same house with them. She said it every night. And in the end, she wore him down, a good man, a man who had done everything right until then, and he turned against his own father and mother.

Never mind, he told her. I’ll take care of it.

Those were the months when the night bands were in the mountains, the ones they called tiznados, who came down on the haciendas and the stores. He told his parents that their cousins in another barrio wanted to see them, and put them in the oxcart, and drove them out on the mountain road. Deep in the woods he gave the reins to his father. Hold these, Papá. The oxen know the road. This is where the tiznados wait for people. I am going to get down and look. And he went into the trees, and blacked his face with charcoal from an old fire, and began to shout, first one voice and then many voices, louder and louder, until it sounded like a whole band coming down through the woods. And his father and mother, sitting blind in the cart, called out to him: Son! Son, we are old! Save yourself! Don’t worry about us, run! And he came out of the trees with his face black and beat them to death with a stick, and threw them down into the ravine, and drove the cart home.

The centro was very quiet.

That was Celestino? somebody said finally.

That was the spirit that came back as Celestino, said Doña Monse. He paid for it a long time in the dark first. Then he came back, again and again, and every time it was paid the same way, with a stick, in the dark, by people he never did anything to. This was the last time. He knew. He didn’t know what for, but he knew it was owed. That’s why he didn’t go out through the roof.

She opened the book that always lay on the white table, the old Kardec with its cover held on by tape, and found the place without looking for it, and read.

The vicissitudes of corporeal life being at once an expiation of the faults of the past and lessons for the future, can we, from the nature of those vicissitudes, infer the character of our preceding existence?

You can do so very frequently, since the nature of the punishment incurred always corresponds to that of the fault committed.

— Allan Kardec, The Spirits’ Book, 399

Then she closed it, and got up, and finished folding the cloth.

•••

Nobody has lived in the house at Piedra Negra since. The government took the land along with the rest of the cane, and nobody has come to build anything on it yet. The door still has the lock Chiqui broke and nobody fixed.

The children still climb the hill on dares to put their eye to the keyhole. You can see straight through the house from there, through the front room and the kitchen and the window over the sink, to the black rock behind it, and past the rock to the cane going down to the sea. Toward evening the sun gets low enough to come all the way through, and lays a little round coin of light on the cheek of whoever is looking, and they come running back down the hill with it still on their faces.

In the commentary, Mahā Moggallāna, one of the Buddha’s two chief disciples, visits the heavens and the hells and comes back to tell people what deeds lead where, and the offerings flow to the Buddha’s community. Rival ascetics pay a thousand coins to bandits to kill him at Black Rock. Twice he escapes, once through a keyhole and once through the roof, but in the third month he stays, knowing an old deed has come due. The bandits break his bones as small as grains of rice and leave him for dead. He pulls himself together, flies to the Buddha to take leave, teaches and works wonders one last time, and returns to Black Rock to die. The killers are caught when they brawl in a tavern over who struck him first, and King Ajātasattu has them and the ascetics burned in pits and plowed under. The Buddha explains that in a past life Moggallāna, turned by his wife against his blind parents, beat them to death in a forest while pretending to be bandits. The south coast of Puerto Rico, 1994

Espiritismo came to Puerto Rico in the nineteenth century through the books of the French writer Allan Kardec, and grew into many forms, from strict Kardecist centros to folk practice mixed with Catholic, African and Taíno elements. At the mesa blanca, the white table, a medium hosts a spirit so that those seated can ask it questions. Kardec’s The Spirits’ Book (1857), written as questions put to spirits and their answers, teaches that spirits are reborn many times and that the trials of one life answer to the faults of earlier ones. Celestino’s past life falls in 1898, after the United States invaded Puerto Rico, when night bands called partidas sediciosas, or tiznados, raided the haciendas and stores of Spaniards in the mountains of the interior.

Sugar had ruled the south coast for a century. The great mill at Central Aguirre, between Salinas and Guayama, ran from 1899 until it closed for good in 1993, and by then the industry had been failing for decades. In 1993 Governor Pedro Rosselló began the anti-crime campaign called Mano Dura, the hard hand. Between June 1993 and March 1999 police and the National Guard carried out about eighty raids on public housing projects, each followed by an occupation that lasted weeks, with checkpoints, helicopters and fences around the projects, as the scholar Marisol LeBrón describes in the Boston Review.
