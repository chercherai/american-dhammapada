The Seed on the Needle

I must behave myself like unto the lotus flower whose purity is not defiled by the mud.
— Sutra of Forty-two Sections 29

Iwa gachi naneun deureotda, off Route 29.

I. Red

Fairfax, Virginia · August 2018

Lee Ha-eun watched the fourth rally on her phone in the kitchen of her apartment, with the sound low because her roommate was asleep. It was Saturday afternoon in Seoul and two in the morning in Fairfax. Seventy thousand women sat on the pavement outside Hyehwa Station in a heat wave, nearly all of them in red, holding signs. One of the signs said MY LIFE IS NOT YOUR PORN. Another said OUR RAGE IS HOTTER THAN THE HEAT WAVE. They were chanting about hidden cameras, in toilets and changing rooms and motel rooms, and about the police, who did not find the men who put them there.

Ha-eun was twenty-six and in her second year of a social work degree at George Mason. She had a red T-shirt on. She had put it on when she got up to watch, without thinking, and then had thought about it.

She was sixteen the fall her uncle died. In her mother's house the story was that Jang-ho samchon had been killed in an accident in the country, on a farm road in the rain, and that the nun from the Centreville temple had something to do with it, and that it was not to be talked about. Her mother had not set foot in a temple since. Ha-eun had found out the rest at nineteen, from a girl at a church retreat who had seen the emails, and she had gone home and sat in her car in the driveway for an hour and not gone in.

That August she was helping two friends put together a zine about the rallies for the Korean American students' group, with interviews. One of the friends, Grace Paik, said what about the nun.

She won't talk to us, Ha-eun said. Why would she talk to us? I'm his niece.

Then she'll talk to you, Grace said.

II. The Townhouse

Centreville · the same month

The townhouse was an end unit off Route 29, with a square of yard, a bus stop at the corner, and a candle in the front window that was not lit in the daytime. Two nuns lived there that year, Cheongryeon and a younger one who was at the temple. Cheongryeon opened the door herself. She was forty-six, in summer grays, with her head new-shaved and a gardening glove on one hand.

She listened to Ha-eun say who she was, standing on the step with the two friends behind her and her face hot. Then she took off the glove and said come in, it's too hot out here.

She made barley tea and set it on the low table and sat on a cushion. Ha-eun had written out what she wanted to say and did not look at the paper. She said she was sorry. For her uncle, for her family, for her mother, for the ten years nobody had said it.

Thank you, Cheongryeon said. That's kind of you. It isn't yours.

Grace had the recorder on. She asked whether Sunim had seen the rallies. Cheongryeon had. She said the young women in Seoul were right, and that the police there should listen to them, and the police here.

Are you angry? Grace asked.

Cheongryeon thought about it. I was, a little, for a day. At the deputy. He didn't know where to look.

Not at him. At the man who did it.

He's dead, she said.

The other friend, Min, who was studying to be a therapist, asked carefully whether she ever thought about it. In the shower, say. Whether it came back.

Sometimes I remember the light fixture, Cheongryeon said. The way you remember a street you used to live on.

Min asked one more thing, and blushed while she asked it, because her own mother went to that temple. Whether Sunim still felt things, the ordinary things. Whether she had ever wanted anybody. Whether she still did.

Cheongryeon did not blush. Things come up, she said. They don't stay.

III. The Argument

Fairfax · that night

They argued about it until one in the morning at Ha-eun's kitchen table, with the recorder off and a bag of shrimp chips going soft.

Min said it was dissociation. She said it kindly. It was the textbook picture, she said: the flat calm, the street-you-used-to-live-on, the not being angry at a man who did that to you. People survived by stepping out of themselves, and some of them never stepped back in, and a temple was the perfect place to never step back in, because everybody would call it holiness.

Grace said maybe it was the opposite, that she was performing it. That a nun had to be calm in front of the congregation the way a pastor's wife had to smile, and they had sat there and gotten the performance.

Ha-eun's roommate's boyfriend, who had come out of the bedroom for water and stayed, said with respect he didn't buy any of it. She was a human being. She wasn't a stone Buddha in a garden. Human beings had bodies, they wanted things, they got angry, they liked things, and anybody who said they didn't was either lying or broken. Things come up, they don't stay, he said. Come on. Everything stays.

Ha-eun didn't say anything. She was thinking about the candle in the window, and the gardening glove, and how Sunim had said it isn't yours, and whether that was true.

IV. The Needle

Annandale · September 2018

The old cook from the Centreville temple was ninety that year and lived with her son's family in a split-level off Ravensworth Road. She had stopped cooking at the temple at eighty-five, when her knees went, and now she sat in a chair by the window of the family room with a sewing basket and a jade plant and mended everything the family owned whether it needed mending or not. Ha-eun's grandmother had known her in the old days. It took two phone calls to get invited.

The cook was sewing a button onto her great-grandson's school shirt when Ha-eun came in. On the windowsill was a jar of small round brown seeds that her son had saved from the gat he grew along the fence, the bitter red-leafed mustard for kimchi, to plant again in the spring.

Ha-eun told her everything. Min's word, and Grace's, and the boyfriend's. She said she did not know which of them was right and she did not know why it mattered to her so much, except that it did.

The cook bit off the thread. She did not answer for so long that Ha-eun thought she had not heard.

Then she unscrewed the jar and took out one seed between her thumb and finger, and held the needle up point first, and set the seed on the point. It sat there for half a second. Then it rolled off and went down into the carpet, and was gone.

She did it again with another seed. It went off the other side.

Did the needle throw it? she said.

Ha-eun said no.

Did the needle hold on?

No.

Is there anything wrong with the needle?

Ha-eun laughed, because she was close to crying. No, Halmeoni.

The seed comes, the cook said. The needle doesn't push it. The needle doesn't hold it. It doesn't stay, because there's no place on a needle for it to stay. She threaded the needle again, squinting, and found a second loose button. The boy is right, she's not a stone. Put a seed on a stone, it sits there till it rots. That's a stone.

She sewed for a while.

There's a sutra, she said. The nuns read it at night where I cooked. It tells the monks how to look at a woman. Like their mother, like their sister. Like the lotus. She said the line in Korean, the way she had heard it every night for thirty years, and Ha-eun looked it up afterward on her phone in the car, in an English translation from 1906, and found that it said what the cook had said.

I am now a homeless mendicant. In the world of sin, I must behave myself like unto the lotus flower whose purity is not defiled by the mud.

— Sutra of Forty-two Sections 29

They wrote it for the monks, the cook said. The monks needed it. She didn't need it written down.

When Ha-eun left, the cook was bent over the carpet with her hand flat, feeling for the seeds. She found one. The other she did not find.

•••

The zine came out in October with an interview that was mostly about the police. In March the cook's daughter-in-law, watering the jade plant by the sewing chair, found a mustard seedling coming up at the edge of its pot, two round leaves and then a third with a jagged edge, already going red at the rim. She left it there. Nobody in that house pulled anything up.

Background

The verse

Him I call indeed a Brahmana who does not cling to pleasures, like water on a lotus leaf, like a mustard seed on the point of a needle.Dhammapada 401, tr. F. Max Müller (1881)

This short commentary story is a second telling of the episode in 5.10, after the nun Uppalavaṇṇā has been assaulted. Some of the monks talk it over among themselves: the awakened are not dry trees or anthills, they say, but beings of flesh and blood, so surely they too still take pleasure in the senses. The Buddha answers that they do not. Pleasures do not cling to such a person, any more than water clings to a lotus leaf or a mustard seed stays on the point of an awl, and he speaks the verse. Here the nun is the same woman as in Honey on the Knife, ten years on, and, as there, the violation is secret filming rather than assault. Read the full commentary story, or about Uppalavaṇṇā.

Seoul and Northern Virginia, 2018

In 2018 hidden-camera crime, called molka in Korean, became the subject of the largest women's protests in South Korean history. Beginning in May, women gathered every few weeks near Hyehwa Station in Seoul, most of them dressed in red, under signs reading "My Life Is Not Your Porn." The August 4 rally, held in a heat wave, drew about 70,000 people; one sign read "Our Rage Is Hotter Than The Heat Wave." South Korean police recorded some 6,800 reports of spycam crimes that year.

Northern Virginia's Korean community is centered in Annandale, in Fairfax County. Gat, the Korean leaf mustard used for a pungent kimchi, is a form of brown mustard (Brassica juncea), whose small round seeds are the mustard seed of the kitchen. The cook's line is from the Sutra of Forty-two Sections, traditionally the first Buddhist sutra translated into Chinese, in D. T. Suzuki's English (1906). All the people in the story are fictional.
