The Blue-Belly

We never understand these things until we experience them.
— Vivekananda, Raja Yoga (1896)

So I heard it told, on the far side of Mount Hamilton.

I. Empty Mr. Pottle

Chicago · winter 1903

Edwin Pottle had the whole Bhagavad Gita by heart in Sanskrit, seven hundred verses, and if you named a chapter and a number he would give you the verse, and then give it to you again in Telang’s English and again in Arnold’s, and tell you where each of them had gone wrong. He was forty-six years old and unmarried, an instructor in Sanskrit at the University on the Midway, and the author of The Yoga Aphorisms of Patañjali, Rendered into English, with a Running Commentary, which had been printed in Boston in 1899 and was going into a second edition. On Sunday afternoons he lectured in a hall on Michigan Avenue, and five hundred people paid a quarter to hear him. On weeknights he had eighteen parlor classes, from Prairie Avenue to Evanston, where women in shirtwaists and a few patient husbands sat in a ring of dining chairs while he took them through the eight limbs of the practice in order: restraint, observance, posture, breath, withdrawal, holding, meditation, absorption.

He explained the breath very well. He drew the nostrils on a slate. He had never done it.

The Swami had come to Chicago for the Parliament of Religions in 1893, as the companion of an older monk who afterward went home, and he had stayed. He lived in two rooms over a harness shop on Thirty-first Street and cooked rice and lentils on a gas ring. He wore ochre cotton under a secondhand overcoat that came to his ankles. Nobody could say how old he was. He laughed easily and said very little, and the people who went to sit with him in the front room, on the floor, on a carpet with the pattern worn off, came out quieter than they went in. Pottle had found him in 1896. Every Wednesday since, after his last recitation of the day, he had ridden the Cottage Grove cable car north to drink a cup of tea with the Swami and tell him about the week: how many had come on Sunday, what had been asked, and what he had answered.

On a Wednesday in January the Swami opened the door and said, Come in, empty Mr. Pottle.

Pottle laughed. He supposed it was a turn of English the Swami had not got right, empty for hungry, perhaps. He came in and stamped the snow off.

Sit, empty Mr. Pottle. The Swami poured. Sugar, empty Mr. Pottle?

They talked about the Sunday lecture, which had been on the gunas. At nine Pottle stood and put on his coat, and the Swami saw him to the top of the stairs and said, Good night, empty Mr. Pottle, and as Pottle went down past the dark harness shop he heard the Swami say it once more, to the room, to nobody: Empty Mr. Pottle has gone.

The next Wednesday it was the same. And the next. In February he brought the proofs of the second edition, a hundred and forty pages in long galleys, and the Swami turned them over slowly on the carpet, one after another, nodding, and said, Very fine, empty Mr. Pottle. Very fine.

Pottle rode the cable car home that night with the galleys on his knees. He was not a vain man, or he did not think he was, and he made himself go over it honestly, the way he would go over a corrupt line of a manuscript. He had the Gita. He had the principal Upanishads and the commentaries on four of them. He had Patañjali in the Sanskrit and in his own English, nearly two hundred aphorisms, every word weighed. He taught eighteen classes. Five hundred people on Sunday. And the Swami called him empty.

Somewhere around Twenty-second Street he stopped arguing and counted. In seven years he had explained the first lesson of the practice to perhaps two thousand people: sit for a time, and let the mind run on, and watch it. He had never sat for ten minutes together. He had always meant to. He had meant to begin after the first edition, and then after the Sunday lectures were established, and then after the second edition. He had read every word ever written about the water and had never once put his hand in it.

He got off the car at the next corner and walked the rest of the way home in the snow, and that night he wrote a letter to the head of his department and another to each of his eighteen hostesses, and he put two shirts and a razor and a tin of tooth powder in a valise. He did not pack a book.

II. The Day Coach

Westbound out of the Wells Street depot · March

He took the morning train west in the day coach, because a man going to learn something ought not to go by Pullman. The car was cold and smelled of oranges and wet wool. A man across the aisle in a brown derby nodded to him at Clinton, Iowa, and offered him a newspaper at Cedar Rapids, and somewhere on the flat white country past Council Bluffs Pottle understood that it was Mr. Hapgood.

Mr. Hapgood had been in the Thursday class at a house in Kenwood for three winters. He sold gloves at Field’s. He was the slowest student Pottle had. He had never once got the eight limbs in the right order, and when he tried to say pratyāhāra it came out as something like a sneeze, and the ladies of the Thursday class were kind to him in the way that is not kind. He did not know Pottle at all. He had only ever seen him standing up, behind a little lectern, with a pointer.

They rode together for three days. Mr. Hapgood was going to his married sister in Oakland, for his chest. He talked about gloves, the weights of kid and the weights of chamois, and about the Platte River, which he had expected to be bigger. On the second night, somewhere in Wyoming, with the lamps turned down, he said that he went to a class on Thursdays, Hindu philosophy, very deep, mostly over his head.

Who is the teacher? Pottle said.

Fellow named Pottle. Wonderful talker. I couldn’t keep the half of it. Mr. Hapgood looked out at the dark. There was one thing I could manage. He said it the first night and I don’t believe he ever said it again. Sit down and let your mind run, and just watch it. So that’s what I do. Every night, on the edge of the bed, twenty minutes by the alarm clock. It’s the only part I ever got.

Pottle said nothing for a long time. Then he said that it sounded like the right part.

At Oakland they shook hands on the ferry, and Mr. Hapgood went off with his sister, a big woman in a feathered hat, and Pottle took the train down the bay to San Jose and in the morning climbed onto the observatory stage. The driver told him the road had three hundred and sixty-five turns, one for every day, and Pottle, who would once have wanted to count them, looked at the poppies instead. They passed under the white dome on the summit at noon, and on the other side the road went down into a country of oak and chaparral and gold grass where there was nothing to read for twenty miles.

III. Down the Line

Beyond Mount Hamilton · April 1903

Mrs. Harriet Lusk had been the Swami’s first student in Chicago. When her husband died, and the money from the Board of Trade came to her, she bought a hundred and sixty acres in the hills past the observatory, and in the spring of 1900 eleven of the Swami’s people came out with her to live on it. They lived in tents and in one board cabin. They had dug their own well and cut their own wood, and twice a day, at dawn and at dusk, they sat. The Swami came out for one summer to start them and then went back to his rooms over the harness shop, and they kept on without him. They did not write many letters. Pottle had thought of them, when he thought of them at all, as the ones who had left the lectures.

He walked in from the stage road with his valise. Mrs. Lusk was sitting in the shade of a live oak shelling peas into a dishpan. She was sixty-eight. She looked up and knew him at once.

Mrs. Lusk, he said. I’ve come to be taught.

Mr. Pottle. She put down the pod in her hand. We read your Patañjali aloud by the lamp, all last winter. It is we who ought to be taught by you.

Please don’t, he said. Please. Teach me.

She looked at him a while longer. She looked at his collar and his good shoes and the valise. Go and ask Captain Ames, she said, and went back to the peas.

Captain Ames had been master of a lake steamer between Chicago and Mackinac for thirty years. He was splitting oak behind the cabin. He heard Pottle out, leaning on the maul, and said there were people on the place who knew a good deal more about it than an old lake captain did, and sent him to Miss Pruitt. Miss Pruitt, who had taught the sixth grade in Oak Park, was carrying water from the spring in two buckets on a yoke, and she would not put them down to talk, so he walked beside her, asking, and at the cabin door she sent him to Mr. Brandt. Mr. Brandt was a German baker from Halsted Street and was kneading bread on a board in the shade, and he sent him to the Lindqvist sisters, who were mending fence, and they sent him to young Mr. Coyle, who was twenty-two and had been a newsboy and was lying on his back under the wagon greasing an axle.

Each of them was courteous. Each of them said, in one way or another, that Mr. Pottle had written the book. Each of them sent him a little further down. It took the whole afternoon. By the time the sun was low over the ridge he had been sent down the entire place, from the oldest of them to the youngest grown one, and the sending had done something to him that the Swami’s word had only begun. He had stopped explaining who he was.

Mrs. Dolan was the last of the grown ones. She had been a dressmaker on the West Side and had come out a widow with her little girl. She was sitting on an upturned crate turning the collar of a shirt. She listened to him, and her mouth moved a little at one corner, and she pointed with her scissors to the cabin step.

Teresa Dolan was seven. Everyone on the place called her Tessie. She was sitting on the step in the last of the sun, hemming a dish towel cut from a flour sack, with the miller’s red letters still faint in the cloth. As Pottle came up she bit off her thread and wet the end of a new one and threaded her needle without looking at it, the way her mother had taught her, by feel, with her eyes on him.

He took off his hat. He held it in front of him in both hands.

Miss Dolan, he said. Will you teach me?

Tessie considered him. You’re the man that wrote the book, she said. Mama reads it to me when I can’t get to sleep.

Mrs. Dolan, on her crate, bent closer over the collar.

Please, Pottle said. Teach me.

If you’ll do what I say.

Anything you say. If you told me to walk into a fire, I would walk into it.

She thought about this seriously, and stuck the needle into the towel for safekeeping, and pointed down the slope past the corral.

Walk into the tank, she said.

IV. The Tank

The same evening

The stock pond lay below the corral in a fold of the hill, brown water with a skin of gold on it from the low sun, the mud of the bank stamped all over with the hooves of a neighbor’s cattle. Pottle had on a grey worsted suit made for him by a tailor on State Street, a waistcoat, a celluloid collar, and his father’s gold watch on a chain across the front. He did not take anything off. He did not stop at the edge to think about it, which was the first thing in his life, he said afterward, that he had not stopped to think about. He walked down through the mud and into the water and kept walking until it was over his belt, and over his watch, and up to his chest, and so cold that it took his breath out of him in one piece.

Up on the slope everyone had stopped what they were doing. Captain Ames stood with the maul. Mr. Brandt had flour to his elbows. Mrs. Lusk had come out from under the oak with the dishpan still on her hip.

Come out now, Tessie called.

He came out. The water ran out of his cuffs and his trouser legs and his pockets, and his good shoes were two lumps of mud. He climbed the slope and stood in front of the cabin step, dripping on the boards, and waited.

Tessie looked him up and down to see that he had done it properly. Then she told him.

This morning there was a blue-belly on the woodpile, and I chased him, and he went down the squirrel holes on the knoll. There’s six holes up there and they all go to the same place underneath. If you run around all the holes you never get him. He just comes up the one you’re not at. She held up her hand with the fingers spread, and folded them down one at a time. So you put a rock on one, and a rock on one, and a rock on one, and a rock on one, and a rock on one. And you sit by the one he went in. And you don’t look at the others.

The one finger stood up by itself.

And then he comes out where you are.

Pottle stood with the pond water running into his shoes. He knew the aphorism; he had rendered it himself, with a page of commentary, the withdrawal of the senses from their objects, the mind gathered toward. He had given it on a slate to two thousand people. It had never once been anything but words, and now a child had held up five fingers and folded them down, and it was as if someone had lit a lamp in a room he had been living in for twenty years without knowing it had walls.

That’s enough, he said. Thank you. That’s enough.

He went up the knoll in his wet clothes before the light was gone.

V. Six Holes

The knoll · April and May

The knoll was round and bare on top, with wild oats on its shoulders and a single blue oak, and the ground squirrels had worked it over for longer than anyone had lived there. Pottle found six holes close together where the earth was loose and pale. He carried a stone to each of five and set it over the mouth. He sat down by the sixth with his legs crossed in the way he had drawn on so many slates, and his knees told him at once what they thought of it.

The first thing that happened was the Gita. It started up in his head by itself, the first chapter, the two armies drawn up on the field, and ran on verse after verse without being asked. Then it was the Sunday lecture he had not given, on the five sheaths, beautifully organized. Then it was the second edition, a misprint on page forty, a letter he ought to write to the printer. Then it was Mr. Hapgood. Then it was his father, and the watch, which had stopped at seven minutes past six. Then it was a sentence about the mind as a drunken monkey, which he had always delivered well, and which he now watched going by like a man watching a parade he had once been in. He did not chase any of it. He sat by the one hole and let it run, the way the first lesson said, and watched it, and when he caught himself following a thing down some other hole he came back.

The suit dried on him stiff, with a tidemark at the chest. When the dark came, Mrs. Dolan brought up a blanket and a plate of beans and went away without speaking.

He came down to sleep and to eat and to carry water, because everyone carried water, and the rest of the time he sat on the knoll. The others sat at dawn and dusk in a ring under the oak by the cabin, and after the first week he could see them from up there, small and still, and once he understood that they could see him. The squirrels got used to him. A red-tailed hawk worked the slope every afternoon at the same hour, and he heard it before he saw it, and then one afternoon he did not look up at it at all. His knees stopped talking. The Gita came less often and left sooner. Tessie came up some days with her sewing and sat on one of the five stones, the one nearest the oak, and hemmed, and did not say anything either, and threaded her needle by feel.

On an evening in the middle of May he looked up and the Swami was standing in the wild oats below the oak.

He was in the ochre cotton and the long secondhand overcoat, buttoned, as if it were still January on Thirty-first Street. The sun was going down behind Pottle’s back, but the light on the Swami came from in front of him, from somewhere behind the Swami himself, so that the oat heads all around him were lit at their edges and the coat was gold at every seam. He was two thousand miles from his gas ring and his carpet, and he was there, and he smiled at Pottle the way he had smiled over the galleys, and said the words from the Swami Vivekananda’s book, which Pottle had quoted to five hundred people on a Sunday without once hearing them:

Practice is absolutely necessary. You may sit down and listen to me by the hour every day, but, if you do not practise, you will not get one step further. It all depends on practice. We never understand these things until we experience them.

— Vivekananda, Raja Yoga (1896)

Then there was only the oak and the oats, and the light was the ordinary light going down.

Mrs. Lusk said afterward that she had seen something on the knoll that evening, from the ring under the oak, and taken it for the sun on a stone. Captain Ames said he had seen nothing at all and did not need to. Tessie said that of course it was the Swami, who else would it be, and that he had been wearing his coat.

Pottle sat on. The light went off the hill. Below him the sixth hole was a small round dark, no different from the dark coming up out of the valley, and he watched it, and there was nothing between him and the watching anymore, no lectern, no slate, no page of commentary, nothing but the hole and the evening and the one doing the watching, and then not even that.

In the last warmth a blue-belly came up out of the sixth hole and climbed onto the nearest stone. It lifted itself on its front legs, twice, three times, the way they do, and the blue of its belly showed in the last light like a scrap of the sky that had got down there. Pottle did not reach for it. It stayed on the stone until the stone went cold.

He stayed on the place through the summer and the fall. Tessie sewed the button back on his waistcoat, the one the pond had taken, with black thread because there was no grey. In November he went back to Chicago on the day coach, and on the first Wednesday he rode the Cottage Grove car up to Thirty-first Street and climbed the stairs over the harness shop, and the Swami opened the door.

Come in, Mr. Pottle, he said. Sit.

Background

The verse

Through zeal knowledge is gotten, through lack of zeal knowledge is lost; let a man who knows this double path of gain and loss thus place himself that knowledge may grow.Dhammapada 282, tr. F. Max Müller (1881)

In the commentary, the elder Poṭhila knows all three baskets of the teaching by heart and recites them to five hundred monks, but has never practiced. The Buddha begins to greet him every day as “Empty Poṭhila”: come, Empty Poṭhila; sit, Empty Poṭhila; Empty Poṭhila has gone. Stung, Poṭhila leaves at dawn in the company of the least of his students, who do not notice who he is, and travels a hundred and twenty leagues to a forest hermitage of thirty monks who have all reached the goal. He asks the eldest to be his refuge, and is sent to the next, and the next, down to a seven-year-old novice sitting at his sewing. The boy agrees to teach him if he will obey, and tells him to walk into a pond in his costly robes; he does. Then the boy tells him how to catch a lizard that has gone into an anthill with six holes: stop five, and watch the one it went in by. So with the six doors of the senses. Chicago and the hills past Mount Hamilton, 1893–1903

The World’s Parliament of Religions met in Chicago from September 11 to 27, 1893, alongside the Columbian Exposition, in the building that is now the Art Institute. Its best-remembered speaker was the young Bengali monk Vivekananda, who stayed on in America to lecture. His lectures in New York in the winter of 1895–96 became Raja Yoga, published in July 1896: an account of the eight limbs of practice with a free translation of the Yoga Sutras of Patañjali. Its chapter on pratyahara, the “gathering towards” or withdrawal of the senses, tells the beginner to sit and let the mind run on, and watch it. The Swami, Edwin Pottle and the Chicago circle are fictional.

In 1900 Vivekananda’s brother disciple Turiyananda took a dozen or so American students to a gift of 160 acres in the San Antonio Valley, some eighteen miles southeast of Lick Observatory on Mount Hamilton. They lived in tents, hauled their own water and meditated. The place, the Shanti Ashrama, is still a retreat. Mrs. Lusk’s ranch is loosely modeled on it. The hills there are full of California ground squirrels, whose colonies dig burrow systems with many entrances, which other animals use as refuges, and of western fence lizards, which every California child knows as blue-bellies.
