Walking the American WayCommentary 13.2 · Suddhodana

Better Than Sleep

Prayer is better than sleep!
— the call to morning prayer, tr. E. W. Lane

Hayk smi‘naha, on Harlem Avenue.

I. Sedlak’s

Bridgeview, Illinois · April 1990

Hajj Musa Barakat came to Chicago in 1956 with one suitcase, and by 1990 he owned the banquet hall on Harlem Avenue. In between there had been a grocery on Forty-Seventh Street, then three groceries, then five, then a brick two-flat in Gage Park, and, when the stores had gone to his nephews, the hall. He bought it in 1984 from a Bohemian family and left their name on the sign, SEDLAK’S BANQUETS, because the sign was paid for and the Lions Club knew where it was. Inside he changed everything. He hung chandeliers from a hotel sale downtown and put a fountain in the lobby that ran pink on wedding nights, and he had chairs for six hundred. In the southwest suburbs people who had never learned his name called him Abu Tariq, father of Tariq. People who had known the family in Beitunia remembered that his grandfather had been mukhtar of the village under the Turks, and that a Barakat did not carry his own packages.

Tariq was the eldest, born over the first store. At nineteen he went to Cairo to study at al-Azhar for two years, and he stayed nine. Letters came, and then cassettes, carried home by men who had been on the pilgrimage or at a cousin’s wedding in Amman. On the cassettes a young man recited the Qur’an, and people listening in parked cars outside the mosque left the engine running to hear the end of the chapter. By the last years the men on Ninety-Third Street were saying Shaykh Tariq. Abu Tariq let them.

He came home in Ramadan, which fell in April that year. Fourteen cars went to O’Hare. He came out of customs with one bag, a thin man of twenty-eight in a gray robe and the red cap with a white cloth wound around it that the Azharis wear, and the family went at him in a wave. His uncles Ismail and Dawud stayed back by the baggage carts with their arms folded. They had held him at his circumcision. Dawud had taught him to ride a bicycle in the alley off Kedzie. They were glad to see him, and they were not going to kiss the hand of a boy.

He did not come to the house. In the last ten nights of the month a man who is able stays in the mosque. He rode from the airport in his father’s Lincoln to Ninety-Third Street, carried his bag inside, and slept on the carpet among the old men.

II. The Lot

The mosque on Ninety-Third Street · the twenty-seventh night

The mosque stood between the railroad tracks and a trailer park, on the lot the first families had been able to afford, and it held three hundred. On the twenty-seventh night more than a thousand came. Rugs and bedsheets and flattened cartons were laid on the asphalt between the cars. When the night prayers were finished one of the elders asked the visitor to give the lesson.

The uncles had prayed in the front row inside, where they always prayed. They came out and stood at the back of the lot beside Dawud’s Oldsmobile with their hands clasped behind them, like men who mean to listen for a few minutes to be polite.

It is told a few ways. The Barakats tell it one way and the families from the other villages another, and a woman who lived then in the third trailer from the fence, who is not a Muslim, will tell it to anybody. They agree on this much. He began in the ordinary manner, standing on the ground by the door. Somewhere in the first quarter hour he started to walk while he talked, up the lot and back, and he was not on the asphalt. He was as high as the roofs of the cars and then as high as the light poles, going back and forth over the people at an easy pace with his hands behind him, the way a man walks the length of a roof in the evening. There was something under his feet where he walked. Some of the families say it looked like the colored glass of the lamps in an old mosque. The woman from the trailer park says it was like the lit top of a jewelry counter, the whole length of the lot, red and green and a yellow like egg yolk, and that a freight on the tracks behind her stood still until he came down.

He came down by the door. Abu Tariq went forward through the rows in his stocking feet, and in front of everyone he knew he took his son’s right hand, kissed it, and touched it to his forehead, as a son does with a father. Ismail came next. Then Dawud, who was weeping and angry about it. Then everybody.

Then it rained. It came hard for some minutes out of a sky that still had stars along one edge, and people ran for the cars. Afterward, in the doorway, they held out their sleeves to one another. Some were wet through to the undershirt. Others, who had stood shoulder against shoulder with them on the same bedsheet, were dry, and the dry ones were those who had on the new clothes they had bought for the feast. They were loud about it. Tariq, wringing the hem of his robe, said it was not the first rain to fall on this family.

In Beitunia, under the Turks, there was a Barakat who could not keep anything. My father knows which one. The family had a white mule, and in a dry year, wherever that mule was lent for the threshing, rain came after. People from across the wadi asked him for it when their cisterns were dust, and he gave them the halter. So his brothers put him out. He lived in a watchman’s hut in the olives. He gave away the seed wheat they sent up to him, and then his blanket, and in the end the door off the hut, to a man who said he needed a door. When the brothers climbed up to bring him home, because nothing had gone right for them since, it rained on all of them where they stood. That kind of rain. Whoever wanted it got it.

Abu Tariq knew which one. It was his grandfather’s uncle, and the family did not tell that story.

III. Six Hundred Chairs

Sedlak’s Banquets · the same night

It was past one when the lot emptied. Everyone had to be up again before four to eat ahead of the fast, and everyone went home. Each family, pulling out onto Harlem, supposed that a man whose father owned a banquet hall did not need to be asked. Ismail supposed Dawud had asked him. Dawud was still wiping his face. Abu Tariq thought, If my son does not eat at my table, where will he eat? and so he did not ask him either. He drove to the hall.

He had been planning it for a week. The next night’s breaking of the fast was to be the Shaykh’s dinner, for the whole community, at his expense. Twenty lambs had been in the ovens since the evening prayer. Three hundred pounds of rice stood soaking, and the cooks were breaking dried yogurt from Jordan into the big kettles, because it was going to be mansaf and it was going to be remembered. There were sixty round tables of ten, with a pitcher and a dish of dates on each. On the low stage where the bride and groom sat at weddings he had set one table apart with the two gilt chairs, and then he had one of the gilt chairs carried away.

At twenty past three he was in the kitchen with a spoon in the broth when the wall telephone rang.

IV. The Round

Palos Hills · before dawn

Tariq woke on the mosque carpet a little before three. The men who were keeping the ten nights there had their trays from home, brought in by sons and grandsons. Nobody had thought to bring one for him.

He sat a while and considered it. For nine years he had lived in two rooms behind al-Azhar, in the Batniyya quarter, and for nine Ramadans he had walked that quarter at night with its waker. This was an old man called Amm Sayyid, who had the round from his father, who had it from his. The first years Tariq carried the lamp for him. The last years, when the old man’s hands shook, he carried the drum. He asked himself what Amm Sayyid did when he came into the quarter on the first night, and what the wakers before him had done. They did not go first to the house of a relative. They began at the first door of the first lane and went on in order to the last, passing by only a house in mourning, and they ate what was handed out to them.

He took the drum out of his bag. It was most of what was in the bag, a copper bowl no bigger than a soup dish with a skin laced over it, and a leather strap to beat it with. He borrowed the flashlight from the mosque kitchen and walked a mile south in the dark to the subdivision where his father lived.

It was called Fontaine Bluff. It had been built in 1974 by a man who named the cul-de-sacs in French after his daughters, Cour Denise, Cour Annette, Cour Lorraine. At the entrance was a fieldstone sign, and under the name hung a smaller board that the homeowners’ association had voted on, NO SOLICITING.

The first house inside the entrance belonged to a family from Beitunia named Mansour. Tariq stood at the foot of the driveway and beat the drum three times with the strap. It made a small dry sound. Then he called what Amm Sayyid called. The words are old. An Englishman who lived in Cairo in the 1830s heard them under his own window and wrote them down like this: He prospereth who saith “There is no deity but God.” Three beats more. The most happy of nights to thee, O Abu Khalil.

A light came on upstairs. The front door opened and Abu Khalil Mansour stood in it in his undershirt, sixty years old, with his mouth open. He had not heard that drum since he was a boy in his mother’s house. His wife came up behind him with a boiled egg and half a round of bread with oil and thyme on it. Tariq thanked her and ate them standing in the driveway, and went on to the next house.

The next house was Mrs. Gorski’s. She was eighty-one and had been awake since two, as she was every night, and her kitchen light was on. He did not beat the drum for her. He stood for a moment at the end of her walk, as he had been taught to stand a moment at every door, and she came out in her housecoat to see what the noise was.

You’re Mr. Barakat’s boy. The priest.

Something like that.

Have you eaten?

She brought him a slice of poppy seed roll on a paper towel and stood there until it was finished.

So it went, door after door in order. The Hamdans gave him three dates and tea in a paper cup. At a dark house he stood his moment and walked on. A man named Dykstra, just home from the night shift at the corn plant in Argo, was sitting in his truck with the dome light on, finishing his Thermos. He rolled the window down, asked if this was a religious thing, and handed out an orange. The Salehs had cold kibbeh. At a house where the grandfather had died in February Tariq put the strap in his pocket and went by with his hand on his chest. Dr. Aziz the dentist came out angry about the hour and the neighbors, went back in, and came out again with cheese folded in bread.

By Cour Denise a Palos Hills squad car had come up behind him with its headlights off and was following at a walk. By Cour Annette his brother Nabil was there. Nabil was to be married the week after the feast, to a girl from Burbank, and their mother had sent him in his car to bring Tariq home. At some doorstep Tariq had handed him the flashlight, to free a hand for a glass of milk, and walked on. Now Nabil was coming along behind in his good shoes, holding the light, because he could not see how to give it back.

Behind them the windows were lit in order, house by house, and ahead they were dark. People stood on their front steps in bathrobes. Some of the old ones were crying, and some of the young ones wished to be dead. Children in pajamas sat down on the curb. The telephones that ordinarily carried deaths and engagements were carrying this.

V. The Lincoln

Cour Lorraine · ten past four

The call to the hall kitchen was from Dawud. Musa. Your son is going door to door in Fontaine Bluff with a drum, like the waker of some alley in Cairo, and the Polish widow is feeding him cake.

Abu Tariq went out through the lobby past the fountain. He had his camel overcoat half on over his shirtsleeves, and he was still working his arm into the second sleeve when he swung the white Lincoln onto Harlem Avenue. He drove the two miles with the coat bunched behind his shoulder.

He found them on his own court. His house was the fourth on Cour Lorraine, brick, with two concrete lions at the foot of the drive, and his son was standing at the third. Abu Tariq stopped the car in the middle of the turnaround and got out and left the door open, so that the chime went on ringing.

Tariq. He spoke low, in Arabic. Why are you doing this to me? I am ashamed to the ground. In this town it would not be fitting for a son of mine to go from door to door in that car, with a driver. You go on foot and take bread from their hands. Why do you put me to shame?

I am not putting you to shame, Yaba. I am keeping the custom of my line.

Your line. Abu Tariq looked at his own house. Is it the custom of my line to get its bread at doors?

No. That is not the custom of your line. Tariq wiped his fingers on his robe. It is the custom of mine. Amm Sayyid had it from his father, and his father from the wakers before him, back and back to Bilal, who called while it was still night so the people would rise and eat. Not one of them went first to his cousin’s house. They took every door in order, and they lived on what came out of it.

Bilal was a slave.

Yes.

Abu Tariq had come to this country with a suitcase of tablecloths and bedspreads. He had gone up and down the three-flats of Forty-Seventh Street knocking, a dollar down and a dollar a week, at doors that mostly did not open, and he had spent thirty years seeing to it that nobody who mattered would have to remember that. His son had been born over the store the suitcase bought. Tariq had said no, and he left it there.

It was close to the hour. Over on Ninety-Third Street the call would be going up inside the walls, where the neighbors did not hear it. Here there were porch lights, a squad car, and Mrs. Gorski coming up the sidewalk in her housecoat with the rest of the poppy seed roll. Tariq did not raise his voice. He said the end of the dawn call there in the turnaround, which everyone on those steps had heard every morning of their lives before they came to America.

Come to prayer! Come to security! Prayer is better than sleep!

— the call to morning prayer, tr. E. W. Lane (1836)

His father was still standing with one hand holding his coat shut. So Tariq gave him the rest, as the preachers of Cairo give it on a Friday from the pulpit stair.

O people! reverence God by doing what He hath commanded, and abstain from that which He hath forbidden and prohibited. The happy is he who obeyeth, and the miserable is he who opposeth and sinneth. Know that the present world is a transitory abode, and that the world to come is a lasting abode.

— a Friday sermon of Cairo, tr. E. W. Lane (1836)

Abu Tariq stood. Then he reached into the Lincoln and took the key out, and the chime stopped. He put his other arm through its sleeve and buttoned the coat to the neck. He held out his hand to Nabil for the flashlight, and Nabil gave it up gladly.

The officer in the squad car wrote in his log, 4:11, Cour Lorraine, religious observance, no complainant, and backed out of the court.

At the fourth house, the brick one with the lions, Tariq beat the drum three times. The most happy of nights to thee, O Abu Tariq, he called to the man standing beside him with the light. Umm Tariq came down her own front steps with a glass of milk and two dates on a saucer, and her son took them from her hand.

VI. The Drum

Fontaine Bluff · the Ramadans after

The dinner was eaten that night at Sedlak’s. Six hundred chairs were not enough. Abu Tariq sent the busboys with covered trays to the trailer park by the mosque, to Mrs. Gorski, to the man Dykstra, and to the front desk of the Palos Hills police. Tariq ate at a round table by the kitchen door. Nobody sat in the gilt chair.

He stayed for the feast and for Nabil’s wedding, and then he went where a teacher was wanted, to a storefront mosque in another state. He has students now who have students. He left the drum on the hall table in his father’s house.

The next year Ramadan came eleven days sooner, as it does, and on its first night, at a quarter past three, the drum was heard in Fontaine Bluff. It was Abu Tariq in the camel coat, sixty-three years old, alone, starting at the Mansours’ and taking every door in order. He kept it up nine years, until his knees would not do the hill on Cour Denise. He was never any good on the drum. People handed him eggs and bread, and he ate them in the driveways. The homeowners’ association took the matter up once, under Noise, and Mrs. Gorski stood and said she had lived her whole life under church bells, and nobody moved the question. After she died her kitchen was dark, and he stood his moment at the end of her walk all the same.

On that first night alone, when he came to Cour Annette, Dr. Aziz’s mother was at an upstairs window. She was a very old woman from Cairo, visiting on a six-month visa, and she did what the women of her street did when she was a girl. She folded a quarter into a piece of the Southtown Economist and lit the paper with a kitchen match, so that the waker would see where it fell, and let it go. It came down past the gutter, turning, a small fire. Abu Tariq stood over it on the driveway until it had burned out. Then he picked his quarter out of the ash, put it in his coat pocket, and went on to the next door.

The verses

Rouse thyself! do not be idle! Follow the law of virtue! The virtuous rests in bliss in this world and in the next.Dhammapada 168, tr. F. Max Müller (1881)
Follow the law of virtue; do not follow that of sin. The virtuous rests in bliss in this world and in the next.Dhammapada 169, tr. F. Max Müller (1881)

In the commentary, the Buddha makes his first visit home to Kapilavatthu. To break the pride of his kinsmen he walks up and down on a jeweled cloister in the air, and they bow to him, his father the king first. A shower falls on the gathering, and he tells them it has rained on his family before, in the life of the great giver Vessantara. They all go home, and no one invites him to eat; the king, sure his son can eat nowhere else, has food and seats made ready for twenty thousand without asking. Next morning the Buddha considers what the Buddhas before him did and goes from door to door through his father’s city with his bowl. The king hurries out, pulling his cloak straight, and asks why he is being put to shame. The Buddha answers that begging is the custom of his line, the line of the Buddhas, and speaks the two verses. The southwest suburbs of Chicago, 1990

Palestinians in Chicago began as peddlers and shopkeepers on the South Side; by the early 1970s they owned nearly a fifth of the city’s small groceries. In the 1980s many families moved out to Burbank, Oak Lawn, Hickory Hills, Bridgeview and Palos Hills. The Mosque Foundation, begun in 1954 by immigrants from the West Bank village of Beitunia, opened its mosque in Bridgeview in 1981 on a lot between railroad tracks and a trailer park, with a prayer hall for three hundred. The Barakats, Sedlak’s Banquets and the Fontaine Bluff subdivision are invented. Ramadan in 1990 ran from late March to late April.

The night waker of Ramadan, the musaharati or musahhir, walks his quarter before dawn with a small drum to rouse people for the last meal before the fast, greeting householders by name, and is paid in food or in coins at the feast. Tradition names Bilal, the freed Abyssinian slave who was the first caller to prayer, as the first of them (see this account of the trade). Edward William Lane lived in Cairo in the 1820s and 1830s and described the waker in his Account of the Manners and Customs of the Modern Egyptians: the drum beaten three times, the greeting to each man of the house, the house of mourning passed by, and the women who wrapped a coin in paper and set the paper alight before dropping it from the window. The waker’s cry, the words of the dawn call and the passage from a Friday sermon are quoted here in Lane’s English, from the text of his fifth edition (1860).
