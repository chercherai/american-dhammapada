I. The Visiting Preacher

Sand Mountain, Alabama · the fall of 1856

There was a man named Eli Tidmore who had a good farm on the brow and no preacher nearer than the valley, so he built a room onto his house and kept one. The preacher’s name was Harmon Self. He had the room and his washing and a place at the table three times a day, and he preached at the log church two Sundays in the month and thought himself well fixed.

One evening that fall an old man came up the mountain road on foot. He was a preacher too, of what persuasion nobody afterward could say. He had a way of walking and a way of standing still that Tidmore liked the minute he saw them, and Tidmore had him in. He fed him the best there was in the house. He saw the state of the old man’s clothes and brought out a length of new jeans cloth his wife had woven. Have this made up and wear it next to you. He saw the hair down on his collar and sent a boy for the neighbor who barbered. He had his own feather bed carried up to the loft. You lie on this. Then he asked both preachers to breakfast and went to bed himself.

Harmon Self had watched the whole of it from his place at the table. He had eaten at that table two years, and nobody had ever sent for a barber.

That night he climbed to the loft where the old man lay and stood over him with a candle. You would do better to eat dung than eat at Tidmore’s table, he said. You would do better to tear your hair out with a wool card than have it cut by his barber. You would do better to go naked than wear his jeans. You would do better to lie on the bare ground than in his bed.

The old man made him no answer. He thought, Let this fool not come to ruin on my account. Before day he got up and went on down the far side of the mountain toward the river, wherever it was he was going.

In the morning Self swept the yard and drew the water. When it came time to wake the company he tapped on the loft ladder with one fingernail and came away. Where is our brother? Tidmore asked at the table. Still abed, said Self. I have swept and I have drawn water and I have knocked for him, and he has slept through every bit of it.

Tidmore knew better than that, and knew about what must have been said. There was nothing to say that would mend it. He gave Self his breakfast as he did every morning, and then he had his wife fill the dinner bucket with the best of what was left, ham and biscuit and a jar of gravy, and he said, If you come up with him on the road, give him this.

Self took the bucket. He thought, If that old man gets one more taste of this cooking he will be here till Judgment. Half a mile down the road he turned it over in the ditch. Then he went on to the forks and looked for the old man, and did not find him.

That was the deed. The old people on the Mountain said a man could pray twenty years on top of a thing like that and not cover it, and they said Harmon Self did.

II. The Table Rock

1857 to 1912

The boy the Mountain called Jim Buck was born the next spring, in a house with plenty in it: four mules, a full smokehouse, bees. Whose house it was depends on who is telling. Some say Self’s own. Some say Self died that winter with his face to the wall, and the child came to people named Buck across the gulf who had never heard of him.

From the time he could walk he would not lie in a bed and he would not eat at a table. He ate off the ground. A good many children on the Mountain ate a little clay out of the chimney, and nobody thought much about it. This one ate from the ground behind the smokehouse, where the family went, there being no such thing as a privy on Sand Mountain then, and it was not only the ground he ate. His mother and father said, He is a baby. He doesn’t know any better. He got older and it was the same. By then he would not keep a stitch on him, summer or winter, and he slept in the yard like a dog. His people said, He is not fit to live in a house. He is fit for those sanctified folks and nothing else.

The sanctified band were camped that year on the brow, a dozen men who had come out from all the churches and went barefoot and bareheaded and begged their meal in the settlement. They took him. Their way of taking a man was to stand him in a sawpit up to his neck, lay planks across his collarbones and sit on the planks, and pull out his hair with a pair of wool cards. His mother and father gave the band a dinner for it and went home.

The next morning the band said, Come on, we are going down to the settlement. He said, You go. I will stay here. They sent food back up to him and he would not touch it. I don’t need it. I get my own. Where? Right here. It was the same the second day and the third. On the fourth, two of them made as if to go with the rest and then lay down in the laurel and watched. When he thought he was alone he went to the trench the camp used, lifted a board, got down in it, and ate with both hands.

When the band heard, they said, If the Methodists get hold of this they will tell it that this is what we live on. He is not fit for us either. And they put him out.

After that he took up at the table rock back of Shiloh church. It was a flat shelf of sandstone on the brow, in the woods on the men’s side, with a basin worn in the top of it, and for want of any other place it was where men went on meeting days. By night he ate there. By day, when anybody came into the woods, they found a man with a guano sack about his middle, leaning one hand on the rock and standing on one leg with the other foot set against his knee, and his mouth wide open to the wind that came up out of the gulf.

Brother, why do you stand with your mouth open that way?

I am a wind-eater. I take no other food.

And why the one foot?

If I was to set both feet down, this mountain would shake. I don’t sit and I don’t lie down. I have stood since I came here.

People will mostly believe a thing that is said to them plainly by a man who asks nothing for it. Before long both ends of the Mountain were stirred up over him. We never had such a man as that. There never was one. Every fourth Sunday, when there was preaching at Shiloh and singing after it, they carried a plate out to the rock. He would not have it. I eat the wind. If I took any other thing it would undo all I have done. They begged him. Don’t do us that way. If a man like you would take one bite from our hands, it would be the making of us for as long as we live. In the end he would reach down and pull a straw of broom sedge and touch the tip of it to their sorghum or their butter or their honey, and lay the tip of the straw on the tip of his tongue. Go on, now. That will do you.

So he lived, month after month, for fifty-five years, counting from the day he could walk. He went naked or the next thing to it. He ate filth. He pulled out his hair, and he slept on the ground when there was nobody to see.

III. The Glass

Shiloh crossroads · the fourth Saturday in July, 1912

The State Board of Health sent a dispensary up the Mountain that summer, a doctor and a microscopist in a hired hack, with a tin trunk of capsules and a stack of bulletins for the schoolchildren to carry home. The doctor was a Dr. Anders from below Montgomery. The microscopist was a Miss Lura Hyatt, twenty-three years old, whose people were from the far end of the Mountain. She had been as yellow as a gourd herself until she was sixteen. Then a doctor at Albertville gave her thymol, and after it she finished school in two years and went down to the State laboratory to learn the glass.

She saw him at sunup from the hack as they came along the brow: a man on a rock, on one leg, the color of tallow. She had been that color. She knew what he ate as surely as if she had stood and watched him. She thought, If that one would carry a tin box to the table, the whole Mountain would.

They set up at the schoolhouse across the road from the church. People had been told from the pulpit, and they came. They came in wagons and on mules and on foot, with the specimen in a snuff tin or a baking-powder can with the name penciled on a scrap of paper, and they stood in the yard and waited their turn and looked at the worms in the bottle on the exhibit table. Miss Hyatt sat at the window with the light on her mirror and did a hundred and forty slides. She called the doctor over for the first few, and after that she only wrote down a mark, and the mark was nearly always the same one.

At the end of the day she said, I am going up to the one on the rock.

You cannot mean to go up there, said Dr. Anders.

I do mean to.

She went when the shadows were getting long. On her way a shower came over out of a sky with nothing in it. It rained hard on the brow for about as long as it takes to draw a bucket of water, and quit. When she came through the trees, the table rock, which had not been clean in fifty years, was as clean as a washed plate.

She made a little noise with her foot. Jim Buck.

He thought, Who is this that calls me Jim Buck? Who is it?

It’s me. One that has done without.

What do you want?

Give me a place to stay tonight.

There is no place to stay here.

Don’t do that way. Just the one night. Like goes to like. People go to people and brutes to brutes, and one that has done without goes looking for another.

Have you done without?

I have.

Then where is your sack, and your staff, and your Testament?

I have got all of them. They are a trouble to tote from place to place, so I keep them inside, and I take them with me when I go.

That put him out. So you aim to take them with you when you go.

Never mind that. Tell me where I can stay.

There is no place.

Under the brow, not fifty steps off, there was a rockhouse, a dry shelf of sand with the bluff hanging over it. She pointed. Does anybody stay in that?

Nobody.

Then let me have it.

Suit yourself.

IV. Three Lights

The rockhouse · that night

She spread her quilt on the sand and lay down. In the fore part of the night four lights came along the foot of the bluff, one from each way, and stood at the four sides of the rockhouse, and it was light as far as the far wall of the gulf. Jim Buck saw it from his rock. What light is that? At midnight one came by itself that was brighter than the four. Who is that? Toward day there came one that lit the whole woods, so that a man could have counted the leaves. The old people had a saying about that one, that with one finger he lights one world, and with ten fingers ten. Who can that be?

At daybreak he went down to her. He spoke to her civilly and stood off to one side.

Who was it came to you in the fore part of the night, the four of them?

Four that keep the corners of this country.

What did they come for?

To see was there anything I wanted.

And the one at midnight?

He is over those four. He sat and held the lamp for me.

And the one toward day?

That is the one the preachers holler to.

Are you greater than them, then?

They came to me. I did not go to them.

He studied her. You are a wonder, girl. I have lived on this rock since before you were born, eating wind and standing up, and in all that time not one soul has ever come to me of a night with so much as a pine knot.

Jim Buck, she said, you have fooled the people on this mountain, and now you are trying it on me. For fifty-five years you have eaten filth and lain on the ground and gone naked and torn out your hair. And you have told them, wind is my food, I stand on one foot, I do not sit, I do not lie down. It is all a result. It never was the cause of a thing.

The result of what?

She told him what an old woman had told her in the schoolyard the day before, while she waited with her snuff tin: the room built onto the house, the old man on the road, the jeans cloth and the barber and the feather bed, the four things said by candlelight, the bucket turned over in the ditch. She did not say whose father it was or whether it was anybody’s. She said, Every one of the four things he wished on that old man, you have done every day you have lived.

It went into him as she told it. Shame came up in him, and fear at what he had done, and he crouched down on the ground with his arms around his knees. She took off the canvas duster she rode in and tossed it to him, and he put it on. Then he sat down, off to one side of her, on the sand.

She had the glass in its box. She set it on a ledge where the first sun came in, and tilted the mirror. Bring me some of what you ate last night, on a splinter. He brought it. She fixed the slide and stood away. Look.

He looked a long time. What are they?

Eggs. That is what you have been eating since you could walk, and that is what has been eating you. They are in near about every child on this mountain that goes barefoot in the dew back of that church. The ground is full of it. You stood fifty years in the very place it starts. She had one of the school bulletins in her grip. It was made up in questions and answers, like a catechism, so a child could say it over at home, and she said three of them to him.

What is a dirt-eater?
A dirt-eater is a person who has an unnatural appetite, and on this account eats clay, sand, plaster, soot, wood, cloth, or other things not intended for food.

Is dirt-eating the cause of hookworm disease?
It is the result of the disease, not the cause.

Can dirt-eating be cured?
Yes, very easily; by curing hookworm disease.

— Soil Pollution, Questions 40–42

When he raised his eye from the glass he was done with the rock. People who knew him before and after said that was the place it happened, bent over a brass tube in a rockhouse with a girl’s coat on.

What do I do?

She put out her right hand to him. Come on down.

V. Fourth Sunday

Shiloh church · the next morning

It was the day in the month when the Mountain came with its plates. They came out of the church at noon and carried them back through the trees to the table rock, and there he was, with both feet on the ground, in a canvas coat, like any old deacon in from the field, standing beside a young woman who had a brass tube under her arm.

They talked among themselves. Which of them is the greater? If it was her, he would have gone down the mountain to her. She came up the mountain to him. So it must be him.

She heard it. Jim Buck, settle that for them.

That is the very thing I would like best to do, he said.

There are people on Sand Mountain today whose grandmothers stood there and saw it. He went up off the ground to the height of a pine tree and called down, This young woman is my teacher, and I am her scholar, and came down and bowed to her. He went up again to the height of two pines, and of three, and so on until he was seven pines high and small against the sky, and each time he said the same thing and came down.

Then he sat down at her feet. Nobody there had ever seen him sit.

The people said, Lord, what must she know?

She was not a speaker and she did not make a speech. She said, All these years he has stood here and taken your sorghum off the tip of a sedge straw one Sunday in the month, and called that serving God. If from today he went hungry one day out of plain sorrow for it, that one day would be worth more. Put all his fifty years of standing together and they are not worth the sixteenth part of what a child knows who has looked down this glass and understood what she saw.

Then she set the glass on the table rock, in the sun, and let them look, one at a time, the men and then the women and then the children held up to it.

Dr. Anders was out of tin boxes before two o’clock. People went home and got snuff tins. On Monday the schoolyard was full again before the dew was off, and Jim Buck was the first in the line, with that name and no other written for him on a scrap of paper. He took his salts that night and his capsules at six and at eight in the morning, and at noon Eli Tidmore’s granddaughter set a place for him, and he ate at a table.

The dispensary went on to the next point. He stayed. The first pit privy on that end of the Mountain was dug that August behind Shiloh church on the women’s side, and the second on the men’s, a little way off from the table rock, and he dug them both. He lived eleven years more, in overalls and brogans, and dug the pit for nearly every privy between the church and the gulf. When somebody carried his dinner out to where he was working, he climbed up out of the hole, washed his hands, and sat down to it.

Background

The verse

Let a fool month after month eat his food (like an ascetic) with the tip of a blade of Kusa grass, yet he is not worth the sixteenth particle of those who have well weighed the law.Dhammapada 70, tr. F. Max Müller (1881)

In the commentary, a resident monk grows jealous when his lay supporter honors a visiting elder with food, a robe, a barber and a bed. He tells the visitor he would do better to eat dung, tear out his hair with a palm comb, go naked and lie on the ground, and next day throws away the meal sent after him. For this he is reborn as Jambuka, who from infancy will eat nothing but excrement and will wear nothing. The naked ascetics take him in and then put him out. He lives at a flat rock used as a latrine, standing on one leg with his mouth open, tells people he eats only wind, and once a month takes their offerings on the tip of a blade of grass. The Buddha asks him for a night’s lodging, is refused, and sleeps in a cave nearby, where the Four Great Kings, Sakka and Brahmā visit him with lights. In the morning he tells Jambuka the truth about his life and its cause, tosses him a cloth to cover himself, and teaches him. Jambuka then rises seven palm trees into the air before his admirers to show them who is the teacher. Sand Mountain, 1912

In 1902 the zoologist Charles Wardell Stiles identified the American hookworm, a parasite half an inch long that enters through bare feet from ground fouled with human waste (the itch it raises was called ground itch, or dew poison) and lives in the bowel on blood. It left millions of Southerners pale, thin and slow, and some of the worst cases craved clay and dirt. From 1909 to 1914 the Rockefeller Sanitary Commission paid for a campaign through the state boards of health. Its field doctors and microscopists held free county dispensaries one day a week at schools and crossroads; people brought specimens in tins, saw the eggs for themselves through the microscope, and were given thymol in capsules with Epsom salts before and after. A visitor quoted in the Commission’s report for 1911 said of the crowds, “It looks like the days of Galilee.” Kentucky’s report for 1912 mentions a young woman among its microscopists. The questions and answers are from Stiles’s school circular Soil Pollution as Cause of Ground-Itch, Hookworm Disease (Ground-Itch Anemia), and Dirt Eating (1910), the Commission’s first publication, which children were told to take home and read to their parents.

Sand Mountain is a broad sandstone plateau in northeast Alabama, a country of small farms, bluffs and rock shelters, where churches commonly had preaching one Sunday in the month and all-day singings from the shape-note books. Alabama’s dispensaries reached Marshall County, on the Mountain, in 1912. The Commission’s report for that year counted 580 children examined there and half of them infected, and of 226 country homes inspected every one fell in the two lowest classes of its privy index. The Encyclopedia of Alabama has more on the disease in the state. Shiloh church, the table rock and all the people in the story are invented.
