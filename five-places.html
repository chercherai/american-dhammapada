Five Places

That even of which a man thinks ‘this is mine’ is left behind by death.
— Sutta Nipāta 806, tr. V. Fausbøll

Evaṃ me sutaṃ, in the Lower Highlands.

I. The Chain

Lowell, Massachusetts · 1994

Kong Sarin sold gold on Middlesex Street, in a narrow shop between a video store and a place that wired money to Battambang, and he was the richest Cambodian in the Lower Highlands. Everybody said so, and he never once denied it, because denying it would have meant saying a number.

The gold in his cases was the old kind, the soft deep yellow that bends in the hand. Chains, mostly, and bracelets, and the thick rope necklaces that a family put on a bride and took back off her to pay for a funeral. He weighed everything on a little brass scale and priced it by the chi, which is three and three-quarters grams, and he could tell you the price of a chi in Phnom Penh that morning without looking at a paper. He had been a goldsmith's apprentice in Phnom Penh before the war. In April 1975, when the Khmer Rouge marched everybody out of the city, he had walked past the bank with his mother and seen the money blowing down the boulevard in drifts, riel notes in bundles and loose, against the curbs, in the gutters, and nobody stopping to pick them up. He told that to his son Vibol many times. Paper, he said. They made it nothing in one morning. Four years later he walked out of the fields with two chi of gold sewn into the waistband of his trousers, and gave one chi to a man who knew the way through the mines to the border and the other to a Thai soldier at the wire at Khao-I-Dang, and that was how he was alive and standing in Massachusetts.

Every other Sunday he had the family to dinner in his house up the hill from the shop, a big two-family with a porch on each floor, which he owned outright, and after the soup he said the same three things to Vibol in front of everybody, the way a monk says a blessing.

Don't think what we have is a lot. It isn't.

What you have, you don't give.

And every day you get a little more.

Then he would lift a gold chain off his own neck and run it through his fingers. A chain is made one link at a time, he said, and it comes apart one link at a time. You never see it go.

His wife had died in the camp. His sisters were in Long Beach and wrote asking for help with this and that, and he wrote back that business was slow. When the men from the wat came to the shop asking for money to buy a building, so the monks would not have to live in a rented apartment, he took them into the back and gave them tea and said he had given in Cambodia, that everybody had given in Cambodia, the Khmer Rouge had taken everything from everybody, and he was finished giving. The old achar who led the chanting at the wat, a thin man named Moeun who mopped floors at the hospital at night, came once by himself. Sarin gave him tea too. The achar drank it and thanked him and asked for nothing, and that bothered Sarin more than the others had, and he spoke of it for a week.

In the summer of 1994 his heart began to fail. In September, when he could no longer walk to the shop, he called Vibol to the bed and said he would show him where it was.

He did not keep it in a bank. Banks were paper too. He kept it in five places, and he took Vibol to each of them, slowly, leaning on his arm, in an order that he made Vibol repeat back.

Under the floor of the cabinet where the Buddha sat in the front room, where the board lifted if you pressed the left corner.

In the chest freezer in the cellar, at the bottom, in four packages wrapped in white paper and marked with a grease pencil as fish.

In the yard, under the lemongrass that came up every summer by the fence, in a coffee can, two feet down.

In the shop, behind the cinderblock in the back room that was a different gray from the others.

And in the iron pole of the clothesline, which was hollow, and capped, and which nobody would ever look inside because nobody looks inside a clothesline pole.

Don't touch it, he said. Add to it. Never take.

He died in October, in the bed, at three in the morning, angry, with his hand around the chain on his neck. The family had the monks to the house and had him cremated and brought the ashes home in an urn, and set his photograph on the cabinet in the front room, above the board that lifted, in a frame of gold.

Vibol took over the shop. He was thirty. He did as he was told.

II. Unlucky

The Acre · 1994–1995

That autumn a crew of Cambodian women, and a few of their husbands, cleaned offices at night out on the highway. There were about forty of them. They rode out together after supper in two vans driven by a man named Heng, who got the contracts and paid the crew in cash every other Friday, and they vacuumed and emptied wastebaskets and scrubbed toilets in the glass buildings of the software companies until four in the morning, and rode home asleep against each other's shoulders. It was not much money, but it came.

In November it stopped coming. Heng said the companies were slow to pay him. In December he said after New Year. All through that winter the crew went out to the buildings every night and worked and came home with nothing, and the women at the Acre began to buy rice on credit at the market and lie awake doing sums.

One of them was Nhem Chann. She was twenty-four, and the man she lived with had gone to Providence in the fall to look for work and had not come back, and by Christmas she knew she was pregnant.

The crew talked about it in the vans. Heng was a cheat, everybody knew Heng was a cheat, but he had been a cheat for four years and had always paid. Something had changed. An old woman named Yeay Pheap said what all of them were thinking. When a crew works and the money doesn't come, there is somebody unlucky in the crew. It happens. You have to find out who.

So they split in two. Twenty went out in one van to the buildings on the north side of the highway, and twenty in the other to the south side, and they worked like that for two weeks. At the end of two weeks a different man came out with an envelope for the north van, and the north van was paid, and the south van was told to wait. Then they changed the people around and did it again. Chann was always in the van that waited.

They did not tell her to her face. Yeay Pheap went to see her in her apartment on a Sunday, with a bag of oranges, and sat with her a long time, and said that the crew was full now, that Heng had too many, and that in her condition she should rest. Chann understood her. The next Friday, when she had been gone from the crew for one week, everybody was paid in full, and Heng paid the back wages too, all of them, in a fat envelope, for reasons nobody ever learned.

After that, nothing Chann did went right. She found work at a laundry and the laundry burned. She found work at a chicken plant in New Hampshire and the ride she had found to get there broke down on Route 3 the first morning, and the second, and on the third morning they gave her place to somebody else. Her rice ran out and the market would not give her credit. She lived that spring on what the wat gave on holy days and on what a woman across the hall left outside her door, and she grew big and tired and frightened.

The baby came at the end of July, at the hospital, in the night. The nurse who handed him to her did not say anything for a moment.

His hands and his feet were not right. His eyes were set at different heights in his face, and his nose was flat to it, and his mouth was open in the wrong place, up into the lip, so that he could not suck. One ear was a small curled knot of skin. He looked like a clay doll that a child had made in a hurry and dropped. The doctors came, several of them, and talked to her through an interpreter about surgeons in Boston.

Chann looked at him for a long time. Then she put him against her chest, under the hospital gown, against her skin, where she could feel him breathe.

She named him Samnang. It means luck.

III. Samnang

The Lower Highlands · 1995–2004

They operated on him in Boston, three times before he was four. They closed the gap in his lip and the roof of his mouth, and moved one eye, and made his hands into hands that could hold a spoon. But they could not make him into what he would have been, and the neighborhood saw him the way it saw him.

It was strange, the women said. Chann was a good worker. People wanted to give her things. But she could not take him anywhere. When she carried him to the market, the man at the register was out of change, or the line was too long, or the thing she came for was gone from the shelf. When she took him to the wat on holy days the monks blessed him like anyone, but the women in the kitchen found the rice already gone. When she left him home with the woman across the hall and went out by herself, she came home with a job, or a bag of groceries, or a ride.

The children called him Unlucky. In English, so their parents would not understand, but the parents understood.

He was a quiet child. He did not cry when the other children threw things. He went to the special classes at the elementary school and learned to read slowly, and the teachers liked him, because he was careful with everything, careful the way an old man is careful. He never broke anything. When he was five he could add columns of numbers in his head faster than the teacher. When he was six a woman from church brought him a little plastic cash register for Christmas, and he spent the whole day weighing things on the scale it came with, buttons, beans, a ring of his mother's, and writing the weights down, and Chann watched him and felt cold without knowing why.

Sometimes in his sleep he talked. Not baby talk. He talked like a man, in a man's Khmer, the old Phnom Penh kind, about prices.

In the fall of 2004 he was nine, and Chann had been out of work since the spring, and the rent was three months behind. She sat on the edge of his mattress one morning in October and held his face in her hands. It was the first week of Pchum Ben, the fifteen days when the gates of the underworld stand open and the dead come back to their families hungry, and every Cambodian house in Lowell was cooking.

Kon, she said. My son. I can't feed you anymore. Since you came, everything we touch falls down. I don't say it's your fault. I say it is what it is. She put a plastic bag in his hand, the kind from the market. This is Pchum Ben. Every house is cooking for the dead, and in Pchum Ben nobody turns a hungry one away. Go and eat. Go to the houses. Bring something home if they give it.

He took the bag. He did not ask why. He went down the stairs one at a time, holding the rail with his good hand, and out into the cold.

IV. The Fourth Room

The Highlands · October 2004

He went up one street and down the next. Some doors he knocked at did not open. At some a woman looked at his face and gave him a sticky-rice cake wrapped in banana leaf and closed the door fast, as if she had paid something off. On Westford Street a grandmother sat him down on her steps and gave him rice and fish soup in a bowl and stood watching him eat with her arms crossed, and when he was finished she took the bowl back inside and washed it twice.

It was past noon when he came to a big two-family house up the hill from Middlesex Street, with a porch on each floor and a chain-link fence and a clothesline in the yard.

He stopped at the gate. He knew it.

He did not know how. He knew the gate stuck at the bottom and you had to lift it. He knew there would be lemongrass by the fence, gone brown now, and there was. He knew the back door would be open in the daytime because the women were cooking, and it was, and the kitchen was full of steam and women and the smell of coconut and banana leaf, a dozen of them, aunts and cousins and neighbors, making num ansorm on every surface for the dead.

He walked in.

He walked through the kitchen, between the women, and not one of them looked at him. He walked through the dining room, where the long table was set with plates covered in plastic wrap and two men were arguing about the Red Sox, and they did not look at him. He walked through the little hall where the coats hung, and a boy his own age came the other way carrying a case of soda and stepped around him without seeing him.

The fourth room was the front room. It was where the Buddha sat on the cabinet, with candles, and incense going in a bowl of sand, and plates of fruit and cakes and a can of Coca-Cola and a pack of Marlboros for the dead, because that was what the dead man had liked. Above it hung a photograph of an old man in a frame of gold. He had a chain around his neck. He was not smiling.

Samnang stood in front of the photograph a long time. Then he went to the cabinet and knelt down and put his hand flat on the floor beside it, on the left corner, the way you put your hand on the head of a dog you know.

Two little children were sitting on the couch in front of the television, a boy of five and a girl of three, Vibol's children, Dara and Malis, eating rice crackers out of a bag. The girl turned around and saw him there at the cabinet with his face, kneeling at her grandfather's altar, and she screamed. Then the boy screamed, because she had. They screamed the way small children do when they have seen a ghost in the house in the season of ghosts, and the women came running from the kitchen with their hands full of banana leaves, and the men came from the dining room.

Vibol's brother-in-law got to him first. Then two cousins.

Get out. Get out of here. What are you, a thief? Get out, you unlucky thing.

They hit him. Not as hard as they could have, but he was nine. They took him by the arms and by the back of his jacket and dragged him back through the hall and the dining room and the kitchen, where the women stood back against the counters to let him go by, and out the back door and down the steps and along the side of the house to the street, where it was trash day, and the green bags were piled at the curb, and they threw him down on top of them.

One of the cousins kicked the plastic market bag after him. A cake fell out of it into the gutter.

The girl was still screaming in the front room. Vibol's wife Sophea had her on her hip. It's all right, she said. It's all right, it's gone, it's only a boy. Look, see. It's Grandpa watching over you. And she pointed to the photograph.

V. The Achar

Middlesex Street · the same afternoon

Achar Moeun had slept four hours after his shift and gotten up and put on his white shirt, which was the shirt the achar wears, and his good trousers, and walked the eight blocks from his apartment because he did not drive. His grandnephew Rith walked with him. Rith was nineteen and studying to be a nurse at the community college, and he had been walking with the old man since he was fifteen, carrying the bag with the candles and the incense and the books, and asking questions, and getting answers that were usually shorter than the questions.

Moeun was seventy-one. He had been a monk for nine years as a young man in Kampong Cham and had left the robes to marry, and when the Khmer Rouge came they were killing monks and men who could read, so he had stopped being able to read. He had stopped for four years. He herded water buffalo and said yes to everything and let his face go stupid, and he lived, and his wife and his two daughters did not. Afterward, in the camp, he had begun to read again, slowly, like a man learning to walk on a leg that had been broken. He had led the chanting at the Lowell wat since 1986. At night he mopped the floors at the hospital, the long corridors on the fourth floor where the old people were, and some of the nurses said that patients who were going to die that night often asked for the janitor, and that he would put down the mop and go in and sit with them, and nobody could ever say what he said to them because he said very little, in any language.

That afternoon Kong Vibol had asked him to come to the house to lead the chanting for his father. It was the tenth anniversary.

They turned the corner at the top of the hill and the old man stopped.

There was a boy lying on the trash bags at the curb in front of the Kong house. He was not crying. He was lying there with his eyes open, looking up at the clothesline in the yard, with blood on his lip, which had been sewn together once.

Rith started forward. Moeun put his hand on the young man's arm and held it.

Lok Ta, he's hurt. Why are we standing here?

The old man stood a long moment. The clothesline creaked in the wind. A woman's sweater on it, bright red, turned slowly on its pin.

Ten years ago, Moeun said, I went to that house and drank tea with Kong Sarin. He gave me tea and nothing else. He had a lot of gold.

Lok Ta, I know. Everybody knows.

That is Kong Sarin, the old man said. On the bags.

Rith looked at him and then at the boy.

He has come home for Pchum Ben, said Moeun, like the rest of them. They threw him out of his house. Then he said, in the same plain voice, Go up and knock. Ask for Vibol. Tell him the achar is here and is waiting in the street.

VI. Five Places

The Highlands · the same afternoon

Vibol came out onto the porch in his socks. He was forty now, heavy in the face, with his father's chain around his neck. He saw the achar at the gate and the boy on the trash bags and his relatives crowding out behind him onto the porch and the steps, and the neighbors coming out onto their own porches, up and down the street, because a crowd draws a crowd and because it was Pchum Ben and everybody was home.

Lok Achar, I'm sorry, come in, come in. There was a boy, a thief, the children were frightened. It's nothing.

Do you know this boy?

Vibol looked at him. He looked at the face. No.

He is your father, said the achar. Kong Sarin.

Nobody on the porch said anything. A woman behind Vibol made a small sound in her throat. Somebody laughed and stopped.

Vibol went red and then white. He came down the steps in his socks onto the cold walk.

Lok Achar, with respect. With great respect. My father has been dead ten years. His ashes are in my house. I have his photograph. I have taken food to the wat for him every day this week. I'm not a stupid man. He pointed at the boy, who had sat up now on the bags. That is Nhem Chann's boy from the Acre. Everybody knows him. They call him Unlucky. He has no father, his father went off to Providence. His voice was shaking. You come to my house on the tenth anniversary and say this to me in front of the whole street.

Moeun did not answer him. He turned to the boy.

Sarin, he said. Show your son the five places.

The boy got down off the trash bags. He went to the gate and lifted it at the bottom where it stuck, and walked up the walk and up the steps between the relatives, who drew back from him, and in at the front door, and Vibol went after him, and the achar, and Rith, and as many of the rest as could fit into the front room, and the rest stood at the windows.

He knelt at the cabinet below the photograph and pressed the left corner of the floorboard, and it lifted. Under it was a cloth bundle. He did not open it. He looked up at Vibol.

Then he got up and went to the cellar door and down the stairs, and they all went down after him, into the cold cellar with the furnace ticking, to the chest freezer against the wall. He could not lift the lid by himself. Rith lifted it for him. He reached down past the bags of frozen lemongrass and the ice cream and the shrimp to the bottom and came up with a white paper package with a word on it in grease pencil, and held it out. It was heavy. It was not fish.

Then out the back door into the yard. He went to the brown lemongrass by the fence and knelt and began to dig with his hands, with the hands they had made for him in Boston, and Vibol stood over him and watched, and then Vibol knelt down too, in his socks, in the dirt, and dug beside him, and at two feet they hit the lid of the coffee can.

Then the clothesline. The boy stood at the iron pole and put his good hand flat against it, and looked up at the cap, and said nothing. Vibol did not make him do anything more.

And the fifth place was at the shop on Middlesex Street, six blocks away. They walked there, the whole procession of them, the achar and Vibol and the boy and Rith and the relatives and a good part of the street, down the hill in the October afternoon, past the video store and the place that wired money to Battambang, with people coming out of the restaurants to see. Vibol unlocked the shop and they went through to the back room. The boy walked straight to the wall and put his hand on the one cinderblock that was a different gray from the others.

Nobody alive knew of these five places but Kong Vibol. His father had shown him in September of 1994, leaning on his arm, and made him say them back in order, and Vibol had never told his wife. He had done as he was told. He had never taken. Every year he had added.

He turned around from the wall. He looked down at the boy for a long time, at the eyes set at their different heights, the knotted ear, the mended lip, the jacket with the trash smell on it. Something went out of his face, all at once, the way air goes out of a room when a door opens.

Pa, he said.

He got down on his knees on the concrete floor of the back room. He did it the way you kneel to a monk, three times, with his palms together and his forehead to the floor. Then he turned on his knees and did it a fourth time to the old achar, who stood in the door of the back room in his white shirt with the bag of candles Rith had given him to hold.

The achar waited until Vibol was finished. Then he said it, quietly, in the Pali first, the way he would have chanted it, and then in Khmer. Rith looked it up that night in the old English translation in the college library, because English was the language he thought in, and copied it out, and kept it.

That even of which a man thinks ‘this is mine’ is left behind by death: knowing this, let not the wise (man) turn himself to worldliness (while being my) follower. As a man awakened does not see what he has met with in his sleep, so also he does not see the beloved person that has passed away and is dead.

— Sutta Nipāta 806–807 (Jarāsutta), tr. V. Fausbøll

Vibol was still on his knees. What do I do, Lok Ta, he said. Tell me what to do with it.

It isn't his, said the achar. He has been showing you that for nine years. It isn't yours either. He looked at the gray cinderblock. You'll know.

VII. Bay Ben

The wat · the last morning of Pchum Ben

Before light on the fifteenth day the yard of the wat was full. It was cold, and people stood in their coats over their good clothes, the old women in white, with their breath showing, and the windows of the building lit yellow behind them, and the monks chanting inside where it was warm. Each family had brought a bowl of bay ben, the little balls of sticky rice mixed with sesame and beans, and before the sun came up they walked around the building three times in the dark and threw them out into the yard and the grass and the parking lot, a handful at a time, for the dead who had no family to feed them. The old women said that was why it was done before light, because the hungry ones are ashamed to be seen.

Achar Moeun stood on the steps in his white shirt and led them. He had come straight from the hospital. There was still floor wax on his shoes.

Kong Vibol walked the circle with his wife and his children. Behind him walked Nhem Chann, whom he had gone to see on the Thursday in the Acre, and had sat with in her kitchen for two hours, and had given the rent and more than the rent and a job in the shop, which she would start on Monday. Between them walked the boy, in a new jacket, with a bowl of his own.

The neighbors watched them go around. People talked, the way people do. It was already a story by then, and the story was already growing, the way they do; by Christmas there were people in Lynn and Long Beach who would swear that the boy had walked into the shop and opened the wall with his bare hand, and that the gold behind it had turned to rice. It hadn't. It was gold. Over the next year Vibol took it out, all five places of it, and paid for the new roof on the wat, and sent money to his aunts in Long Beach, and to a village outside Battambang that his father had not written to in thirty years, and paid for the surgeons in Boston to do one more operation, which was the one the boy had wanted most, on his ear. Some of it he kept. He was not a saint. But he never again put any of it in the ground.

That morning, though, nobody knew any of that yet. They only went around in the dark, three times, throwing rice.

On the third time around the boy stopped at the corner of the building, where the light from the windows did not reach. He took a ball of rice out of his bowl and turned it in his fingers, and then he did not throw it. He ate it, standing there, the way a hungry one would, slowly, all of it.

Then he took another out of the bowl, and leaned back, and threw it as far as he could into the dark.

Background

The verse

“These sons belong to me, and this wealth belongs to me,” with such thoughts a fool is tormented. He himself does not belong to himself; how much less sons and wealth?Dhammapada 62, tr. F. Max Müller (1881)

In the commentary, Ānanda is a rich man of Sāvatthī with eight hundred million in treasure, who warns his son Mūlasiri every fortnight never to think it a large sum, never to give anything away, and always to get more. He shows his son his five great stores of treasure and dies in his avarice. He is reborn in a village of outcastes who hire themselves out as a crew; from the day he is conceived the crew is paid nothing, until they search out the unlucky one and drive his mother away. The child is born misshapen, and when he is old enough his mother puts a bowl in his hand and sends him to beg. He wanders into his old house, passes through three rooms unseen, frightens his grandchildren in the fourth, and is beaten and thrown on the dust-heap. The Buddha, passing with Ānanda the Elder, tells Mūlasiri the beggar is his father, and when the son will not believe it, has the beggar point out the five buried stores. Lowell, 2004

Under the Khmer Rouge, from 1975 to 1979, Cambodia’s cities were emptied, banks were raided and the currency and its records were burned, and something like a fifth to a quarter of the population died. Survivors who reached the Thai border after the regime fell came through camps such as Khao-I-Dang, opened in November 1979, and many were resettled in the United States. From the mid-1980s they settled in Lowell, Massachusetts, the old mill city on the Merrimack, opening dozens of small businesses; by 2010 Lowell had the highest proportion of Cambodian residents of any city in the country. More on the Cambodian genocide and on Cambodian Americans.

Pchum Ben is a fifteen-day festival at the end of the rains retreat, in September or October, when the gates of the underworld are said to open and the dead return to their families. Cambodians honor up to seven generations of ancestors with food brought to the monks, and many throw balls of sticky rice into the temple grounds for the wandering dead. An achar is a layman, often a former monk, who serves as master of ceremonies in the temple’s rites. In Cambodia gold is still bought and sold by the chi of 3.75 grams.
