Walking the American WayCommentary 10.1 · Chabbaggiyā (the Group of Six)

The Parking Chair

All that breathe
Will share thy destiny.
— William Cullen Bryant, “Thanatopsis”

The way they tell it up the street, on Nevada Avenue.

I. The Dig

Dormont, Pennsylvania · Sunday, March 14, 1993

It started to snow on Friday night, and all day Saturday it came down two and three inches an hour, with thunder in it. Downtown they held the St. Patrick’s parade anyway. By dark there were two feet on the ground in Dormont and the Turnpike was shut. Sunday came up blue and bitter, with a wind. The borough plow reached Nevada Avenue about nine and did what a plow does on a street of parked cars. It opened one lane down the middle and laid a wall against every car on the block, chest high and packed like concrete.

Nevada Avenue is brick foursquares shoulder to shoulder, built in the twenties for men who rode the streetcar to work, and not one of them has a driveway. Parking is on the even side. There had been a custom about that for longer than most people on the street had been alive. You dug out your space, you stood a kitchen chair in it, and it was yours until the street was bare. Nobody had ever written it down.

The houses at 1245 and 1247 had been cut into apartments, and that winter seventeen people lived in them, none over thirty-one. There were four nursing students, two boys from the Art Institute, a line cook, two brothers from Altoona who hung drywall, and a respiratory therapist named Jim Otto who came from wheat country in eastern Washington and had not heard of a parking chair before that fall. They had been shut in together since Friday with the same four videotapes. At ten on Sunday morning all seventeen came out with five shovels, a coal scoop from the cellar, two dustpans and a cookie sheet, and dug the whole frontage of both houses, six car lengths, down to the brick. It took them until two. They had no chairs to spare. They stood a laundry basket, a milk crate and a floor lamp along the curb and went in to eat.

The Leskos lived at 1251 and 1253 and had since 1951. There were six Lesko men who counted: Stan, who was fifty-eight, his brother Ray, Stan’s two sons, a son-in-law and a nephew. They ran a paving business, and in winter they hung plows on three pickups and cleared lots. They had been out since Saturday noon without sleep, doing a supermarket, two churches and a bank. At three on Sunday they came up Nevada Avenue in a line and found their own curb under the plow wall and, two doors down, six clean spaces with a floor lamp in one of them.

Ray Lesko set the lamp on the sidewalk.

The seventeen came out on the porches, and then down the steps.

We dug that, said one of the nursing students. Her name was Dawn Kubiak. It took us four hours.

We’re on this street forty-two years, Stan Lesko said. You’re here since September. That’s our curb.

We were here first this morning.

It went the way those things go. Somebody put the lamp back. A Lesko son shoved him. Then Ray swung the flat of a grain shovel into the side of a boy’s head. The boy was an art student from Erie named Tim Fahey, twenty years old, and he sat down in the street with blood running out of his nose onto the snow. After that there were shovels up on both sides, and the Leskos were the ones who knew how to use them. The nephew got Dawn Kubiak by the back of her coat, put her face down in the plow wall and leaned on her. She could not lift her head. She could not get air. The ones who could scream screamed, and it carried the length of Nevada Avenue between the brick fronts.

II. The Porch

The same afternoon

Mrs. Haney lived at 1249, between them. She was eighty-one. She had come to that house as a bride in 1936, and nothing on the street was older except the sycamores. She had been at her front window since the trucks pulled up.

She came out on the porch in her husband’s car coat and a pair of galoshes, with nothing on her head.

What was that?

She did not have a big voice. But everybody on Nevada Avenue under the age of sixty had at one time or another been sent to her door with a covered dish or a snow shovel, and they stopped. The nephew let go of the coat. The girl came up out of the snow with her mouth wide open.

The young ones told her, all at once. The Leskos looked at their shovels.

Stanley, said Mrs. Haney. Put it down.

He put it down.

Nobody raises a hand to anybody on this street. Not a hand and not a shovel. Not over a parking place, and not over anything else.

Mrs. Haney, with respect. Stan Lesko was breathing hard. Four winters before he had had his chest opened at Mercy Hospital, and he had just spent twenty-seven hours in a truck. We got forty-two years here. That has to count.

I was fifteen years on this street the day your father backed his truck up to that house, Stanley, and you were a boy carrying lamps. So you can leave off telling me about years. She came down one step and took hold of the rail. Behind her on the porch, where it waited every winter, stood a chrome kitchen chair with a red seat. You think that chair is about a parking space. This whole street has thought so since Truman, and I let you.

Nobody had parked in front of 1249 with snow on the ground in forty-three winters. She had not owned a car since 1951.

Thanksgiving of 1950 it snowed three days. Thirty inches. They had tanks out pushing it. On the Sunday morning Walter went down with the coal shovel to get the Plymouth out for Monday. He was thirty-eight years old. He ran the 42 car and had never been sick a day. He cleared the space down to the brick, square at the corners, and stood the shovel in the bank, and lay down. I watched him do it from that window. I thought he was fooling.

I took a kitchen chair out and sat by him. It was four o’clock before they could get anybody up the hill. I didn’t know a prayer that was long enough. So I said him a piece Miss Kearns made us get by heart in the eighth grade, because it was the longest thing I had.

Nobody moved. She said some of it to the street then, holding the rail, without hurry, the way she had been taught to say it.

So shalt thou rest—and what, if thou withdraw
Unheeded by the living, and no friend
Take note of thy departure? All that breathe
Will share thy destiny.

— William Cullen Bryant, “Thanatopsis”

That’s whose space that is, she said. He dug it. I put the chair in it every snow so I’d remember there is one place apiece kept for us, and nobody has to fight to get it.

She looked at Stan Lesko.

I watched you get down out of that truck, Stanley. You stood with your hand flat on your chest before you ever reached for the shovel. I know what you were listening for. That girl with her face in the bank was listening for the very same thing, and so was that boy. There is nobody standing in this street who is afraid of anything you aren’t. So that is the end of the hitting.

She went in and shut the door, because she was cold.

By then Jim Otto had the Fahey boy sitting up against a tire. He tipped the boy’s head forward, pinched his nose shut and held it. A school bus driver had done that for him once, when he was six. One of Stan’s sons went into 1251 and came back with a bag of frozen peas, and stood holding it until somebody took it from him.

The trucks spent that night in the bank lot on West Liberty Avenue.

III. The Brick

Nevada Avenue · afterward

After that it was simply how Nevada Avenue was. You could chair a space you had dug, and you could not lay a hand on anybody. People who moved onto the street were told both things the first time it snowed, and as a rule it was a Lesko who told them. The next winter the complaints about chairs got so bad across the borough that the Dormont police went around and took them out of two hundred spaces. They say the officer who worked Nevada Avenue took every chair on the block but one.

That was later. On the Monday morning Mrs. Haney came to her window at six, as she always did. The wind had dropped. Five kitchen chairs stood along the curb at 1245 and 1247, no two alike. In front of 1251 a pickup was nosed in behind a chair she knew from Stan Lesko’s own kitchen. And in front of her house, where the plow wall had stood as high as the porch rail when she went to bed, a space had been cut down to the brick, square at the corners, long enough for a Plymouth. The red chair stood in the middle of it.

She never did find out which of them dug it. A little new snow had come in the night, and it lay on the seat.

The verse

All men tremble at punishment, all men fear death; remember that you are like unto them, and do not kill, nor cause slaughter.Dhammapada 129, tr. F. Max Müller (1881)

The Group of Six are the standing troublemakers of the monastic rule. In this story the younger Group of Seventeen have made a lodging ready, and the Six arrive and say, We are older; this belongs to us. The Seventeen answer, We will not give it to you; we were the first to make it ready. The Six strike them, and the Seventeen, in fear of death, scream at the top of their lungs. The Buddha hears the outcry, asks what it was, and lays down the rule against striking a blow. Then he says that a person should reflect that others tremble at the rod and fear death just as he does, and speaks the verse. Read the full commentary story. Dormont and the parking chair, 1993

Dormont is a borough of less than a square mile, four miles south of downtown Pittsburgh, that filled with houses when the streetcar tunnel opened the South Hills. It holds more than ten thousand people to the square mile, and a great many of its houses have no driveway. The parking chair is the custom, strongest in and around Pittsburgh, of marking a street space you have shoveled out with a kitchen or lawn chair. It has no standing in law and is generally honored anyway; photographs of it go back at least to the 1950s. In 1994, according to press accounts, the Dormont police removed the markers from some two hundred spaces after too many complaints. Nevada Avenue and everyone on it are invented.

The storm of March 12–14, 1993, left about twenty-five inches in Pittsburgh, 23.6 of them on Saturday the thirteenth, at two to three inches an hour; the downtown St. Patrick’s Day parade went ahead that day, and the Turnpike and most roads were closed. The Thanksgiving-weekend storm of 1950 left about thirty inches, and tanks were used to help clear it. William Cullen Bryant drafted “Thanatopsis” as a very young man in western Massachusetts, and it was first printed in 1817; generations of American schoolchildren learned it by heart. The lines here follow the text of his collected Poems. The poem ends by asking its hearer to go to the grave not “like the quarry-slave at night, / Scourged to his dungeon,” but as one who lies down to sleep.
