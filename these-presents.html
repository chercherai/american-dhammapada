These Presents

I who had been a slave in the morning, trembling at the will of another, was become my own master, and completely free.
— Olaudah Equiano, The Interesting Narrative, ch. VII (1789)

This is how I heard it, at the Deep Creek lock.

I. Ran Away

Near the Great Bridge, Norfolk County, Virginia · January 1801

TEN DOLLARS REWARD.

RAN AWAY from the subscriber, or rather did not return to him at Christmas from his hire, a Negro man named JEM, about twenty-eight years of age, five feet nine or ten inches high, stout made, with a white scar across the back of his left hand got from a froe. He has been hired these three years past to the getting of shingles in the Dismal Swamp and is well acquainted therein. He is a professor of religion, a member of the Baptist society, sings well, and can say over a good deal from books though he cannot read. It is supposed he is lurking about the Swamp. Whoever takes up the said fellow and secures him so that I get him again shall have the above reward.

EZEKIEL HODGES.

Elder Ezekiel Hodges wrote it out three times at the kitchen table before he was satisfied, and each time he struck the words a member of the Baptist society and each time he wrote them in again. They were true. He had put Jem under the water of the Northwest River himself, in the spring of 1797, with the ice just off the banks, and Jem had come up out of it gasping and laughing with his arms thrown wide, and the people on the bank had sung him out. It did not seem to Hodges a thing a man ought to leave out of a description.

He was fifty-two that winter. He had preached thirty years without a license and without a salary, and as a young man, before the laws changed, he had spent two months in a county jail for it, preaching to whoever would stand in the road outside the window. He kept a farm of a hundred and sixty acres on the edge of the swamp, and six people to work it, and of the six Jem was the strongest, and so it was Jem whom he hired out by the year to the shingle men, for sixty dollars and his keep. The shingle men paid by the thousand. A good getter could rive his task by Thursday and work the rest of the week for himself, and Jem had always been a good getter. Hodges knew that Jem kept the overwork money. He had thought of that as generosity.

The hiring year ran out at Christmas. Every other hired man came walking out of the swamp on Christmas Eve with his pay in his pocket and a sack over his shoulder, to see his people and get drunk or get religion or both. Jem did not come out.

On Sunday evenings that winter Hodges went on reading aloud to his household from the books he had, as he had always done, the Bible and the hymn book and a few others. One of the others was the life of the African, Gustavus Vassa, in the New York printing of 1791, which he had bought in Norfolk because he had heard it called a powerful testimony. He had read it to them twice. The first time through, he noticed afterward, Jem had asked him to read one page over, and then a second time, and then had said it back to him nearly word for word, the page about the man running to the register office with his feet winged with joy. Hodges had been pleased at the time. He had taken it for an interest in the conversion, which came later in the book.

He did not read it that winter. He found he could not, and could not have said why.

II. The Island

The Great Dismal · 1801–1805

There are places in the swamp where the ground lifts a few feet out of the black water and stays dry, and on them the old people built houses of juniper poles and reeds that nobody outside ever saw. Sib had lived on one of them since before the war with the King. Nobody knew whose she had been. Some said she had come in as a girl from the Company's plantation by Suffolk, in the time when they first set people to ditching for the gentlemen who meant to drain the whole Dismal and plant it in rice. Some said she had been born on the island. She was small and very dark and walked with a stick of gallberry, and when Jem came to her in the first week of January 1801, he came with everything he owned on his back.

He had the froe and the maul. He had his blanket and his pot and a sack of meal, and the overwork money sewn into the waist of his breeches, nineteen dollars and some shillings, and a knife, and a pair of shoes he did not wear in the water. He had the whole of a list he had been keeping for nine years: every Christmas, every hire, every dollar the shingle men had paid to Hodges for the use of his arms. He could say it off the way he said off Vassa. He said it to Sib the first night, sitting by her fire, and she listened to the whole of it.

That's heavy, she said.

He said it was his.

I know it is. Set it down a while.

He did not know what she meant, and she did not explain. She did not explain anything, that year or the next. She got up in the dark and went to bed in the dark and between the two she did whatever there was to do, and when there was nothing to do she sat in the door of her house and watched the water. She owned a pot, a gourd, a knife, a blanket and the stick, and when Jem brought her things, a tin cup, a fish, a bolt of osnaburg he had got from the getters, she used them and gave them away. People came to her from the other islands. A woman came with a burned arm and Sib sat with her until the arm was dressed and the woman stopped shaking. A man came who had heard the dogs behind him for two days, and Sib sat with him until he could hear that they had stopped. The getters said the dogs would not go past the stand of juniper on the near side of her ground, and that a man from Gates County who had followed them past it with a gun had walked in a circle all day and come out where he went in.

Jem rived shingles. That was the trade of the island and of all the islands: the getters who were hired out by the year would take a man's shingles at the edge of the ditches and count them in with their own, and pay him in meal and pork and cloth and whatever money they had over. Every week he carried his thousand out on his head, two bundles lashed together, and every week Sib asked him the same thing when he came back.

What you carrying?

The first year he told her. The list, the dollars, the men in Norfolk who knew his face, the river in 1797 with the ice off the banks, which was the worst of it, because he had come up out of the water free and walked up the bank to the same man. The second year he told her less. In the third year, on a still afternoon in October, when the gums had gone red over the water and he came back from the ditch with the empty lashings in his hand, she asked him, and he stood a long time trying to find it, and could not.

He did not take it for anything. He went down to the water and washed his face. When he came back up she was looking at him the way people look at a boat that has been unloaded, how it sits.

III. The Lock

Deep Creek · March 1806

The canal company had been digging its ditch through the east side of the swamp since 1793, with hired men and bought men up to their waists in the peat, and in 1805 they let the water through from end to end, twenty-two miles from the Pasquotank to the Elizabeth. It was too shallow yet for anything but flats and log rafts. But the flats came, loaded to the gunwales with shingles and staves, and at the Deep Creek end, where the water had to be let down to the river, the company built a lock of swamp timber with a keeper's house beside it.

Sib had not come out of the swamp in eleven years. She came out that March because a getter named Moses, who had been taking the island's shingles for most of that time, had been sold away to Carolina over the winter, and the new man would not deal with anybody he had not seen. She sat in the bow of the flat with her stick across her knees. Jem poled. There were eleven thousand shingles in the flat and Moses's brother walking the towpath, to say the load was his.

Ezekiel Hodges was at Deep Creek that morning to see about corn. He had not been looking for anything. He was standing on the walk across the lower gates with the keeper, talking about the price of freight to Norfolk, when the flat came down into the chamber and the keeper went to his paddles, and the water began to go out of the lock under their feet with a long sucking roar, and the flat sank with it slowly between the wet timber walls until the people in it were below him, and he looked down, and it was Jem.

Jem had stepped up from the flat onto the ledge at the gate to fend her off. Hodges reached down and took hold of the shoulder of his shirt. He did it without thinking, the way you grab at something going over a side, and when he had it he closed his hand.

Sib turned around in the bow.

What do you mean by this?

Hodges heard his own voice. This is my man. This is my man Jem.

The keeper had stopped with his hand on the paddle gear. The water went on going out. Jem stood on the ledge with his pole in one hand and his shirt in the elder's fist and did not pull away and did not lift his eyes, and Hodges, looking down at him, saw that he was not afraid. He had seen Jem afraid. This was a different face. It was the face of a man resting.

His burden is off him, Sib said.

She said it the way you would tell somebody the river was down. She did not say anything after it.

Hodges had preached the burden for thirty years. He had preached it in barns and in the road and through the bars of a jail, the burden that falls off the back at the foot of the cross and rolls away and is not seen again. He had felt it go from himself at nineteen in a tobacco barn in Sussex County and had told the story at every baptizing since. He had read it out of the African's book to his own household, to this man, by his own fire. He knew what she meant before she had finished saying it. And he knew it was so, because he had seen it in the face before she said it, and had not had a word for it.

Is that so? he said. It came out very quiet.

Yes, said Sib. It's off him.

The lock was empty. The keeper looked at the elder and then at the lower gates and then at the elder again, and the elder opened his hand.

He watched the gates swing and the flat go out into Deep Creek on the low water, with the old woman in the bow and Moses's brother walking alongside on the bank, and Jem poling, and he stood on the walk long after the keeper had gone in, with his hand still half closed, as if there were still cloth in it.

IV. These Presents

Near the Great Bridge · March and April 1806

That night he took the African's book down for the first time in five years and sat with it at the kitchen table after the house was asleep. He found the page Jem had said back to him, about the register office and the winged feet, and read it, and could not stay on it. He turned forward to the place he had always liked best, the conversion, where the man at sea in his trouble is shown the cross, and he read the sentence he had read aloud a dozen times in a voice he now could not stand to remember.

I felt an astonishing change; the burden of sin, the gaping jaws of hell, and the fears of death, that weighed me down before, now lost their horror.

— Olaudah Equiano, The Interesting Narrative, ch. X (1789)

He sat with that until the candle guttered. A man could have that, the burden of sin lifted, and keep the other burden on another man's back, and call him by his Christian name at a baptizing. He had done it. He would have gone on doing it. What he could not get past was that the one he held had put down both, out there on a hummock among the bears and the reeds, the one the elder had laid on him and the one the elder had preached, and had come up into the lock with nothing on him at all; and that the elder, whose whole life was the falling of burdens, had stood on the walk above him with his fist full of shirt.

He did not know how a deed of manumission was drawn. He had never drawn one. The law since 1782 allowed it, by will or by a writing under hand and seal, proved in the county court. But the Assembly had sat in January and passed a new law, and from the first of May any slave freed after that day who stayed in Virginia more than twelve months could be taken up and sold again. Hodges read it in the Norfolk paper. If the writing was to mean anything for a man who would never leave the swamp, it had to be proved before May.

So he copied the form out of the African's book. It was the only one he had, the paper the merchant of Montserrat had signed in 1766, printed at full length because, the African said, it showed what power one man claims over another. Hodges wrote it out on a clean sheet with the names changed. Where the merchant had written the sum of seventy pounds current money, he wrote the sum of and stopped and struck it through.

To all men unto whom these presents shall come: I Ezekiel Hodges, of the county of Norfolk in the commonwealth of Virginia, send greeting: Know ye, that I the aforesaid Ezekiel Hodges, for and in consideration of the sum of to the intent that a negro man named Jem, aged about thirty-three years, shall and may become free, have manumitted, emancipated, enfranchised, and set free, and by these presents do manumit, emancipate, enfranchise, and set free, the aforesaid Jem, for ever, hereby giving, granting, and releasing unto him all right, title, dominion, sovereignty, and property, which as lord and master over the said Jem I had, or now I have, or by any means whatsoever I may or can hereafter possibly have over him, for ever.

Acknowledged in court, the 21st day of April, 1806.

The clerk read it over twice and looked at the struck words and said the court would take it as it was. Hodges paid the fee and took two attested copies, because the law said a freed man going about without one could be jailed. Then he rode to Deep Creek and waited at the lock for three days, on the walk above the gates, until a flat came down out of the swamp with Moses's brother on the towpath and Jem poling and no old woman in the bow.

She had not come out again. Jem said she was well.

Hodges held out the paper. Jem looked at it a while on the elder's open hand and then took it, and folded it small, and smaller, and put it inside his shirt against his chest, at the shoulder, where the elder had held him. He did not say anything about it. There was nothing in his face that had not been there in March.

He sold his shingles to the new man at Deep Creek and poled the flat back into the chamber, and the keeper shut the gates behind him and opened the paddles, and the water came in roaring from the high side, brown as tea, and the flat came up on it with nothing in her, riding high, past the wet timber, past the elder's boots on the walk, until she was level with the swamp again and the upper gates swung and she went on into it.

Hodges rode home. He had five more names to write, and he knew it, and that was the first night he knew it.

Background

The verse

Him I call indeed a Brahmana who, even here, knows the end of his suffering, has put down his burden, and is unshackled.Dhammapada 402, tr. F. Max Müller (1881)

In the commentary, a Brahmin's slave runs away, is ordained (this was before the Buddha made the rule against ordaining slaves) and becomes an arahant. One day the Brahmin sees him entering the city with the Buddha and seizes him by the robe in the gateway. The Buddha turns and asks what he means by it. This is my slave. His burden has fallen from him. The Brahmin understands at once that the man is an arahant, asks Is that so?, hears Yes, and is himself established in the first fruit of the path. Here the gateway is a canal lock, the ordination is four years on a swamp island, and the rule that came afterward is a Virginia law. Read the full commentary story; the verse belongs to the Dhammapada’s last chapter, on the true Brahmin.

The Dismal Swamp, 1801–1806

For more than two centuries the Great Dismal Swamp on the Virginia–North Carolina line sheltered maroons, people who had freed themselves and lived on its dry “islands.” After draining for rice failed, the swamp’s companies turned to cutting juniper (Atlantic white cedar) for shingles, working enslaved men hired from their owners by the year; the getters could earn money for shingles beyond their task, and maroons supplied them shingles in exchange for goods and cash, a trade Encyclopedia Virginia describes as an informal economy that let the swamp’s hidden residents live. The Dismal Swamp Canal, dug largely by enslaved laborers from 1793, opened its full twenty-two miles in 1805, at first to shallow flats and log rafts carrying shingles and staves.

Virginia’s manumission act of 1782 let an owner free a slave by will or by a deed proved in the county court, and required the freed person to carry an attested copy. On January 25, 1806, the Assembly passed a new act, in force from May 1, under which anyone freed afterward who stayed in the state more than twelve months could be seized and sold. Olaudah Equiano’s Interesting Narrative (London 1789; New York, W. Durell, 1791) prints in full the deed by which the Montserrat merchant Robert King freed him in 1766; the form the elder copies, and the two passages he reads, are Equiano’s words.
