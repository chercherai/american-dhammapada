I. Uncle No

Westminster, California · 1980–2008

Phạm Văn Lộc came out of Vietnam in 1980 on a fishing boat with fourteen taels of gold leaf sewn flat into the waistband of his trousers, paid nine of them to the boat owner, two to a guard on the beach, one to a man on Pulau Bidong for a place in the shade, and arrived in Orange County with two. He never forgot the arithmetic. In twenty-eight years he never again paid one cent for anything he could get for nothing or do with his own hands.

By 2008 he owned two nail salons on Brookhurst Street and a ranch house on a side street off Bolsa, paid for in cash. He did his own taxes, his own plumbing and his own haircuts, and they looked it. He drove a 1994 Camry with a cracked dashboard. At Tết he put one-dollar bills in the red envelopes, new ones, so they would crackle. He had never given the temple so much as an orange, and the whole of Little Saigon called him Chú Không, Uncle No, because no was the answer to everything, and he did not mind it. A man named No keeps what he has.

What he had, he kept mostly in a floor safe under the carpet in the bedroom closet, in gold. He did not trust banks. In Saigon he had watched the banks close and the money change overnight and the people who had trusted it stand in the street with sacks of paper. Gold you could sew into your pants. In 2008, when the banks in America began to fall down one after another and the price of gold went over a thousand dollars an ounce, he went to the jewelry stores on Bolsa every Saturday with cash and bought more, and came home and knelt in the closet and turned the dial.

His wife, Thu, did the books for the salons and said nothing. They had one child, a son, Bảo, which means treasure. Lộc loved him the way he loved the gold, which was entirely.

When Bảo was fourteen he wanted diamond studs in his ears like the other boys at school. Lộc said no. Bảo asked for a year. At last Lộc went to a jewelry store on Bolsa and bought one chỉ of gold, a tenth of a tael, a little flat bar like a stick of gum, and when the jeweler said forty dollars to make it into studs, he said no. He took it home to the garage and cut it in two with a hacksaw and beat each half round on the vise with a ball-peen hammer and filed them and soldered posts on them with a torch from the hardware store. They came out lumpy, not quite the same size, and very yellow. Bảo wore them anyway. He wore them every day. The boys at school called him Bông Tai, Earrings, and after a while everybody did, and that was his name.

II. Yellow

Spring, 2009

In March of the year he turned sixteen, Bảo's eyes went yellow.

It started as tiredness. He fell asleep in class. He stopped eating. Then one morning Thu looked at him across the breakfast table and put down her spoon, because the whites of his eyes were the color of old piano keys, and by the end of the week his skin had gone the same color, and his urine was dark as tea.

Anh. He's sick. Take him to the doctor.

A doctor means the hospital. The hospital means three thousand dollars before they say hello. We have no insurance. You want to give them the house?

Then what will you do?

I'll take care of it, said Lộc. Without throwing money in the street.

He went to the herb shops on Bolsa, one after the other, and asked at each one what they gave for yellow eyes, as if he were asking about the weather. At each one they told him nhân trần, and gardenia, and rhubarb root, and he wrote it down. He bought the cheapest he could find and brewed it himself on the back burner in an old aluminum pot, and the whole house smelled of it, bitter, like wet hay. Bảo drank it. Bảo got worse. By the middle of April he could not get up the stairs, and then he could not get out of bed, and his belly swelled, and he slept most of the day and did not always know where he was when he woke.

Lộc saw then that his son was going to die. He called a doctor he knew from the salons, a man with a clinic in a strip mall on Magnolia, and asked him to come to the house after hours, as a favor, for cash.

The doctor came at nine at night in his golf shirt and looked at Bảo's eyes and pressed his belly and looked at his fingernails, and stood up.

Anh Lộc, he needs a hospital. A liver unit. Tonight. Maybe a transplant list. There's nothing I can do here. I have patients at seven in the morning. He would not take the money. He went out without looking at either of them.

Lộc stood in the kitchen a long time after the car was gone. When a boy is dying, he thought, people come. The aunts come, the cousins come, the neighbors, the whole salon. They come into the house and sit down and drink tea and look at everything. The television. The new refrigerator. The closet door. They talk afterward, at the market, at the temple, in the salons, and somebody's nephew hears it, and one night men in ski masks come through the back slider with a pistol and duct tape and make you open the safe. He had heard of it happening on this street. Everybody had.

The next morning he cleared out the garage, set up the folding cot, put the electric fan beside it, and carried his son out to it in his arms, because there was more air out there, he said, and the boy was too hot in the house. He left the big door up. Anyone who came to see Bông Tai could see him from the driveway.

III. The Garage

Before dawn, the last day of April

Two streets over there was a temple in a ranch house. The lawn had been taken out and gravel put down, and in the middle of the gravel stood a white statue of Quan Âm taller than the mailbox, pouring water from a vase, and on the porch there was a rack for shoes. Three nuns lived there in gray robes with shaved heads. The neighborhood called the eldest of them, who was not old, Sư Cô Tịnh Liên. Every morning at five-thirty she walked to the bakery on Bolsa to buy bread for the others and walked back, and she went by the Phạm house both ways. That morning, the other nuns said afterward, she went out a quarter of an hour early and took the long way around, which she never did.

Old Mr. Huỳnh across the street was always out in his driveway at that hour, doing his exercises, and he is the one who told it.

He said the boy was lying on the cot in the open garage with his face to the wall, toward the house, where his father had put him. He said the nun came up the sidewalk in the dark with her bag of bread, and stopped at the foot of the driveway, and looked in. The boy did not see her. Then, Mr. Huỳnh said, a light came out from her. Not from a flashlight. From her. It went up the driveway the way headlights do when a car turns in, and it lay across the cot and across the back wall of the garage, over the rakes and the water heater and the shelves of motor oil, so that every one of them threw a shadow. He said it was the color of the inside of a lemon. He said he stood there with his arms out in front of him like a fool and could not put them down.

The boy turned over, slowly, to see what it was.

He saw the nun standing at the end of his father's driveway with her bag of bread. He had passed that temple every day of his life on his way to the bus. He had never gone in. He had never taken a stick of incense there, or a bag of oranges, or sat on the floor and listened, because his father said they wanted money, and his father had known everything. Now he could not lift his hands.

He looked at her, and that was all he could do, and he did it with everything that was left in him.

Mr. Huỳnh said the nun put her palms together, and bowed to the boy in the garage, as if he were somebody, and said something he did not catch, and then said, quite clearly, Đủ rồi. That's enough. And turned and went on up the street with her bread, and the light went with her.

Bảo lay looking at the place where she had been. He did not turn back to the wall. Somewhere behind the houses on Bolsa the sky was going grey. He was thinking of her face, and he went on thinking of it, and somewhere in the thinking, quietly, the way a car settles when you turn the engine off, he died.

Mr. Huỳnh had taught school in Huế before the war ended, and he read English at the Westminster library every afternoon. A week later he found a passage in an old book there and copied it out by hand, and he kept it folded in his wallet for the rest of his life and showed it to anyone who would stand still.

O Bhagavat, if those beings who have directed their thought towards the highest perfect knowledge in other worlds, and who, after having heard my name, when I have obtained the Bodhi (knowledge), have meditated on me with serene thoughts; if at the moment of their death, after having approached them, surrounded by an assembly of Bhikshus, I should not stand before them, worshipped by them, that is, so that their thoughts should not be troubled, then may I not obtain the highest perfect knowledge.

— Larger Sukhâvatî-vyûha 8

IV. The Wall

The memorial park on Beach Boulevard · May and June

They cremated him on a Thursday. Lộc paid for the cheapest container the funeral home would allow and argued about the price of the chapel. He took the gold studs out of his son's ears himself before they closed the lid and put them in his shirt pocket, and he did not know why he did it, and he kept them there.

The urn went into a niche in the long granite wall at the memorial park on Beach Boulevard, the third row from the top, with a little brass vase and a photograph behind glass of Bảo in his school shirt, grinning, with the lumpy studs showing.

Then something happened to Uncle No that nobody on the side street had expected. He began to go to the wall every day. He went before he opened the salons and again after he closed them. He stood in front of the niche in his work clothes with his hands at his sides and cried out loud, in front of anybody, the way the old women cry at funerals in the countryside, and the groundskeepers learned to leave that row for last. Bảo ơi. Con ở đâu? Bảo. Where are you? He stopped eating. He let the salons run themselves. He stood in front of the wall in the sun until the security guard came and asked him to go home.

Thu bought the paper offerings for the forty-ninth day. A paper house with a two-car garage. Paper clothes, paper sneakers, a paper cell phone, bundles of paper gold. And at the shop on Bolsa she found a paper car, a long gold car with silver trim, a beautiful thing, sixty dollars. Lộc saw the price and said no and bought the twelve-dollar one, flat, with the wheels printed on the sides. They burned it all in a steel drum in the back yard, and he watched his son's car curl up and go black, and cried again, and did not know whether it was for the boy or the sixty dollars, and that made him cry harder.

•••

Where Bảo was, it was very bright.

He had woken the way you wake from a nap in the back seat of a car, not knowing for a moment where you are, and it was exactly as the old women at the temple had always said, the ones he had never listened to. There were lakes with golden sand on the bottom and stairs going down into them of gold and silver and beryl and crystal. There were trees made of jewels that rang when the wind went through them. There were lotuses on the water as big around as the wheels of a chariot. He was standing beside one of them, and he was wearing his studs, and they were not lumpy anymore.

He wondered how he had gotten there. He had never done one thing for it. He had not kept a single precept on purpose or given the temple one orange. And then he remembered the light in the garage, and the nun at the foot of the driveway, and the one look, and he knew.

Then he looked back the way you look back over your shoulder at a house you have moved out of, and he saw his father standing in front of a granite wall on Beach Boulevard in the heat, crying.

That man, Bảo thought. He wouldn't buy me medicine because it cost money, and now he stands there every day and cries. Somebody has to straighten him out.

V. Wheels

Beach Boulevard · late June

It was a weekday, past noon, and the memorial park was empty except for the sprinklers. Lộc stood at the wall saying what he always said. After a while he became aware that someone else was crying, not far off. A boy, at the end of the same row of niches, sixteen or so, in a school shirt, with both hands over his face, sobbing like a child.

Lộc thought, I am crying because my son is dead. What is that boy crying for? He went down the row.

Hey. Em. Why are you crying? You're young. You have everything. What's the matter?

The boy took his hands away from his face. He looked very much like Bảo. He wore two gold studs in his ears. Lộc could not stop looking at them.

I have a car, the boy said. A beautiful car. All gold. But it doesn't have any wheels. I've looked everywhere and I can't find wheels for it, and without wheels I'm going to die.

Lộc wiped his face on his sleeve. Something in his chest that had been closed since April came open a crack, and what came through was the oldest thing in him, the thing that had sewn gold into its trousers and got off the boat with two taels left.

That's all? Wheels? I'll get you wheels. Tell me what kind. Gold? Jade? Chrome? Silver? Tell me, and I'll buy them for you.

The boy looked at him with a strange expression. How big a set?

As big as you want.

The boy pointed up. It was a clear day, and the sun was over the freeway, and very faint in the blue on the other side of the sky, the way it is some afternoons, there was the moon.

I want those two. The sun and the moon. My car is gold, and those would match.

Lộc stared at him. Are you crazy? You're crying for something nobody can have. You'll die crying, and you still won't get the sun and the moon.

But you can see them, the boy said. Look. Right there. Both of them. Your son, nobody can see. He's not anywhere. So which one of us is crazy, Uncle? Me, crying for something that's up there in the sky, or you, crying for somebody who isn't here?

Lộc stood in the sprinklers' hiss with his mouth open. Then he said, very slowly, You're right. I'm the crazy one. I'm a child crying for the moon.

And it was as if he had been standing in a fire for two months, he said later, a fire somebody kept feeding with cooking oil, and someone had poured a bucket of cold water over his head. The thing that had been stuck in his chest since April, like a fishbone in the throat, came out. He breathed. He stopped crying. He stood there in the hot afternoon, dry-eyed, cool.

Who are you, em? Whose son are you?

Ba, the boy said. It's me. Bông Tai. The one you burned.

Lộc did not fall down. He was surprised, afterward, that he had not fallen down.

Where are you?

In the West. In the Land of Ultimate Bliss. The one they talk about at the temple.

How? You never went to the temple. You never gave anything. You never kept a precept in your life. I know. I raised you.

I was sick in the garage, Bảo said. I couldn't lift my hands. The Sư Cô from the temple on the next street came by with her bread, and she looked at me, and I looked at her. That was all. I looked at her with my whole heart. That's all I did.

Lộc felt his whole body go warm, from his feet up, the way it goes warm when you get into a bath. Is that true? One look?

It's true. Ba, listen to me. Go to her. Take refuge. Keep the five precepts. Don't kill anything, don't take anything that isn't given, don't lie, don't drink, don't run around on Má. You have all that gold in the closet. Give some away. Invite her to the house, feed her, ask her whatever you want to ask. You'll see.

I'll do it, Lộc said. Everything you said. You're my teacher now.

And there was nobody at the end of the row, only the sprinklers ticking around on the grass and the moon very faint over the freeway.

VI. The Meal

The house on the side street · a Sunday in July

He went home and told Thu they were inviting the Sư Cô and the other nuns to the house to be fed. Thu sat down on the kitchen floor.

Then he walked the two streets to the temple in his work clothes. He did not take off his shoes on the porch, or bow to the Buddha on the altar, or greet anyone. He found Sư Cô Tịnh Liên in the kitchen cutting tofu and stood in the doorway.

Sư Cô. Come with the others and eat at my house on Sunday. I want to ask you something.

She looked at him, and at his shoes, and put down the knife, and said yes.

He spent more money that week than he had spent in twenty-eight years. He hired a vegetarian caterer on Bolsa and ordered everything on the list. He bought new folding tables and borrowed chairs from the salons, and a white cloth for the table where the nuns would sit, and set up an altar in the living room with Bảo's photograph and a bowl of oranges stacked in a pyramid, the most expensive oranges in the market.

The whole side street came, and half of Bolsa. They came the way people come to see an accident. Some of them came because Chú Không was paying for a meal and they wanted to see it with their own eyes. Some of them came because they had heard he was going to ask the Sư Cô a question, and they had decided that Uncle No would ask her something she could not answer, and they wanted to be there when he did. And some of them came because they had heard about Mr. Huỳnh and the light in the driveway, and they wanted to see what the Sư Cô would do. The house filled up and the garage filled up and people stood out in the driveway in the heat, holding paper plates.

When the nuns had eaten, and the plates were cleared, Lộc took a low stool from the kitchen and set it down in front of Sư Cô Tịnh Liên and sat on it, so that his head was lower than hers, and asked his question in front of everybody.

Sư Cô. Can a person be born in the Pure Land without ever giving anything to the temple? Without keeping the precepts? Without chanting, without the full-moon days, without one single good deed? Just by believing, one time?

The room went quiet. Out in the driveway somebody shushed somebody.

Why are you asking me? she said. Your son already told you.

When?

Last Tuesday, at the memorial park on Beach Boulevard. You were crying at the wall, and a boy was crying at the end of the row, and you went to him and said, Hey, em, why are you crying, you're young, you have everything. And she told the rest of it, every word, the car and the wheels and the gold and the jade and the chrome and the silver, and the sun and the moon, and who was the crazy one, and the bucket of water, and the five precepts. Lộc had told no one. Not even Thu. He sat on his stool and listened to his own words come out of her mouth, and the tears ran down his face, and he did not wipe them.

It isn't one, and it isn't two, she said, to the room. It isn't a hundred or a thousand. Nobody can count how many.

People shifted. Somebody in the back laughed, not unkindly. A woman from the salons said, loud enough, That's easy to say.

Sư Cô Tịnh Liên turned her head toward the altar where the photograph was, as if someone had spoken to her from there.

Bảo, she said. Come here, please.

•••

Everyone who was in that house tells it a little differently, and none of them have stopped telling it. They agree that the light came first, the color of the inside of a lemon, and filled the living room so that the paper plates and the folding chairs and the faces all threw shadows backward. They agree that Bảo was standing in front of the altar in his school shirt, and that he was taller than the ceiling and the ceiling was not in the way. Some say he had come in a gold car and some say a gold house and Mr. Huỳnh says it was a lotus as big as a tire, but they agree about the earrings, two gold studs, round and bright, not lumpy at all. He put his palms together and bowed to the Sư Cô, all the way down, and stood up again.

Bảo, she said. What did you do, when you were here, to get all this?

Sư Cô, I looked at you one time, he said. From the cot in the garage. That's all.

One look?

One look.

The woman from the salons put her hand over her mouth. Old women all over the room began to say the Buddha's name, Nam mô A Di Đà Phật, Nam mô A Di Đà Phật, low, the way the wind goes through a palm tree, and the people out in the driveway pushed in to see, and nobody stopped them.

Sư Cô Tịnh Liên waited until they were quiet. Then she said it to all of them, sitting on the borrowed chair with her hands in her lap, in the plain voice of somebody reading out the time of the next bus.

Beings are not born in that Buddha country of the Tathâgata Amitâyus as a reward and result of good works performed in this present life. No, whatever son or daughter of a family shall hear the name of the blessed Amitâyus, the Tathâgata, and having heard it, shall keep it in mind, and with thoughts undisturbed shall keep it in mind for one, two, three, four, five, six or seven nights,—when that son or daughter of a family comes to die, then that Amitâyus, the Tathâgata, surrounded by an assembly of disciples and followed by a host of Bodhisattvas, will stand before them at their hour of death, and they will depart this life with tranquil minds.

— Smaller Sukhâvatî-vyûha 10

When the light went, the boy went with it. Uncle No was sitting on his low stool with his hands open on his knees, looking at nothing, smiling.

VII. Sun and Moon

The side street · afterward

People on the side street said afterward that the money came out of that house the way water comes out of a broken main. The temple got a new roof, and a parking lot that satisfied the city, and a van. The herb shop on Bolsa found an envelope under its door with a note in it, for the next family who cannot pay. The forty-dollar jeweler got forty dollars and did not know what for. At Tết the children on the side street opened their red envelopes and found hundred-dollar bills, so new they crackled, and ran to show their mothers. Nobody called him Chú Không after that, though for a while nobody could think what else to call him.

He kept the gold studs. He did not give those away. He set them on the altar in the living room in front of Bảo's photograph, side by side on a saucer, the two lumpy yellow things he had hammered out on the vise in the garage to save forty dollars.

In the mornings, when he lit the incense before he went to open the salons, the sun came in low through the front window and lay across the altar, and one of the studs, the bigger one, caught it and burned like a coal. The other one sat just outside the light, in the shadow of the incense bowl, pale and round and plain. He would stand there with the incense in his hands until the smoke reached the ceiling, looking at the two of them, before he went out.

Background

The verse

All that we are is the result of what we have thought: it is founded on our thoughts, it is made up of our thoughts. If a man speaks or acts with a pure thought, happiness follows him, like a shadow that never leaves him.Dhammapada 2, tr. F. Max Müller (1881)

In the commentary, a Brahmin of Sāvatthī is so stingy that people call him Adinnapubbaka, “Never Gave.” He beats out a pair of earrings for his only son himself to save a goldsmith's fee, so the boy is known as Maṭṭhakuṇḍalī, “Burnished Earrings.” When the son falls ill with jaundice, the father doses him with home remedies rather than pay a physician, and when the boy is dying he lays him out on the terrace so that visitors will not see the wealth inside. The Buddha, passing on his alms round, sends out a ray of light; the boy turns, sees him, and dies with a heart full of faith, and is reborn among the gods. Later he appears to his grieving father at the cremation ground, weeping for a pair of wheels for his golden chariot, and asks for the sun and the moon. The father, shamed out of his grief, takes refuge, invites the Buddha to a meal, and asks whether anyone reaches heaven by faith alone. Little Saigon, 2009

After 1975, and above all after the boat exodus that began in 1978, refugees from Vietnam settled in Westminster and Garden Grove, and by the mid-1980s their business district was known as Little Saigon, now the largest concentration of Vietnamese people outside Vietnam. Many first-generation families ran small businesses such as nail salons and restaurants. The boat people often paid for their passage in gold, and many passed through camps like the one on Pulau Bidong in Malaysia. A 1992 study for U.S. law enforcement described home-invasion gangs that chose victims they believed ran businesses and kept their savings at home instead of in banks. Gold passed a thousand dollars an ounce for the first time in March 2008, as the financial crisis began.

About one in twelve Asian Americans and Pacific Islanders lives with chronic hepatitis B, according to the Centers for Disease Control, as many as two in three of them without knowing it, and the group accounts for more than half of all Americans with the infection. In Vietnam, as across East Asia, Pure Land devotion to the Buddha Amitābha is an important part of Buddhist practice. His eighteenth vow promises rebirth in his western paradise, Sukhāvatī, to anyone who sincerely trusts him and calls on him even ten times. The Amitābha Sūtra, or Smaller Sukhāvatīvyūha, describes that land's golden sands and jewel trees and its lotuses as large as chariot wheels. The passages in the story are from F. Max Müller's translations of the two Sukhāvatīvyūha sūtras in the Sacred Books of the East (1894).
