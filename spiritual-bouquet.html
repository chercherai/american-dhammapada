I. The Last Conference

The White House, St. Louis County · March 1949

Every year in the third week of Lent, Norbert Vogt packed a shaving kit and two clean shirts and drove down the river road with the men of his parish to the White House, where the Jesuits had been giving retreats to laymen since 1922. It stood on the bluffs south of the city, eighty acres of oaks and lawn above the Mississippi. For three days nobody spoke. When the bell rang the men went to the chapel and sat in rows in their good suits and listened to Father Brockmeier.

He had given the White House retreats for twenty-four years. He was a big, slow German from Hermann, on the Missouri, with a farmer's hands and a voice that did not rise, and the men who came back every year could have given his conferences for him. Thursday night, what a man is made for. Friday, death and judgment. Saturday, the two standards, Christ's and the other one. Sunday, love. They called him Father Brock. They had brought their sons to him, and he had married the sons.

Norbert was forty-six. He was regional sales manager for Ralston Purina in southeast Missouri and southern Illinois, the Bootheel and Little Egypt: eleven salesmen and two hundred and some dealers. He spent four nights a week in hotels in Sikeston and Cape Girardeau, talking Pig Chow with men in overalls under red-and-white checkerboard signs. Over the desks at Checkerboard Square hung the four squares Mr. Danforth had drawn for his salesmen, physical, mental, social and religious, a man's life in balance. Norbert had made fifteen retreats. He went home from every one of them a cleaner man, the way a man is cleaner after a haircut, and it lasted about as long.

On the Sunday afternoon, at the last conference, Father Brock did what he did every year. He opened the little black book he had carried since his novitiate and read them the page he had read on Thursday night, so they would take it home with them.

Man is created to praise, reverence, and serve God our Lord, and by this means to save his soul. And the other things on the face of the earth are created for man and that they may help him in prosecuting the end for which he is created. From this it follows that man is to use them as much as they help him on to his end, and ought to rid himself of them so far as they hinder him as to it. For this it is necessary to make ourselves indifferent to all created things in all that is allowed to the choice of our free will and is not prohibited to it; so that, on our part, we want not health rather than sickness, riches rather than poverty, honor rather than dishonor, long rather than short life, and so in all the rest; desiring and choosing only what is most conducive for us to the end for which we are created.

— Spiritual Exercises, Principle and Foundation

Then he closed the book and took off his glasses.

The doctors at Desloge tell me I will be here until about the Fourth of July, he said. That is four months, if you are counting, and you will be. I wanted you to hear it from me and not from the Sisters. Now go home to your wives.

He gave them the blessing and went out by the sacristy door.

For a while nobody moved. When the bell rang the end of silence, seventy men came out onto the lawn and stood in knots along the bluff with their hats in their hands. Al Huelsmann, who had a plumbing business on Gravois and had been head captain for the parish men since before the war, went from knot to knot, and in every one somebody asked him the same thing. Al, what are we going to do? Al said they would do something. He did not know yet what.

Norbert stood by himself at the rail. Below him a towboat was pushing nine barges up the river against the spring water, so slowly that it hardly seemed to move.

He was thinking about his first retreat, in 1934, when half his dealers were carrying their farmers on credit and he could not sleep. On the Saturday he had found Father Brock out here on the bluff with his breviary, and Father Brock had closed it on a finger and looked at him.

You make a nice retreat, Norbert. Some year, make the Exercises. All of them. Thirty days.

Norbert had laughed. Thirty days. Purina gave him two weeks a year and he had a mortgage on Delor Street. Father Brock had laughed too, and opened his book again.

It came to Norbert at the rail that Father Brock had said it to him once and never again, and that in four months nobody alive would ever have said it to him at all. He had heard the page in the black book read fifteen times. He did not know that he had ever once done what it said.

That night he told Agnes what he was going to do. She sat at the kitchen table a long time. Then she asked who was going to cut the grass.

II. The Bouquet

South St. Louis · April and May

The retreat captains met on Tuesday nights in Al Huelsmann's basement among the pipe fittings, and by the end of April they knew what they were going to do. They were going to give Father Brock a spiritual bouquet.

Every Catholic child in St. Louis knew what that was. You did not send flowers, or not only flowers. You sent a card, and on the card was written what you had offered up for the person: so many Masses heard, so many Communions, so many rosaries, so many Stations of the Cross, so many visits to the Blessed Sacrament, so many of the little one-line prayers the Sisters called ejaculations. Al's idea was to send the cards to every parish that sent men to the White House, forty-one parishes. The Holy Name men and the sodalities and the schoolchildren of the Notre Dame Sisters would fill them in, and the captains would add the whole thing up.

Frank Dunne, a bookkeeper at Anheuser-Busch, brought home an adding machine from the brewery. The cards came back by the hundred. By the middle of May the bouquet stood at 11,206 Masses, 9,840 Communions, 41,315 rosaries, 3,077 Stations of the Cross and 312,000 ejaculations, which the schoolchildren had counted on their fingers and the Sisters had certified. A bookbinder on Cherokee Street bound the cards in red leather and stamped the rosaries on the cover in gold.

There were real flowers too. By May the Sisters of St. Mary at Firmin Desloge had stopped carrying them into his room and were sending them on to the charity wards, and there were still lilies on the radiator. The captains made a list and came two at a time every evening, so that Father Brock was never alone. When the Sisters would not let them in, they sat on the bench in the corridor with their hats on their knees and asked each other in low voices who would give the retreats now. What are we going to do.

They planned a testimonial dinner for the twenty-second of June, seven hundred men at the Chase Hotel. They wanted to give him a car. The Father Provincial wrote to Al, very kindly, that a Jesuit under a vow of poverty had no use for a Buick, so they took up a collection for an oil portrait for the White House dining room instead.

Norbert Vogt's name was not on the list for the corridor bench. He did not come to the basement on Tuesdays. Al telephoned the house on Delor Street and got Agnes.

He's on a retreat, Agnes said.

He was on a retreat. In March. With me.

He's on another one.

III. Number Seventeen

The Coral Court, Watson Road · May

He had his two weeks of vacation, and he asked the general sales manager for sixteen days more without pay. He said it was a family matter. Agnes said that was a lie and Norbert said it was not. He went to the Jesuit residence at the university and asked for a priest to give him the Exercises, and the Father Minister, when he had stopped looking surprised, gave him Father Gallagher, who was thirty-four and taught chemistry, and had made the thirty days twice, as every Jesuit does, and never once given them.

The book said a man making the Exercises would profit more the more he separated himself from friends and acquaintances, leaving his house for another room where he could get to Mass each day and nobody he knew would get in his way. Norbert could not afford a month in a hotel downtown. So he took a room by the week at the Coral Court Motel, out on Watson Road where Watson Road was Route 66, twenty minutes from his own front door, because it was cheap and because nobody he knew would ever admit to having been inside it.

Everybody in St. Louis knew what it was. The Coral Court was a long curve of low yellow buildings in the trees, two rooms to a building, each room with its own garage, so that a man could drive in and pull the door down behind him and nobody going by on the highway would see his car. The walls were glazed tile the color of egg yolk, with brown trim, and the windows were glass block. The man at the desk gave him the key to Number 17, looked at the one suitcase, looked past him at the empty seat of the Pontiac, and said, Anybody joining you?

No, said Norbert.

The man at the desk had heard that before.

He went in on Monday, the ninth of May, and pulled the garage door down behind him, and for thirty days he did not speak except to a priest. He walked a mile up Watson Road every morning to the six-thirty Mass among the old ladies. He prayed five hours a day, an hour at a time, on the one hard chair, and when an hour was bad he sat it out to the end, because the book warned that the enemy was not a little used to making a man cut it short. At seven every evening Father Gallagher came and sat on the end of the bed, because Norbert had the chair, and gave him the points for the next day in about ten minutes, and left.

The first week was sin. The book told him to choose a single fault and keep account of it. He was to draw a mark like a large G with a line after it, and a second line under the first, and every time he fell he was to make a dot on the line, the first line for the morning and the second for the afternoon. He used the backs of Purina order blanks, which had the checkerboard printed in the corner. His fault was that he said more than was so. He had sold feed for twenty years by saying a little more than was so, about the feed and the other company's feed, and about himself, and to Agnes. On the first morning there were eleven dots on the first line by noon. Each time he caught himself he was to put his hand on his breast, which the book said could be done in front of a crowd without anyone noticing. There was no crowd in Number 17. He put his hand on his breast eleven times and felt like a fool all eleven.

The second week was the life of Christ. He sat in the yellow room with the light coming in through the glass block wavy and gold, as through water, and tried to see the stable at Bethlehem, and saw the cow lots of Pemiscot County and the dealers' boys throwing sacks of Chow onto trucks. Father Gallagher said that was all right. That was where most people saw it. There was a page in the book for men who were already married and could not choose a new life. In place of a choice it gave them a reckoning: how large a house to keep, how to teach and rule it by word and by example, how much to take for the household and how much to give to the poor. Norbert spent four days on that page and filled both sides of nine order blanks.

By the third week, the Passion, his sister-in-law Hildegard had told the Altar Society that Agnes had told her that Norbert was out at the Coral Court, and that she did not care what kind of a retreat Agnes wanted to call it. By the Holy Name breakfast on the first Sunday in June it was all over the parish.

IV. Desloge

Grand Avenue · the second of June

On Thursday afternoon Al Huelsmann and Frank Dunne drove out Watson Road in Al's plumbing truck, because they had decided that somebody had to. They walked the curve reading numbers and knocked at Number 17, and Norbert opened the door in his shirtsleeves.

Al had a speech ready. He had worked on it in the truck. When he saw the room he forgot it. The bed was made as tight as a barracks bed. On the dresser stood the crucifix from Norbert's bedroom at home, propped against the mirror, and next to it an order pad and a little black book, and nothing else. There was no woman. There was no bottle. There was no sign that anybody had been in that room but a man and a chair.

Norbert, Al said. He's been asking. Seventy of us on that bench, and you never came once. Not once.

Norbert looked at the two of them. He did not say anything. Then he went and got his hat.

Father Brock had a room high in Desloge looking east over the roofs toward the river. The Sisters had cleared out most of the flowers, but there were still lilies on the radiator and peonies in a milk bottle on the sill, and on the bed table lay the red leather album with 41,315 Rosaries on the cover in gold. He was yellow now, and smaller in the bed than seemed possible for so big a man. Five or six of the captains were on the bench in the corridor. They got up and came and stood in the doorway.

Norbert, said Father Brock. Where have you been?

Norbert stood at the foot of the bed with his hat in his hands. Then he took a pencil and an order blank out of his shirt pocket and wrote on the back of the blank and handed it over.

Father Brock held it out at arm's length, because his glasses were somewhere. Making the Exercises. Coral Court, No. 17. Day 25. You said July.

He began to laugh. He laughed until a Sister came in from the corridor, and then he coughed, and she held the basin, and when he could talk again he waved her off and lay back with the order blank on his chest.

Al, he said. How many rosaries?

Forty-one thousand three hundred and fifteen, Father.

I'm grateful. I mean it. I'll need every one of them where I'm going. He turned his head on the pillow toward the men in the doorway. And the flowers, and the dinner, and the picture. I'm not too proud for any of it. But I stood up in that chapel twenty-four years and told you men what you were made for, and here is the one who went and did it. Al, hand me my book. It's in the drawer.

Al found it in the drawer of the bed table. Father Brock found the page without looking, the way a man finds the light switch in his own house, and read it to them in a voice not much above a whisper.

In these he will, ordinarily, more benefit himself, the more he separates himself from all friends and acquaintances and from all earthly care, as by changing from the house where he was dwelling, and taking another house or room to live in, in as much privacy as he can, so that it be in his power to go each day to Mass and to Vespers, without fear that his acquaintances will put obstacles in his way.

— Spiritual Exercises, Twentieth Annotation

He closed the book on his finger.

Love ought to be put more in deeds than in words. That's in here too. Any of you who love me, I don't need your Buick. Do what he's doing.

Then he looked at Norbert.

Go on back to your motel.

V. The Contemplation

Route 66 and the Bootheel · June to August 1949

On the last day, the seventh of June, Norbert got up at five and did not go to Mass right away. He sat in the chair in Number 17 with the book open to the Contemplation to Gain Love. The light came up behind the glass block, grey and then yellow, the color of the tile, and the trucks began on Route 66, one, then another, then too many to count, carrying whatever they were carrying to Joplin and Tulsa and the coast. The book told him to look at how God dwells in creatures: in the elements, giving them being, in the plants, giving them growth, in the animals, giving them feeling, in men, giving them understanding, and so in him. He looked. He saw the trucks and the yellow tile and his own two hands on his knees, and for about as long as it takes a cup of coffee to go cold there was nothing in Number 17 that had not been given to him, and nothing in it he wanted to keep. Then he said the prayer at the end of the page, the one that gives back to God a man's liberty and his memory and his intellect and all his will, and walked up Watson Road to Mass.

The man at the desk took back the key. Have a good time?

Yes, said Norbert. It was the first word he had said out loud in thirty days to anybody but a priest.

•••

The dinner at the Chase went on without Father Brock on the twenty-second of June. He sent word by Father Gallagher that the men should eat, and seven hundred of them ate. He died on Friday, the eighth of July, four months less five days after he had told them, and was buried from the College Church on Grand Avenue with the red album under his hands. The portrait, when it was finished, did not look like him at all.

That fall Al Huelsmann went to the Jesuit residence and asked for somebody to give him the Exercises the long way round, the way the book allows for a man with a business to run, an hour and a half a day for as many months as it takes. Frank Dunne went after him. By the next Lent there were nine of them, and Father Gallagher had written to the Provincial for help.

Norbert went back on the road. On a Tuesday in August he sat in the Pontiac outside the Purina dealer's in Sikeston at seven in the morning, before the store opened, with the windows down and the heat already coming up off the gravel and the checkerboard sign over the door. On the seat beside him lay an order blank. He drew a G on the back of it, and two lines after the G, one for the morning and one for the afternoon. Then he went in to sell feed. A little before eight, talking with the dealer about the price of corn, he put his hand on his breast, and the dealer, who had known him twenty years, did not notice a thing.

Background

The verse

Let no one forget his own duty for the sake of another's, however great; let a man, after he has discerned his own duty, be always attentive to his duty.Dhammapada 166, tr. F. Max Müller (1881)

In the commentary, the Buddha tells his monks that he will pass away in four months. Seven hundred monks who have not yet entered the stream are shaken, and they stay at his side, whispering to one another, Friends, what are we to do? Elder Attadattha (the name means "one's own good") thinks instead that he has not yet freed himself from the pollutants, and resolves to strive for arahatship with all his might while the Teacher still lives, so he stops keeping company with the others. They bring him before the Buddha as if he had done wrong. The Buddha praises him, says that those who honor him with perfumes and garlands do not truly honor him, and that only those who practice the Dhamma do, and speaks the verse. Attadattha reaches arahatship. Read the full commentary story, or about the Spiritual Exercises whose words stand in for the verse.

St. Louis, 1949

Since 1922 the White House Jesuit Retreat has given three-day preached retreats, based on the Spiritual Exercises of Ignatius of Loyola, on an eighty-acre campus on the Mississippi bluffs south of St. Louis. Laymen who wanted more than one retreat a year formed the first of the monthly Manresan clubs in 1921, and parish captains still recruit the men for each weekend. The full Exercises, in four "weeks," take about thirty days, and most laypeople who make them now do so in daily life, following Ignatius's Nineteenth Annotation, rather than by withdrawing for a month as the Twentieth describes. The translation quoted here is Father Elder Mullan's of 1914, from the Spanish autograph. Its Particular Examen tells the retreatant to mark on the lines of a G a dot for each time he falls into the fault he is working on. A spiritual bouquet is a Catholic gift of prayers rather than flowers: a card listing the Masses, Communions, rosaries and other devotions offered for someone.

The Coral Court Motel was built in 1941 on Watson Road, part of Route 66, in suburban Marlborough. Its two-unit buildings of yellow glazed tile and glass block, in the streamline moderne style, each had a garage, and, as Built St. Louis puts it, the motel "became famous for illicit encounters, as the individual garage units allowed one to park the car out of sight." It was demolished in 1995. Ralston Purina, which sold its animal feeds as "Chows," took its checkerboard from the four squares, physical, mental, social and religious, that its founder William H. Danforth drew in his 1931 book I Dare You! The Jesuits of Saint Louis University and the Sisters of St. Mary opened Firmin Desloge Hospital on Grand Avenue in 1933, about a mile south of the Jesuits' College Church.
