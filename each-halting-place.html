Walking the American WayCommentary 8.5 · Sāriputta’s Uncle · 2,311 words · 11 min

Each Halting Place

To soothe by a kindness one heart has more grace,
Than a thousand prostrations at each halting place.
— Sa'di, Bustan II, tr. G. S. Davie

Chunīn shunīdam, in the kabob houses on Fremont Boulevard.

I. The Envelope

Fremont, California · the first Friday of February 2026

Haji Wahid sold floors. He had come to Fremont in 1984 with a wife, a sister, two rugs and a cousin's telephone number. He sold the two rugs, and then other men's rugs, and when the people who bought rugs began to want laminate he learned laminate. Now there was a showroom on Fremont Boulevard with forty feet of sample racks, a warehouse in Union City, three vans, and his name on the pens.

On the first Friday of every month, after the prayer, he sat down at the desk in the back of the showroom and counted ten hundred-dollar bills into an envelope. He had done it since the spring of 2004. He wrote the month on the flap in pencil and walked the envelope two doors down to the travel agency, where the man at the rear counter wrote a line in a book and made a telephone call, and by Sunday a cousin of the mullah in his father's village above Charikar had the money in his hand. In twenty-two years the envelope had built a mosque of fired brick with a blue door. Then it had built a wall around the mosque, a well, a room for the boys to learn in, a loudspeaker, and last year four solar panels. A stonecutter had put his father's name over the door. Wahid kept a photograph of it under the glass of the desk. He had never seen it any other way.

His sister's son came in while he was licking the flap.

Yusuf was forty-three. He had been a clever boy. At twenty-four he had gone back over there to interpret for the Americans, for more money than Wahid cleared in those years, and he had come home at thirty with the money gone nobody knew where and a habit of sitting with his back to a wall. He had not married. He lived in an apartment in Sacramento and interpreted by the hour at the immigration court, Dari and Pashto. What he did with the rest of his time his mother had told Wahid on the telephone the night before, crying. Every Sunday since December he drove to a prison in the desert, eleven hours there and back, to see an old man who was no relation.

Sit, Wahid said. Your mother says you are killing yourself on the highway. For what? What good does it do?

Yusuf sat. He looked at the envelope.

Māmā, he said. Do you do any good? One thing?

It was not how a man spoke to his mother's brother. But Wahid had been waiting twenty-two years for somebody to ask.

This, he said, and put his hand flat on it. A thousand dollars. Every month since you were in college.

Who gets it?

The mullah. For the mosque, for the boys' teacher, for whatever the mosque needs.

And what do you get?

Wahid was not ashamed of the answer. A house. There. Whoever builds a house for God, God builds a house for him in the Garden.

Who told you that?

Mullah Sahib. It is hadith, bachem. Ask anybody.

I know it is hadith. I am asking who has been up that road to look. You have not been, and Mullah Sahib has not been. He knows the road as far as the counter at the travel agency. Yusuf said it without heat, the way he would give a client the date of a hearing. I know one man who knows the rest of it. He is where I go on Sundays. Come with me and I will ask him for you.

Wahid looked at his father's blue door under the glass. Then he looked at his sister's son, briefly, the way he had looked at him for years, as a man glances at a bill he means to dispute.

What time, he said.

II. The Road

Interstate 5 · Sunday, before light

They left at four in one of the vans, because Wahid would not ride eleven hours in a Corolla. Yusuf's mother came out to the driveway in her coat with a pot of qabuli wrapped in two bath towels. She set it in the back among the sample boards and stood there until they turned the corner.

Over the Altamont and down into the valley the fog lay on the road as if it had been poured. Yusuf drove at fifty with the trucks. At the Westley rest area Wahid got out, washed at the tap with the cold running into his sleeves, took a carpet sample from the van, a two-foot square of gray Saxony with the price on the back, and prayed the dawn prayer on it beside the picnic tables. Yusuf prayed behind him on the concrete.

After that there was light, and Yusuf talked.

The teacher's name was Abdul Latif, and in Herat they called him Ustad Latif. He had taught Persian literature to boys for thirty-one years in a government high school. When the schools for girls were shut he taught girls, Sa'di and Hafez and their own grammar, in his guest room with the curtain drawn, until a neighbor warned him. He was sixty-seven. He went out through Iran. Brazil was giving visas then, so he flew to São Paulo and slept three weeks in the airport under a blanket hung over a row of chairs, and then he walked north with people a third his age, through the jungle between Colombia and Panama and on through Mexico. In the fall of 2023 he asked for asylum at the fence south of San Diego. They let him go on to a former student in Sacramento with a monitor on his ankle and a date to check in.

In Sacramento he did what he had always done. On Friday nights, in an apartment off Fulton Avenue, he read the Bustan with whoever came: taxi men, a pharmacist, two widows, Yusuf. He read a story and then asked each person what was in it. He did not correct anybody. He waited, and the next person said something better.

They called him at six in the morning on the first of December, Yusuf said. Him and the others. Come to the federal building now. He put on his good coat. The people who stand outside with clipboards counted six that day. The handcuffs went on in the doorway.

What did he do?

They have not said.

At Kettleman City Wahid bought two coffees and would not let Yusuf pay. The road ran on between almond orchards coming into bloom, then east, then up through Tehachapi among the white windmills, hundreds of them turning on the ridges, and down the far side into a brown country without trees. California City had streets. Wahid had laid floors in three counties, and he noticed the streets before anything else. They were paved and had their names on posts, and they ran off square to the horizon through the creosote with not one house on them.

Somebody was going to build a city, Yusuf said. In the fifties. They did the streets first.

III. The Glass

California City · eleven o'clock

It had been a prison, and it was one. There were three gates that slid. A woman behind a window took their licenses, and they waited in a room with six chairs. Then a guard gave each of them a badge and pressed a stamp on the back of each right hand that left no mark at all. They put keys, belts and Wahid's prayer beads in a gray bin and took off their shoes.

The visiting room was large and nearly empty, with two vending machines at the far end. Along one side ran a row of booths with glass in them, and at each booth a chair and a telephone on the wall. At the first one a woman was holding a baby up to the glass.

They brought him in on the other side. Wahid had expected a holy beggar, or else a professor. He saw a small man in the loose clothes they give them, with a clipped white beard and heavy glasses mended at the hinge, who looked at Yusuf first and laughed with pleasure, and then looked at Wahid and put his hand on his heart. There was one telephone. Yusuf took it.

Ustad, this is my mother's brother, Haji Abdul Wahid of Fremont. He is building a mosque in Parwan. He has sent a thousand dollars every month for twenty-two years, and he hopes for a house in the Garden. I told him he does not know the road there and his mullah does not. Tell him.

He held the receiver out, and Wahid took it. It was warm.

Haji Sahib, said the voice in his ear, in the Persian of Herat. Is it as he says?

It is, Ustad-jan.

The old man nodded. He was in no hurry, though somewhere a clock was running on both of them.

A mosque is a good thing. I have prayed in a hundred that men like you built, and I asked a blessing on every builder. Send your thousand. Send it a hundred years. His eyes moved to Yusuf, who stood with his arms folded. Now look there. That one has come to me ten Sundays. I am not his father. He drives through the night to hold this telephone, and he asks me who in my unit has no lawyer, and he carries the names out in his head, because they do not let him bring a pen. If one time you looked at him with a clear heart, Haji Sahib, the way you looked at me when I came through that door, for as long as it takes to say bismillah, it would weigh more than the hundred years. Or if you put one spoon of rice on his plate with your own hand. I do not say it to shame you. You asked for the road.

Then he said two lines into the mouthpiece, quietly, as a man gives a street address. Ba ehsānē āsūda kardan dilē, beh az alf rak'at ba har manzilē.

Do not think that because you have worshipped so fine,
You have carried a gift to this Presence Divine!
To soothe by a kindness one heart has more grace,
Than a thousand prostrations at each halting place.

— Sa'di, Bustan II, tr. G. S. Davie

Wahid had known the lines since he was nine. A master in Charikar had put them into forty boys with a mulberry switch. He had said them himself, at funerals, to men richer than he was.

He turned his head. It was a small thing to do. Yusuf's lips were moving a little. He was going over the names. There was gray in his beard that Wahid had never taken in, and his collar was frayed and very clean, and he stood in that room the way the old man sat in it, as if nothing in it could be taken from him.

Wahid looked for as long as it takes to say it. He had pulled up a great deal of bad carpet in his life, and now and then there was oak under it, tight and dry, laid by somebody who knew how. That was the nearest he could come to it afterward.

Ustad, he said into the telephone, and could not go on. The old man raised one hand a few inches off the counter, as you do for a student who need not finish the sentence.

Let me put money in your account, Wahid said, when he could. Whatever they allow.

Twenty dollars. There is a boy from Guinea in my unit whose people do not know where he is. It will buy him soup and a call.

The guard said time.

IV. The Plate

The parking lot · noon

On the way out a guard passed a violet lamp over the backs of their hands, and the stamp that had not been there showed for a second, a small green wheel. Then they were through the three gates with their shoes on.

The wind was blowing. Yusuf opened the back of the van and unwrapped the pot, which was still warm in the middle. He found the two plates his mother had put in, and the spoon.

Wahid took the spoon out of his hand.

He was the elder. In sixty-six years nobody younger had been served in front of him. He dug down to where the rice was hot and lifted a spoonful with the carrots and raisins on it and a piece of the lamb, and put it on his nephew's plate. Then he put another.

Eat, he said. You have to drive.

Yusuf looked at the plate and then at him. He ate standing up, with the wind lifting a corner of the towel, and Wahid, who had not yet taken any for himself, watched him.

On the first Friday of March he counted ten bills into the envelope and wrote the month on the flap, as before. A village is not hurt by a well.

On the Sunday after that the van went south at four with the sample boards out and the seats bolted back in, nine people from the apartment off Fulton Avenue, and a pot the size of a truck tire. At Westley he passed out the carpet squares, gray Saxony, one to a person, and they stood in a row beside the picnic tables in the fog and put their foreheads down on them.

Background

The verse

If a man for a hundred years sacrifice month after month with a thousand, and if he but for one moment pay homage to a man whose soul is grounded (in true knowledge), better is that homage than sacrifice for a hundred years.Dhammapada 106, tr. F. Max Müller (1881)

In the commentary, the elder Sāriputta visits his uncle, a Brahmin, and asks him whether he ever does a single good deed. The uncle answers that month after month he gives a thousand coins to the naked ascetics, because his teachers have told him this is the way to the world of Brahmā. Sāriputta says that neither he nor his teachers know the way there, and takes him to the Buddha. The Buddha tells him that a hundred years of such giving would bear less fruit than a single moment of looking at his disciple with a believing heart, or one spoonful of boiled rice put in his bowl. Fremont and California City, 2026

Afghan families began arriving in the East Bay as refugees after the Soviet invasion of 1979, and Fremont came to hold the best-known Afghan American community in the country; Sacramento County now has the largest. After the Taliban took Kabul in August 2021, Brazil issued humanitarian visas to several thousand Afghans, and some of them went on north overland, through the Darién Gap and Mexico, to ask for asylum at the United States border. Hundreds camped for weeks in the São Paulo airport on the way. In the days after an Afghan man shot two National Guard soldiers in Washington in November 2025, immigration lawyers reported a wave of arrests of Afghans, most of them in Northern California. In Sacramento, men who had asked for asylum at the border were telephoned at six in the morning on December 1, told to report to the federal building at once, and handcuffed as they came in; volunteers counted six arrests that day. The Associated Press, which reported this, could not confirm the reasons given in each case.

California City was laid out in the Mojave Desert in 1958 for 400,000 people who never came, and it is still a grid of paved streets with few houses, the third-largest city in California by area. Its private prison, built in 1999, reopened in late August 2025 as an immigration detention center with room for 2,560 people. By January 2026 it held more than 1,400 and was the largest in the state. Visitors there have described the three sliding gates, the invisible hand stamp, and visits through glass by wall telephone. Haji Wahid, Yusuf and Ustad Latif are invented. The lines the Ustad recites are from the second chapter of the Bustan of Sa'di of Shiraz (1257), in G. S. Davie's verse translation of 1882, where an angel says them to a pilgrim who is proud of praying at every step of the road to Mecca.
