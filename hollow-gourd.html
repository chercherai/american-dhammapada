Walking the American WayCommentary 19.2 · The Group of Six

Hollow Gourd

Hollow gourd mek mos’ noise.
— Jamaica Negro Proverbs and Sayings (1910), no. 364

A so mi hear it, by the schoolyard on Fox Street.

I. Sweepings

Westchester Avenue, the Bronx · a Saturday in October 1979

The Saturday after a jam, Winston Brissett and Hector Colón always ate breakfast at the bakery under the el on Westchester Avenue, because Winston’s aunt worked the counter and gave them the patties that had split in the oven for a dime apiece. They were fourteen and fifteen. They had been on the mic the night before for the first time in front of more than a hundred people, at the end of the night, when most of the older heads had gone home. Little Winnie and Kid Hex. They had worked out their routine on the roof of Hector’s building all summer, trading lines back and forth over a beat Hector kept on a garbage can lid, and the night before it had gone the way it went on the roof, which almost never happened.

They sat at the counter with coco bread and a beef patty each and went over it line by line, the way you go over a ball game.

Six young men came in out of the rain without shutting the door.

Everybody under the el knew who they were. They called themselves the Mighty Six, from Kelly Street, and they went from jam to jam all over the Bronx, the schoolyards and the parks and the P.A.L. gyms, and wherever they went they took the mic. They did not ask for it. They walked up to the table and took it out of whatever hand had it, and talked into it until the DJ gave up or the police came. They were not bad on the mic. Raheem, who did most of the talking, had a voice like a tenor sax and never once ran out of words. That was the trouble. He never ran out.

All that fall the radio had been playing a record by three men from Jersey nobody had ever seen at a jam, fourteen minutes of rhymes some of which, people said, had come out of a Bronx MC’s own notebook. The Mighty Six had taken it hard. They said there were only six real MCs left in the Bronx and they were all of them standing right here.

Raheem saw Winston at the counter.

Little Winnie, he said. Kid Hex. I heard about you. I heard you were on Lindo’s set last night talking like you had something to say.

Winston’s aunt said, Raheem, you buying something?

We the only ones nice, Raheem said to the room. Everybody else is just talking. These little Jamaican boys are taking up the air.

The counterman had been sweeping behind the display case and had the morning’s sweepings in a dustpan, flour and crumbs and cigarette ends and the wet grit that came in on everybody’s shoes. One of the Six took it out of his hand the way you would take a plate. Two others caught Winston and Hector from behind by the collars and held them on their stools. Raheem took the dustpan and poured it slowly over Winston’s head, and then what was left over Hector’s, and set it on the counter and wiped his fingers on a napkin.

Go home and practice, he said, for about five years.

Somebody laughed. The Six went out into the rain. The counterman picked up the dustpan and looked at it. Winston’s aunt came around the counter with a dish towel and started on Winston’s hair, hard, saying things under her breath in the voice she used at home, and he let her, and did not look at Hector, and Hector sat very still with flour in his eyelashes and his patty in his hand.

II. The Boiler Room

Fox Street · that afternoon

Mr. Fitzroy Lindo was the super of a six-story building on Fox Street, one of the few on that block that still had all its windows. He had come up from Kingston in 1967. In Kingston, people said, he had run a sound in Jones Town that played against the big ones and was never once outplayed, and you could still hear a little of it in the way he lifted a needle. Here he fixed boilers and changed fuses and kept the hallways swept, and on Saturday nights in good weather his son Delroy carried the speaker boxes up the basement steps and out to the schoolyard, and Mr. Lindo plugged his amplifier into the bottom of a lamppost and played.

He did not talk on the mic. Delroy did, a little, and the boys who came around, and lately half the young men in Longwood who could put two lines together. Mr. Lindo stood behind the turntables with a felt hat on and chose records. He chose them the way a man picks fruit, slowly, turning each one in his hands, and then he dropped the needle exactly where the drums came in, and the whole schoolyard went up at once like birds.

The speaker boxes lived in the boiler room. That was where the boys went.

They had washed their hair at Hector’s. It did not matter. They came down the basement steps with their hands in their pockets and stood by the boxes and told it to Delroy, who was on the floor rewiring a plug, and Delroy got up with the plug in his hand and went and told his father.

Mr. Lindo was at the bench by the boiler with the tone arm of one of the turntables in a little vise, cleaning the stylus with a brush made of a few hairs. He listened to Delroy. He listened to Winston, who said more than he meant to, and to Hector, who said one sentence in Spanish and stopped. He put the brush down.

On a nail over the bench hung a calabash gourd, dried brown, with a hole cut in the top. His mother had carried water in it, in St. Elizabeth, before any of them were born. He reached up and knocked on it with one knuckle, twice, and it gave back a sound much bigger than itself, hollow, a sound that filled up the boiler room and kept going.

He let it stop.

Hear that? he said. Nothing in it.

Then he said the thing his mother used to say, the way she said it, and after it another one, and went back to the stylus.

Hollow gourd mek mos’ noise.

Patient man ride jackass.

— Izett Anderson and Frank Cundall, Jamaica Negro Proverbs and Sayings (1910)

Delroy laughed. Winston did not get it, or not all of it. He got the first part. A man who talks the most is not the one with the most in him. He did not know what the jackass was for. He stood by the boxes with his hands in his pockets and watched Mr. Lindo clean the needle, and the needle was so small, and the hands so careful with it, that after a while he forgot about his hair.

III. The Lamppost

The schoolyard on Fox Street · the next Saturday night

It was the last warm night of the year and three hundred people came. Delroy ran the cable across the sidewalk to the lamppost and taped it down, and the boxes stood at the end of the schoolyard against the fence, two bass bins as tall as Winston, and the amplifier warmed up with its little red light, and Mr. Lindo laid out his crates on a folding table under the streetlamp, where the light was orange and fell on everything the same.

Winston and Hector were on the mic early, when the crowd was still thin. Then the older MCs from Longwood had it, and then Delroy, and the yard filled up and the noise of it rose off the asphalt like heat.

The Mighty Six came in through the gate on the Fox Street side a little after eleven, in a line, and the crowd opened in front of them because it knew them. Raheem walked straight to the table and took the mic out of the hand of the boy who had it.

He talked. He was good. He talked about himself, and the Six, and Kelly Street, and about the Jersey record and the men who made it, and about every fool holding a mic from Mott Haven to Fordham Road, and about little Jamaican boys who ought to be home in bed, and his voice went up and down the yard over the break like a man running his hand along a fence. The crowd hollered for him. Then it hollered less. He kept going. He did not let the record breathe. Every place in the music where something might have happened, Raheem was there first, talking.

Mr. Lindo let him. He stood behind the turntables in his hat and kept the break going, catching it on one record as it ran out on the other, back and forth, the same eight bars of drums over and over, so that Raheem would never be without something under him.

That was when two of the Six came around behind the table with the schoolyard trash can.

It was a wire can chained to the fence, and they had unhooked it, and it was full of what a schoolyard is full of on a Saturday night, paper cups and chicken bones and bottle caps and the sweepings of a week, and they lifted it up over Mr. Lindo’s head and turned it over.

It came down on his hat and his shoulders and onto the folding table and into the open crates. A cup rolled across a record and off. The yard made a sound, all of it at once, and then went quiet in a ring around the table. Delroy was coming over the cable with his face changed. Winston could not move at all.

Mr. Lindo did not stop the record.

He did not look up, or around, or at the two of them standing behind him with the empty can. The break was running out on the left turntable and he caught it on the right, clean, with grit on the backs of his hands, and the drums came in again on the one exactly where they had come in a thousand times, and the yard heard them. He put out one hand flat toward Delroy without turning, and Delroy stopped where he was.

Then he reached over and turned the mic down.

He left it on. Raheem was still talking, and you could hear him, a little, if you were close, a small voice somewhere under the drums. Mr. Lindo let the break go round once more by itself, and then he lifted the needle off the left record and laid it into the right one at a different place, and what came out of the bins was a horn line nobody in that yard had heard before, long and slow, over a bass that you felt in your knees.

The yard went up. It went up like birds. Three hundred people danced on the asphalt in the orange light, and nobody was listening to Raheem any longer. Nobody had stopped him. He had nothing in him the music did not already have. He stood with the mic in his hand. After a while he put it down on the table, carefully, among the bottle caps.

Mr. Lindo picked a chicken bone off the record on the right turntable without stopping it and dropped it on the ground. He still had not looked at any of them. He did not look angry. He did not look like anything. He looked like a man choosing fruit.

When the record ran out he leaned to Raheem, and Winston, who was close enough by then, heard him. He said it the way you would tell somebody the time.

You have a good voice. Come Tuesday. Help carry the boxes.

IV. Tuesday

Fox Street · Tuesday evening

Raheem did not come Tuesday. He never came. The Mighty Six went on taking mics at other jams for a while, and then, the way crews do, there were four of them, and then nobody could say.

But a boy named Tito, who was the youngest of them and had held Hector by the collar at the bakery, came down the basement steps on Fox Street on Tuesday evening with his hands in his pockets. He stood by the boxes and did not say anything, and Mr. Lindo did not say anything, and after a while Delroy handed him the end of a cable to coil.

The felt hat was on a nail over the bench, next to the gourd. Mr. Lindo had not brushed it. There was still a little grit in the crease of the crown.

Winston and Hector worked on their routine all that winter on Hector’s roof, in coats, with the garbage can lid. It got shorter. They started leaving holes in it, places where neither of them said anything and Hector just hit the lid, once, and let it ring, and you could hear the el go by on Westchester Avenue, and then the next line came. In the spring they tried it at the schoolyard. When they got to the first hole and stopped, the whole yard leaned in toward them, waiting, and Winston stood there in the orange light with his mouth shut and his heart going, and let it ring.

Background

The verse

A man is not learned because he talks much; he who is patient, free from hatred and fear, he is called learned.Dhammapada 258, tr. F. Max Müller (1881)

The Group of Six (Chabbaggiyā) are the troublemaking monks of the Buddhist monastic code, blamed for the misconduct behind many of its rules. In this short commentary story they go from monastery to monastery and village to village making trouble in the refectories, boasting that they alone are intelligent and wise. One morning, after breakfast in a village, some young monks and novices come back and report that the Six grabbed them from behind and poured sweepings over their heads. The monks tell the Buddha, who says that he does not call a man wise because he talks much, only one who is patient, free from hatred and free from fear. Read the full commentary story, or about the Vinaya, the monastic code so many of whose rules began with something the Six had done.

The Bronx, 1979

Hip hop began in the West and South Bronx in the early 1970s, at parties and outdoor jams where DJs, among them the Jamaican-born DJ Kool Herc, brought the island’s sound system culture north and extended the drum breaks of funk records on two turntables. Outdoor jams often ran on power drawn from the base of a lamppost. MCs, at first the DJ’s helpers, began rhyming over the breaks, and crews toured the borough’s schoolyards, parks and community centers. In September 1979 Sugar Hill Records released the Sugarhill Gang’s “Rapper’s Delight”, often called the first commercial rap single, recorded by a group from New Jersey; part of its lyrics had been borrowed from the notebook of the Bronx MC Grandmaster Caz.

Mr. Lindo’s sayings come from Jamaica Negro Proverbs and Sayings, collected by Izett Anderson and Frank Cundall and published by the Institute of Jamaica in 1910. The book glosses “Hollow gourd mek mos’ noise” as the old saw about empty vessels, and “Patient man ride jackass” as meaning that a patient man will put up with a great deal, or will more slowly attain his end. The Longwood neighborhood around Fox and Kelly Streets was among the hardest hit by the fires and abandonment that emptied much of the South Bronx in the 1970s.
