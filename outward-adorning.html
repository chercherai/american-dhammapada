Outward Adorning

Whose adorning let it not be that outward adorning of plaiting the hair, and of wearing of gold, or of putting on of apparel…
— 1 Peter 3:3

The way I heard it told, on Sycamore Fork.

I. Miss Guyandotte Valley

Logan County, West Virginia · 1998–2006

Crystal Hollins was the prettiest girl anybody in Logan County could remember, and in Logan County people remember. She was Miss Guyandotte Valley at eighteen, in a white gown her mother drove to Charleston to buy, and the picture of her in the sash with her hands folded and her hair down her back ran on the front of the Logan paper and stayed taped up in the window of the drive-in at Chapmanville until the sun bleached it blue. A man from a modeling agency in Columbus saw that picture and drove all the way down to give her his card, and she kept it in her wallet for nine years and never called him. Men who had known her since she was in diapers still turned around in the Walmart. Old women at the post office told her mother, That girl could have been on television, and her mother said, Well, she could have.

She was the baby of the family. Darrell was the oldest, fifteen years ahead of her, and Darrell had done what the Hollinses had always meant somebody to do. He had started with one dozer and a dump truck and a man who knew powder, and by 2002 Hollins Contracting had sixty men and a shop full of equipment and did the blasting and the hauling for the strip jobs from Blair to the Kentucky line. When a coal company wanted the top taken off a mountain, they called Darrell. People said he was the king of Sycamore Fork. He had a house on the ridge with a three-car garage, and a son, Caleb, who was going to take it over.

In the spring of 2003 Darrell Hollins went to a revival at the little Holiness church at the mouth of Sycamore Fork because his wife asked him to, and went up to the altar on the second night, and did not come back down the same man. He sold the dozers and the drills and the trucks. He sold the house on the ridge. He paid off every man who worked for him with a year's wages and gave the rest away, so much of it and so fast that the bank in Logan sent a man out to the house to ask if he was all right. Then he moved into the parsonage behind the church, four rooms with a coal stove, and started to preach.

Inside of three years, people were driving from Mingo and Boone and Pike County, Kentucky, to hear him. They parked up and down the creek road on Sunday nights.

The rest of the Hollinses went after him one at a time. Caleb, at sixteen, was preaching beside him. Their mother, Wanda, who had worn slacks to the grocery store for forty years, let her hair grow and put on long skirts and joined the church at sixty-four. And Crystal's own husband, Travis Mullins, who had been on OxyContin since a piece of roof fell on him in the mine in 2001 and had sold her mother's wedding ring to get it, went down to Darrell's altar one night in 2005 and came up off the floor clean, and stayed clean, and took his guitar and went out on the road with Darrell to the revivals.

So by the summer of 2006 Crystal was the only one left. She had a tanning-and-nail salon in Logan called Crystal Clear, and a red Mustang, and a house on Sycamore Fork with nobody in it at night but her. She sat in the house and thought about it. Her brother had given up the strip jobs. His boy had given up the business. Her mother had given up her slacks. Her husband had given up everything there was to give up, and he was happy. Every one of them was over at that church.

Well, she said, to the kitchen. Then I'm going too.

It was not the Holy Ghost that took her there. She said so herself, years later. It was that she could not stand to be the only one on the outside of the window.

II. The Sisters

Sycamore Fork · 2006–2007

She sold the salon. She put her jeans and her shorts in a garbage bag and took them to the Goodwill in Logan, and bought long denim skirts and long-sleeved blouses. She washed off her makeup and did not put it back on. She stopped cutting her hair, which had never been cut much anyway, and learned from her mother how to twist it up and pin it, the way the Holiness women wear it, high on the back of the head. She took off her rings and the little gold cross she had worn since she was confirmed at the Methodist church in Chapmanville when she was twelve. The church called that outward holiness, and she kept every bit of it.

What she did not give up was the mirror. Every morning she stood in front of the bathroom glass on Sycamore Fork to pin up her hair, and every morning she looked a while longer than it took. Without the makeup her face was plainer, she could see that, and she could also see that it hardly mattered. Her skin was still what it was. Her eyes were still what they were. She would turn her head to one side and the other and think, with a little guilty pleasure she never told anybody about, that they could take away the lipstick and the gold and the jeans and the salon, and there was still this, and nobody could take this. Some mornings she caught herself smiling at it. Then she would pin the last pin and go to prayer meeting.

The sisters at church took her in like a lost calf. They called her Sister Crystal. They said, Don't she look like an angel, because even with her face scrubbed and her hair pinned up she did, and they could not help it any more than she could.

But she would not sit under Darrell's preaching.

She went on Wednesday nights when Caleb preached. She went to the sisters' prayer meetings in people's kitchens. She went on the Sundays Darrell was off holding revivals in Kentucky. When she heard his truck in the church lot she found something to do in the fellowship hall. Because she had heard what he preached. Everybody had. He had a sermon he was known for, out of First Peter, the third chapter, on outward adorning, on the plaiting of the hair and the wearing of gold and the putting on of apparel, and how the flesh is grass and the body goes to the worms, and people said that when he preached it women went home and threw their curling irons in the creek. Crystal thought, He'll find fault with me. If he gets a look at me up there, he'll find fault even with me. So for a year her brother never once saw her face in his congregation.

After service on Sunday nights the sisters stood around in the gravel lot by their cars in the dark, under the one light on the pole, and talked about him. Crystal stood with them, because she had no way not to.

Sister Opal said she had never in her life seen a man's face shine like that when he was preaching. Like a light was on behind it.

Sister Rhonda said it was the voice. She said she would drive to Kentucky just to hear him read the begats.

Sister Faye said it was what he gave up. A man with sixty men under him and a three-car garage, and now he drives a 1991 Ford with no tailgate and owns one suit, and wears it to funerals and weddings and the pulpit both.

Old Sister Bernice, who was eighty-four and had been in the Holiness since 1939 and had heard every preacher in the coalfields, said it was none of that. She said it was that there was nothing crooked in him anywhere. She said, I have looked for it, and they laughed, and she did not.

Crystal listened to them and thought: they go on about him like he was Jesus. He's my brother. I know what he looks like with the flu. How much could he say about me in one night? And one night in July, in the lot, she heard herself say, I believe I'll go to the revival.

The sisters were so glad they hugged her. Sister Opal said she had prayed for it for a year. Sister Rhonda said, Now he'll really preach.

Crystal thought: I'll sit in the back behind the sisters. I'll see him and hear him, and he won't ever see me, and I'll leave before the altar call.

Darrell heard about it from his mother before the week was out. He sat at the kitchen table in the parsonage with his coffee and thought about his sister for a long time. He thought about the picture in the drive-in window. He thought that you take out a splinter with a needle, and that some things you can only get out with the thing that put them in.

III. The Girl in Red

Little Sycamore Holiness Church · August 2007

The marquee sign out by the creek road said REVIVAL AUG 12–19 BRO DARRELL HOLLINS ALL WELCOME, in red letters Caleb had put up on a stepladder. The rose of Sharon bushes along the church steps were in bloom, the pink kind with the red eye, where every flower opens in the morning and is shriveled on the ground by the next one. It was the third night of the revival and the church was full to the doors, with folding chairs down the aisle and men standing along the back wall with their hats in their hands. The windows were open and the box fans were going and it did not help. Every few minutes the windows shook in their frames, because the company that had bought Darrell's old contracts was still blasting on the strip job across the hollow, on what had been Cazy Mountain, and they blasted at four and again at seven.

Crystal came in late with the sisters and sat down in the next-to-last pew, right behind Sister Opal and Sister Rhonda, whose hair was pinned up so high that between the two of them she could hardly see the pulpit. That was why she had picked them.

Caleb led the singing. Travis played guitar. Then Darrell got up, and the church went quiet, and Crystal leaned a little to the side to look past Sister Rhonda's hair, and saw her brother for the first time in a year, standing behind the pulpit in his one suit with his Bible open, and his face did shine, she could not have said how. She thought: Opal's right. She thought: I'd forgotten.

Then she saw the girl.

She was standing just to the right of the pulpit, at the end of the altar rail, where nobody stands. She was about sixteen. She had on a long red gown that shimmered when she moved, with a rhinestone sash across it and a little rhinestone crown in her hair, and her hair was down, all the way down, dark and shining, past her waist. She was fanning herself with one of the cardboard church fans from the pew racks, slowly, back and forth, and looking out at the congregation with a face like a full moon coming up over the ridge.

Nobody else saw her. Crystal knew that right away, the way you know things in dreams, from how nobody turned to look. Sister Opal did not see her. Sister Bernice, in the front row, did not see her. Only Crystal saw her. And Darrell, who never once looked at the girl, but stood beside her as if he knew exactly where she was.

Crystal could not stop looking. She looked at the girl's hair, and thought, oh, look at her hair. She looked at her forehead and her cheekbones and her bare arms and the way she held the fan, and the way the gown fell, and she thought of herself at that age in the white dress at the Guyandotte Valley pageant, which she had thought was the most beautiful she would ever be. And she understood that she had never been anything like this. Sitting there in her long skirt with her hair pinned up and no lipstick, she felt like a crow next to a swan. She wanted, more than she had wanted anything since she was a girl, to look like that.

IV. The Fan

The same night

Darrell began to preach. He did not preach out of First Peter. He did not preach about adorning at all. Crystal never could remember afterward what he preached about, because while he preached she was watching the girl, and the girl was changing.

It went slow at first. The girl was sixteen, and then she was twenty. That was all. But Crystal felt it go through her like cold water, and she thought, she's not the same. She's not as pretty as she was a minute ago.

Then the girl was holding a baby on her hip, and fanning the baby with the church fan, and her face had gone a little thick under the jaw and there were blue half-moons under her eyes. Then the baby was gone and she was forty, in a sweatshirt with a Dollar General name tag pinned to it, with a home permanent and reading glasses on a string around her neck, and she was rubbing the small of her back with one hand while she fanned with the other. Crystal sat very still in the pew. She kept thinking, now that's gone. Now that's gone too.

Then the girl was old. Her hair was white and thin and her teeth were gone and her mouth had fallen in, and she was bent over nearly double, the way the old women on the creek get bent from a life of stooping, so bent she had to lean on a cane with both hands and could hardly lift her face to see the congregation. She trembled all over. The rhinestone crown hung crooked in what was left of her hair. She still had the fan, hooked over one finger.

Crystal looked at her and felt sick.

Then the sickness took the old woman. She dropped the cane and the fan both and cried out, and went down on the floor in front of the altar rail, and rolled back and forth on the carpet, and fouled herself, and lay in it. Up in the pulpit Darrell went on preaching. The box fans went on turning. Nobody in the church but Crystal saw any of it, and she sat with both hands over her mouth.

V. Bones

The same night

Then the old woman on the floor was dead.

Crystal watched it happen, and then watched what came after, all of it, in the time it takes to preach a sermon. The body swelled up in the red gown until the rhinestone sash cut into it. The face went dark. What was inside started coming out of every opening there is, and there were worms in it. And then the church was not a church any more but a bank by the side of the creek road, and the buzzards came down out of the sky in long circles and landed, and the crows came, and the dogs from the trailers came up out of the hollow and pulled at it, and pulled the red gown apart, and the bones came through.

Crystal looked at it, and something in her went down to the bottom and stopped.

She thought: that was her, and it happened right here. She got old right here, and got sick right here, and died right here. And it is coming to me. It is coming to this body I am sitting in. It is not coming someday to somebody. It is coming to this.

Then she saw it was coming to everything. To the sisters in front of her, and their high pinned hair. To her mother. To Travis with his guitar. To every face in the church, and every face in the Walmart, and the picture in the drive-in window. It was like seeing every house on the creek on fire at once, in the night, and knowing there was not one of them you could go back into. She wanted out. Not out of the church. Out of all of it.

Up in the pulpit Darrell stopped preaching in the middle of a sentence.

He had seen, in her face, what she had seen. He had seen too that it had taken her down to the bottom and that she did not know how to stand up on it by herself. So he came out from behind the pulpit and down the two steps, and walked up the aisle between the folding chairs, past the sisters, all the way back to the next-to-last pew, and stood at the end of it. The whole church turned around in their seats to see who he was looking at. It was the first time in a year he had looked his sister in the face.

He did not say anything about her face. He opened his Bible to where his thumb already was and read it out to her, quietly, as if there were nobody else in the building.

As for man, his days are as grass: as a flower of the field, so he flourisheth. For the wind passeth over it, and it is gone; and the place thereof shall know it no more.

— Psalm 103:15–16

Crystal looked up at him. She had been crying and did not know it. She felt something under her feet that had not been there before, something like a floor.

Darrell saw that too. He sat down beside her on the end of the pew, an old man's way of sitting, with his hand on his knee, and said it plain, the way he would have said it to her at the supper table when she was ten.

Sissy, there's nothing in it. There's not a thing in it to hold onto. It's a frame of bones they've plastered over, and painted, and what's inside is getting old, and dying, and proud of itself, and putting on a face. That's all it is. That's all mine is too.

Then he turned a few pages and read the hardest thing in the Book, the thing the Lord said to the preachers of His own day, and Crystal heard that he was reading it to himself as much as to her.

Woe unto you, scribes and Pharisees, hypocrites! for ye are like unto whited sepulchres, which indeed appear beautiful outward, but are within full of dead men’s bones, and of all uncleanness.

— Matthew 23:27

The windows shook. It was seven o'clock, and across the hollow they were blasting on Cazy Mountain.

Crystal Hollins sat in the next-to-last pew with her brother beside her and her hands in her lap, and the last of it went out of her, all of it, the white dress and the sash and the picture in the window and the crow and the swan, like the dust going out of the air after the blast. The sisters said afterward that she never went to the altar that night. She did not need to. She got up off that pew already standing on the other side of something.

VI. The Strip Job

Sycamore Fork · afterward

Sister Opal said, on the way out to the cars, that she had never seen anything like it, and Sister Rhonda said she had told them, didn't she tell them, now he'd really preach. Sister Faye said that was the first time anybody ever saw Darrell Hollins walk out of his own pulpit in the middle of a sermon. Sister Bernice did not say anything. She took Crystal's face in both her old hands in the gravel lot, under the one light on the pole, and looked at it a long time, and let it go.

Crystal never told the sisters what she had seen by the altar rail. She told Darrell once, years later, sitting on his porch, and he nodded and said he knew, and they did not talk about it again.

She did not make much of herself after that, in the church or out of it. She worked at the clinic in Logan, at the front desk, and then in the back with the old people, washing them and turning them in their beds, which she was good at and did not mind. She was the one the nurses sent for when somebody was dying and the family could not stand to be in the room. Women in the church who were scared of getting old came to her, and she sat with them at their kitchen tables and did not tell them it would be all right. She told them what it was. They said afterward it was the strangest thing, how that helped.

Her hair went grey early, in her thirties, all at once, and she let it. Travis came home off the road for good the next year and they lived quiet on Sycamore Fork, and when he said one night across the supper table that she wasn't as pretty as she used to be, he said it wondering, the way you'd say the creek was down, and she laughed until she had to wipe her eyes, because it was true and because she did not mind.

That fall she drove her mother up the ridge road to the overlook on a Sunday afternoon, the one where you used to be able to see Cazy Mountain from the porch of Darrell's old house. The company had finished with the top of it. Where the trees had been there was a long flat bench of grey rock, terraced and raw, with the seams showing through like the bones of something, and below it the valley fill where the hollow used to be, and a water truck going back and forth on it laying the dust. Her mother said, Lord, look what they did.

Crystal looked. Then she said, It was always under there, Mama, and put the car in reverse, and drove her mother home down the creek road in the last of the light.

Background

The verse

After a stronghold has been made of the bones, it is covered with flesh and blood, and there dwell in it old age and death, pride and deceit.Dhammapada 150, tr. F. Max Müller (1881)

In the commentary, Rūpanandā, "Joyous Form," the Buddha's own half-sister and the most beautiful woman in the land, becomes a nun because everyone she loves has already left home to follow her brother: his son, her betrothed husband, her mother. She does it for love of them, not out of faith, and because she has heard that the Buddha calls the body impermanent and unsatisfying, she keeps out of his sight, sure he will find fault with her beauty. When the nuns finally bring her to hear him, she hides among them. The Buddha conjures a girl of sixteen in crimson, more beautiful than Rūpanandā, fanning herself beside him. As Rūpanandā watches, the girl ages, bears a child, grows old and bent, falls sick, dies, and is torn apart by crows and dogs. Seeing that the same is coming to her own body, Rūpanandā is shaken loose; the Buddha steadies her with verses, then tells her the body is a city of bones, and she reaches awakening. Logan County, 2007

Logan County sits in the southern West Virginia coalfields along the Guyandotte River; it saw the Battle of Blair Mountain in 1921 and the Buffalo Creek flood in 1972. By the 2000s much of the coal there came from mountaintop removal, in which explosives take off as much as 400 feet of a summit to reach the seams beneath and the leftover rock is pushed into the hollows below as "valley fills." The method supplied about 30 percent of West Virginia's coal in 2006 and needed far fewer workers than underground mining. The same years brought the prescription-painkiller epidemic to the region; in May 2007 Purdue Pharma pleaded guilty to misleading the public about the addiction risk of OxyContin and agreed to pay $600 million.

Holiness and Pentecostal churches have deep roots in the Appalachian coalfields. Many of the more conservative ones keep what they call outward holiness: women do not cut their hair, wear dresses or skirts rather than trousers, and give up jewelry and makeup, citing among other passages 1 Peter 3, the verses on "outward adorning" that Crystal feared her brother would preach at her.
