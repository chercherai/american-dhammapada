Walking the American WayCommentary 16.9 · Nandiya

Everlasting Cottages

But when he was yet a great way off, his father saw him, and had compassion, and ran, and fell on his neck, and kissed him.
— Luke 15:20

The way it was told, in Walnut Creek Township.

I. Church at Our House

Walnut Creek Township, Holmes County, Ohio · spring 1993

Noah Troyer was the kind of young man the old women pointed out to their daughters. He joined church at nineteen without being told twice. He sang the slow hymns from the Ausbund in a voice that held the long notes as steady as a level. When church was at somebody else's house he went over the night before to help unload the bench wagon, and when it was at his father's he scrubbed the shop floor himself so the men's horses would have a clean place to stand in the rain.

His mother was Fannie Troyer, and across the township road lived her cousin's family, the Hershbergers, and their daughter Rebecca. Both mothers had settled long ago, over a quilt frame, that Noah and Rebecca would make a pair. Noah did not want her.

Rebecca was twenty-two and had not joined church. She worked at a tourist restaurant in Berlin, carrying plates of broasted chicken to people from Cleveland, and she kept her tips in a coffee can and did not tell her father how much was in it. When the deacon came by with the alms list she was always upstairs. When a peddler or a man walking to Florida knocked at the Hershbergers' back door, it was never Rebecca who gave him bread. She was quick and good-looking and she laughed at the wrong people, and Noah said to his mother that he could not marry a girl who had never once given anything away.

Fannie went across the road.

Church is at our place in three weeks, she told Rebecca. Come help me get ready for it. Scrub the walls and the windows. Help the boys set up the benches. When the ministers come in, take their hats and hang them on the nails. Keep their water glasses full, and when they've eaten, wash up the cups and the dishes and put them back in the bench wagon the way they came. If you do it, you'll win him.

Rebecca came. She came every evening for a week. She stood on a chair and washed the kitchen ceiling with ammonia water. She carried benches with the Troyer boys and set them in rows in the shop, and laid the bench extenders across them for the noon tables. On the Sunday she stood at the door in a white cape and took the black hats of the bishop and the two ministers and the deacon as they came in out of the rain and hung each one on its nail. She kept their glasses full through the long first sermon and the second. At noon she carried the peanut butter spread and the red beets and the bread down the tables. In the afternoon she washed two hundred cups.

Fannie watched all of it. That night she said to Noah, She takes correction now.

Rebecca was baptized in the fall. They were married on a Thursday in December, in the Hershbergers' barn, and the cooks served roasht and mashed potatoes to three hundred people. That night, when the last of the cousins had gone home, Noah said to his wife, plainly, the way he said everything: If you'll take care of the church and my mam and dat, you can live in that house as long as you like. Be careful with it.

All right, said Rebecca.

II. The Pallet Shop

State Route 515 · 1994–2015

She kept the promise. In a few years nobody remembered that she had once been the girl upstairs when the deacon came. She was the first one there when a barn burned and the first one home from a funeral to start the coffee. She bore two boys, Eli and Mose, and she nursed Noah's father through his last two years when his lungs went, and carried his mother's meals up the stairs until his mother died too. Then she was mistress of the Troyer place, and Noah was a rich man.

Old Eli Troyer had started with a sawmill. Noah built it into a pallet shop that ran two shifts, then a shop that made porch furniture for the tourist stores along Route 39, and then, because the Amish of Holmes County were building faster than they could find carpenters, a crew that put up pole barns from Wooster to Coshocton. He did not have a telephone in the house, but there was one in a shanty at the end of the lane that rang all day.

He gave. He gave to the alms fund until the deacon told him to stop because people would talk. And he put a long table in the break room at the shop, with an urn of coffee and a slow cooker, and he told anyone who asked that it was for the drivers.

The drivers were the English people, and a few who were not quite English, who drove the Amish wherever a buggy could not go: to the hospital in Akron, to a funeral in Indiana, to the dentist, to a wedding in Wisconsin. They charged by the mile and they waited. They waited in parking lots and hospital lobbies and at the ends of lanes, sometimes all day, and at Amish funerals, where there might be eighty vans in a hayfield, they ate their dinner in their seats, handed to them through the windows. Noah's table was the one place in the county where a driver could come in, sit down with a plate and be fed like company. Truckers who had lost their way found it too, and a man walking from Maine to Key West with a dog, and once a busload of Chinese tourists who were looking for the cheese house. Rebecca kept the slow cooker filled, and on Saturdays she baked for it.

III. The Driver

Interstate 75, southbound · January 2016

Sadie Mast had grown up Amish on Township Road 606 and left at eighteen, the year before she would have joined church, and because she had never been baptized she had never been put out. She could still go to her mother's for Sunday dinner. She still talked to her passengers in Deitsch, which made the old ones easy with her and the young ones careful. She had been driving for twenty-two years in a white fifteen-passenger Ford with a hundred and ninety thousand miles on it, and she had driven most of Walnut Creek Township to most of its funerals. She was fifty-four. She did not talk much. She listened to the radio low, gospel or the farm report, and switched it off when the old people prayed.

In January of 2016 Noah hired her to drive him and Rebecca down to Pinecraft for two weeks, because his back had gone out and he could not sit for twenty hours on the bus. Somewhere in Georgia, after dark, with Rebecca asleep on the back bench, he moved up to the seat behind Sadie and they talked.

He asked her about Pinecraft, because she had been driving people down there for years. She told him what she had seen. The little white cottages on Kauffman and Graber and Yoder streets, rented a year ahead. The shuffleboard courts in the park where the old men played three games and went to the back of the list. The Pioneer Trails bus coming in at the Tourist Church across from the post office, and the crowd that came out to meet it.

Who goes down? Noah asked.

Whoever can pay for it, Sadie said. Then, after a mile: Abe Weaver never has.

Abe Weaver was the bishop of Noah's district. He was eighty-one. The lot had fallen on him at thirty-four, when he was a young dairyman with six children, and for forty-seven years he had preached and married and buried and gone to sit with the dying, every week, for nothing, because Amish ministers are paid nothing, and he had milked thirty cows twice a day besides. He had never seen the ocean.

There's a lot of them like that, Sadie said. The ministers. They say yes to God at the lot and that's it, that's their life. They spend it on everybody else. They don't get a winter. She was quiet a while. The ones who give the most get the least of anything you can hold in your hand. You'd think it would work out the other way.

That was all she said. Noah sat behind her watching the white lines come up out of the dark, and by the Florida line he had decided.

IV. Four Rooms

Pinecraft, Sarasota, Florida · January 2017

He bought a lot on Schrock Street with a tired cinderblock bungalow on it and had his own crew drive down in two vans and take it to the slab. On the slab they built a cottage with four bedrooms opening off a long screened porch, so four couples could have their own doors, and a kitchen big enough for all of them, and a shed for the tricycles. He had beds made in his own shop, oak, with rope springs for the old ones who said modern springs hurt their backs. He had rockers made for the porch. Then he went to the bishop's council and gave the whole thing to the ministers of the Walnut Creek districts and their wives, to be used in turn, every winter, for as long as there were ministers.

The Amish do not dedicate buildings. There was no ceremony. Sadie drove the first four couples down herself in January, Abe Weaver among them, in his Sunday coat, and she was there when Noah walked them through it.

The old bishop stood on the porch and looked at the four doors for a long time and could not say anything. His wife sat down in a rocker and wept and laughed at herself for weeping.

Noah wanted to show them the water. The well man had put in a new line from the street that week, and an outside spigot by the porch steps for washing sand off feet. Noah turned the handle and the pipe coughed and spat air and then ran clear. He looked around for something to catch it in to show them it was clean, and there was nothing, and Sadie was standing closest.

Hold out your hand, he said.

She held out her right hand, cupped, the way you hold it at a pump. He ran the water into it until it spilled over her fingers onto the white sand.

Cold, Sadie said, surprised. It was eighty degrees.

V. What Mose Saw

Sarasota Memorial Hospital and the road north · February 2018

Mose Raber was one of the two ministers in Noah's district, a lean, quiet harness-maker of seventy-three who had been to Pinecraft once before in his life. He was in the cottage on Schrock Street with his wife the next winter when his heart stopped on the shuffleboard court.

A retired fireman from Indiana, a Mennonite, worked on him on the concrete until the ambulance came. At the hospital they said his heart had not beaten for six minutes. He came back on the second day and lay in the bed with the tubes in his arms, and when his wife had gone down to the cafeteria he told the night nurse, a Haitian woman who listened to him as if he were telling her the weather, where he had been.

He had been standing outside a house. It was white and so large he could not see where it ended in any direction, larger than all of Holmes County, and it went up until he could not see the top, and every board of it shone like the inside of a shell. There were flowers in the air in front of the door, pink and red, as if somebody had thrown a double handful of them up in the air and they had forgot to come down. At every window there were women in white, more than he could count, in white capes and white coverings like a wedding, and they came down to him on the steps.

Whose house is this? Mose asked them.

Noah Troyer's, they said. From Walnut Creek. He gave a house to the ministers, and this house came up the same minute. We're waiting for him. We are so lonesome for him we can hardly stand it. Tell him to come. Tell him leaving there for here is like breaking a cracked crock and getting a silver pitcher.

Then the women were gone and he was on the shuffleboard court with a stranger's mouth on his.

He could not ride the bus home after that, so his sons hired Sadie Mast to come down and drive him. On the second night, north of Valdosta, with his wife asleep behind him, Mose leaned forward in the dark and told Sadie the whole thing, because people tell drivers everything, and when he had told it he asked her what he had been wanting to ask someone.

Sadie. Is it true? That what a man does here is built for him there, while he's still living?

Sadie drove for a while. A truck went by with its windows lit like a kitchen.

Mose, she said. You were there. You saw it. Why are you asking me?

Then it's true.

You know the bus, she said. At Pinecraft.

He knew it. Mondays and Wednesdays and Fridays in the busy months, the Pioneer Trails bus from Millersburg came in at the Tourist Church lot after twenty-some hours on the road. A hundred people would be waiting there for it in the sun who had no one on it, just to see. And somebody always saw it first, some boy on a tricycle out at the corner of Bahia Vista, and came pedaling back hollering, It's here, it's here, and if he knew who was on it he hollered the names. The old men left their games on the shuffleboard court. The women came out of the cottages drying their hands. When the door opened and the first passenger came down the deep steps holding the rail, they all pressed in to get to him, to shake his hand, to take his suitcase. You came all this way. You made it. Let me. No, let me.

It's like that, Sadie said. Somebody who's been gone a long time comes up the road, and whoever sees him first runs to tell. And they all come out. It's like that when somebody who did good goes over. What he did is standing in the lot waiting for him. It's all pushing up to the bus. Me first. She was quiet a mile. My grossmammi used to say it out of the German Bible. The father who ran.

And she said it, first the way they had both heard it all their lives in church, and then in English.

Und er machte sich auf und kam zu seinem Vater. Da er aber noch ferne von dannen war, sah ihn sein Vater, und es jammerte ihn, lief und fiel ihm um seinen Hals und küßte ihn.

And he arose, and came to his father. But when he was yet a great way off, his father saw him, and had compassion, and ran, and fell on his neck, and kissed him.

— Luke 15:20

Mose sat back. After a while he said, And the other one. In the next chapter. About the friends.

Sadie said that too.

Und ich sage euch auch: Machet euch Freunde mit dem ungerechten Mammon, auf daß, wenn ihr nun darbet, sie euch aufnehmen in die ewigen Hütten.

And I say unto you, Make to yourselves friends of the mammon of unrighteousness; that, when ye fail, they may receive you into everlasting habitations.

— Luke 16:9

In die ewigen Hütten, said Mose. The everlasting cottages. He laughed for the first time since the shuffleboard court, very quietly so as not to wake his wife, and then he slept all the way to Chattanooga.

He told Noah, in the spring, sitting on a bench outside the harness shop. Noah listened, and thanked him, and told no one, and went on as before. He doubled the alms. He kept the drivers' table. He was fifty-three that year and stronger than any man on his crews.

VI. Rebecca

Walnut Creek Township · 2021–2023

Noah Troyer died in October of 2021 on the floor of his own pallet shop, at fifty-six, between one board and the next. They buried him on a Saturday in the Troyer graveyard on the hill behind the house, and there were a hundred and forty vans in the hayfield. The drivers ate standing up at his table in the break room, which was still set, because nobody had thought to tell Rebecca not to.

She told them after that. In November she unplugged the slow cooker and took it to the house and had the table carried out to the scrap pile behind the shop. When a driver came in out of the rain in December looking for coffee she told him the shop was a business. She told the deacon the alms would be what the Ordnung asked and not a dollar over. When it came the Troyers' turn for church that spring, she sent word to the bishop that it would have to be at somebody else's house, she was not running a hotel for preachers. And she went to the bishop's council with an English lawyer from Millersburg about getting back the cottage on Schrock Street, which was hers, she said, by rights, and her sons'.

Her sons were grown and married and lived down the road and did not know what to do with her. Nobody did. People said she had never really been converted. People said it was grief, that grief turns some people to water and some to stone. People said that Noah had held her the way a hand holds a bucket under a spring, and when the hand was gone the bucket was a bucket. All of it was probably true.

What happened next is told in Walnut Creek in more than one way.

Her daughter-in-law was staying with her that winter, and it is her telling that most people go by. Rebecca came to her bed in the night in February, shaking, and said that two men had knocked at the kitchen door. They were dressed for church, in black, but she did not know them, and they would not come in. They had taken her by the arms and walked her out the lane, and the lane had not ended at the road. It had gone on and on, and at the end of it they had shown her a white house so big she could not see the ends of it, with flowers hanging in the air before the door and women in white at all the windows, and she had known whose house it was. And then they had turned her around and shown her the other place, the one that had been got ready for her. She would not say what it was like. She said only that it was made out of everything she had kept.

In the morning she could not speak. The stroke had taken her right side and all her words.

She lived. She lay in a hospital bed in the front room of the Troyer house for the next two years and more, with her eyes open, looking at something in the corner of the ceiling that no one else could see. Whatever it was, she did not look away from it, and her face when she looked at it was the hardest thing anyone in that house had ever had to watch.

The church took care of her. That is the part no one tells differently. The women of the district sat with her in shifts, day and night, the way Amish women sit with the sick, and fed her with a spoon, and turned her, and sang to her out of the Ausbund. Mose Raber's wife came on Tuesdays. Abe Weaver came on Sundays until he died. The women who lived too far to come by buggy hired a driver, and most often the driver was Sadie Mast, who would not take money for it, and who sat in the van in the lane for the four hours with the radio off.

VII. The Bus

Pinecraft · February 2023

Sadie drove three of the couples down again that winter, as she had every winter since the first. The cottage on Schrock Street had held a hundred and four ministers and their wives by then. Mose Raber's wife kept the count in the back of the guest book. The beds had not squeaked once. The rockers had worn two pale arcs in the porch boards.

The fourth couple came by bus. In the fall the lot had fallen on Eli Troyer, Noah's older son, who was twenty-eight and ran the pallet shop and had two babies, and who had stood up white in the face in the Raber barn when the bishop opened the hymnbook in front of him and found the slip of paper. He had never been to Florida. His mother lay in the front room at home with her eyes on the corner of the ceiling, and his brother's wife was sitting with her, and he had not wanted to go. The bishop had told him to go.

On the Wednesday Sadie walked over to the Tourist Church lot at three o'clock. A boy on a red tricycle came tearing back from Bahia Vista standing up on the pedals. It's here! The old men laid their cues across the shuffleboard courts. A hundred people came out into the sun. The bus turned in across from the post office and sighed down onto its springs. The door opened. Eli Troyer came down the deep steps in his black hat, holding the rail with one hand and a baby with the other, and the boy on the tricycle hollered his name, and the whole lot pressed toward him at once, everyone wanting to be first to take his suitcase.

Sadie stood at the back of the crowd and let them.

When she walked back to the cottage the four old couples were all out on the porch in the rockers, and somebody had hung a tin cup on a nail over the spigot by the steps. Sadie filled it, and drank, and it was still cold.

Background

The verses

Kinsmen, friends, and lovers salute a man who has been long away, and returns safe from afar.Dhammapada 219, tr. F. Max Müller (1881)
In like manner his good works receive him who has done good, and has gone from this world to the other;—as kinsmen receive a friend on his return.Dhammapada 220, tr. F. Max Müller (1881)

In the commentary, Nandiya is a devout young man of Bārāṇasī whose parents want him to marry his cousin Revatī, who does not believe and never gives alms. His mother sets her a test: get the house ready for the monks, prepare their seats, take their bowls, strain their water and wash up after them. Revatī does it and they marry. She becomes devout, bears two sons and runs the household when his parents die. Nandiya, now rich, feeds the monks, the poor and travelers at his door, and gives the order a four-chambered hall at Isipatana. As he pours the water of donation into the Buddha's right hand, a jeweled mansion rises for him in the heaven of the Thirty-Three. The elder Moggallāna sees it on a visit there, and the heavenly women in it beg him to tell Nandiya to come. When Moggallāna asks whether such rewards come while a man still lives, the Buddha answers that he has seen it himself, and compares the welcome of the good in the next world to kinsmen running out to meet one who comes home from far away. The commentary to the Vimānavatthu adds that after Nandiya died, Revatī stopped the alms, abused the monks and was cast alive into hell. Read the full commentary story.

Holmes County and Pinecraft, 1993–2023

Holmes County and the counties around it hold one of the largest Amish settlements in the world, about 39,000 people in 2025. Old Order Amish worship every other Sunday in a member's home or barn, on benches carried from house to house in a bench wagon. The host family spends a week scrubbing walls and windows and serves a plain noon meal of bread, peanut butter spread, pickles and red beets (how a house gets ready for church). They sing from the Ausbund, first printed in 1564. Bishops, ministers and deacons are nominated by the members and then chosen by lot, by drawing hymnbooks until one turns up a hidden slip with a Bible verse. They have no formal training, they serve without pay, normally for life, and support themselves at their own work. Baptism is required before marriage, and only baptized members can be shunned, so a young person who leaves before joining church is not put out. In Holmes County most weddings fall on Thursdays, and they were once clustered in the winter months (Holmes County wedding customs). For trips a buggy cannot make, the Amish hire drivers by the mile, and those drivers spend much of their lives waiting in vans (a driver's account).

Pinecraft, a neighborhood of small cottages in Sarasota, took its name in 1925–26 from a tourist camp. It became the winter village of Amish and Mennonites from across North America, with the Mennonite Tourist Church on Bahia Vista Street, bought in 1946, as its landmark. The Pioneer Trails line of Millersburg, Ohio, runs buses down from Holmes County and the other northern settlements. In the busy months a crowd of a hundred or so gathers in the church lot across from the little post office to watch each bus come in, and the welcomes are over in about fifteen minutes (when the bus comes in). In the park the old men play shuffleboard by sign-up list, and adult tricycles take the place of buggies (Pioneer Trails to Pinecraft).
