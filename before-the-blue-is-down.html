Walking the American WayCommentary 25.8 · Pañcasatabhikkhu

Before the Blue Is Down

Lord judge, here I pluck a flower; if you can burn this flower and me, you have justly condemned me.
— Leonhard Keyser, 1527, in the Martyrs Mirror

The way we tell it, on the James River.

I. Five O’Clock

A colony on the James River, South Dakota · Tuesday, July 16, 1918

We are one people, so we tell it as one. The bell rang at five on its post by the kitchen, the way it had rung every morning since our grandfathers set the post in 1874, and we got up, a hundred and nineteen of us, and washed, and the men went out to the barns and the women to the kitchen and the children to wait for the children’s table.

From the yard you could see the flax. It was the forty acres on the bench above the river, and that morning it was blue all the way to the cottonwoods. It is not a blue you see anywhere else. It is the color of a thing that is going to be gone by afternoon. Every flower on that field opens at sunup and by the middle of the day lets go of its five petals, and the next morning there are new ones, and nobody has ever seen one flower last two days.

Vetter Paul did not come out to look at it. Vetter Paul was ninety-one and had been our preacher since before most of us were born, and since Easter he had lain in the small room at the end of the long house with the window toward the field, and his right hand did not work and his words came few. At Easter, when they carried him in to sit at the Lord’s Supper for what everyone thought was the last time, he gave us one thing to keep for the summer. He had been a boy in Russia and an old man in Dakota and he did not waste words.

Look at the flax, he said. Before the blue is down, set it down.

Set what down, nobody asked. We knew. In the spring of 1918 we had a great deal to set down.

From the colony book, in the steward’s hand, July 9: Taken by the men of the bond committee from town, to be sold for Liberty Bonds: steers, 64. Hogs, 30. Nothing paid. The men said we would get the sheep next.

We were Germans, they said in town, because we spoke German. We would not buy the bonds, because a bond is a gun with interest on it. We would not wear the uniform. In the spring they had taken our young men to the camps. Four boys from the Rockport colony down the river had been court-martialed in Washington State, and the word was that they were to go to the island prison in San Francisco Bay. Our own Samuel Kleinsasser was in the guardhouse at Camp Funston in Kansas for refusing to put on the shirt. His brother Andreas was nineteen and had not slept a night through since.

So that was the meditation of the summer. Before the blue is down. Every morning the field came up blue, and every morning a hundred and nineteen people went out under it carrying everything the town had done to us, and the hate came up with the sun as regular as the flax. And every afternoon the petals lay on the ground between the rows, and most days the hate was still standing.

II. Nine O’Clock

The flax bench · the same morning

We were weeding the flax by hand that week because the wild mustard had come up in it, and it was the young men and the girls in a line across the field, bent, pulling. When you weed flax in bloom the flowers brush your face. It is like walking through the sky in your work clothes.

Andreas was at the end of the line, next to the road. He pulled mustard as if every plant were a man on the bond committee. Basl Rebekka, who was the field boss for the girls and had a voice that could stop a runaway, watched him a while and then said, not loud, Before the blue is down, Andreas.

It is nine o’clock, he said. I have till noon.

Some laughed. It is a thing we do; we laugh at ourselves before anyone else can. But in the line, bent over, each of us knew what we were holding. For one it was the steers; she had raised a red one on a bottle and seen him go down the road on a rope. For one it was a word a storekeeper said to his wife at the counter. For one it was the boy in the guardhouse at Funston, whose one letter said he was well and was not allowed to say more, which was worse than anything it could have said. For one, and none of us said this aloud, it was the nice young woman in the bank who used to give the children peppermints and had stopped.

And for some of us it was not hate but its sister, the wanting. To have it back. To have it as it was before. To be let alone on our own ground with our own cattle. That holds as hard as hate does. Vetter Paul had said set it down and had not said which.

By ten the first petals were falling. You hear them if the wind is still: a very small sound, all over the field at once, like somebody a long way off turning the pages of a book.

III. Noon

The eating house · the same day

They came at dinner. Three automobiles and a stock truck from town, and a dozen men we knew by name, because we had sold them geese and bought their binder twine for forty years. Mr. Lindahl from the elevator was one. He did not get out of the car.

They had come for the sheep, as they had said they would. Our steward, Vetter Michel, went out wiping his mouth and stood in front of them and said what he had said the week before, that we could not stop them and would not try, and that what they did they would answer for and not we.

Somebody had painted the word on the side of the stock truck already, in yellow. One of the younger men threw a can of the same paint against the wall of the eating house, and it ran down the boards. Another of them, who was drunk at noon, which was the only thing about it that surprised us, spat on Vetter Michel’s beard. Vetter Michel stood there with it on him.

That was when Andreas came round the corner of the wash house with a hay fork.

We tell this part plainly because it happened. He did not raise it. He came round the corner with it in his two hands and stopped, and everything in the yard stopped, the men from town and our men and the women in the doorway with the soup ladles, and the sheep in the pen. Andreas was a big boy and the fork was a three-tine fork with a fresh handle, and he stood holding it, and we could all see on his face that he wanted to, as much as he had ever wanted anything.

Basl Rebekka said from the eating-house door: Andreas. It isn’t noon yet.

It was twelve minutes past. He knew it and she knew it. But he let the tines down onto the ground and leaned on the fork and stood there shaking while they loaded the sheep, a hundred and forty-one ewes and the two rams, and drove off with the paint still wet on the truck, and Mr. Lindahl in the back of the first car did not look at us.

July 16: Taken for Liberty Bonds: sheep, 143. Nothing paid. A can of yellow paint left, three-quarters used.

Nobody ate the rest of dinner. Vetter Michel washed his beard at the pump. The women scraped the soup back into the kettle for supper, because we do not throw away food, not even then.

IV. Three O’Clock

The flax bench

We went back to the field because the mustard was still in it. By three the blue was mostly down. The petals lay in the furrows between the rows like a blue sheet somebody had torn and dropped, and the flax stood above them green with its little bare stars, already making seed.

Nobody spoke for a long time. Then the girls in the line began, very low, one of the old hymns, the long ones from the old country that take a quarter of an hour, and the young men took it up, and Andreas did not.

He said, to nobody: It’s easy for him. He’s lying in bed. He doesn’t have to stand in the yard.

Nobody answered that. It was what we all thought. An old man in a bed can say set it down. He has already set down everything; his hands can’t hold a spoon. We were young and strong and the town was taking us apart one animal at a time, and our brother was in the guardhouse at Funston, and the four Rockport boys were on their way to an island we had never seen. And it was coming on to evening, and the blue was down, and we were still holding it. Every one of us, the whole line. That was the first time in the summer that all of us knew it at once, standing in one row in one field.

We said to each other, then, out loud, which we had not done before: Before the last of it falls. Not tomorrow. Today. We went back to pulling mustard as if it mattered more than anything, and in a way, out there, it did.

V. Six O’Clock

The flax bench · toward evening

This is the part that is told more than one way, so we tell it the way most of us saw it.

The sun was low over the river and came across the field flat and yellow, and at the far end of the flax, where it runs out against the cottonwoods, Vetter Paul was standing.

He was in his black coat, with his hat, the way he went to the Lord’s Supper. He was standing up straight, which he had not done since Easter. The light was behind him and also, some say, came from him; the girls at the near end of the line said the flax around his feet was blue again, a ring of it, as if it had opened for him late. He did not walk toward us. He stood, and he looked down the line at each of us, and then he raised his right hand, the one that did not work, and held it out flat and turned it over, the way you turn your hand over to let something drop out of it.

Then he said, and we all heard it at our different distances the same, as if he were standing beside each one: the story of Leonhard Keyser, that we had all had from him as children. How Keyser was a priest who came over to our people, and they took him to burn in Bavaria in 1527. How on the cart he leaned down and pulled a flower from the edge of the field, and held it up to the judge riding beside him. How they could not burn it.

He held it up, Vetter Paul said. He didn’t hold on to it.

And then he gave us the words he had read to us at the long table every year of our lives.

… but in the morning, yea, also in the evening, all its beauty and all its glory are vanished. Thus it is also with all the comeliness of man; but few know themselves.

— Matthias Servaes, letter, in the Martyrs Mirror

… to requite hatred with love, evil with good, cursing with blessing; yea, must they, according to the doctrine of Christ, pray for them who oppress and persecute them.

— Martyrs Mirror

Then he turned his hand back over, empty, and was not there.

We are not people who see things. Our grandfathers came out of Russia without one vision among them, only the Book and the bell. We will tell you only what happened next. A hundred and nineteen of us, and the line in the field was fewer than forty, but the women in the kitchen garden saw him too, and the old men mending harness at the barn door, and the children at the children’s table through the window. Each of us had in our two hands what we had carried out that morning. The steers, the sheep, the paint, the word at the counter, the boy at Funston, the girl at the bank. The wanting it back. And each of us, wherever we stood, turned the hand over. Andreas Kleinsasser opened his and looked at it a long time and then laughed, short, as if somebody had told him a joke he had heard before, and sat down in the furrow on the petals and cried, and nobody went to him because nobody needed to.

It was not noon and it was not tomorrow. The last petals were still coming down.

His granddaughter Sanna, who sat with him afternoons, told us that at six o’clock he was asleep in his bed with the window open toward the field. She did not leave him for a minute. She said his right hand was lying on the cover turned palm up, which it could not do, and she had not turned it.

VI. Gebet

The evening service · and after

At the evening service Vetter Michel prayed for the men from town, every one by name, Mr. Lindahl too, and that night for the first time that summer we could all say amen to it.

Vetter Paul died in September, before the move. By then the elders had been north and bought land across the line, and that fall and the next spring we went, nearly all of us, the way our people have always gone when a country could not keep us: Moravia, Hungary, Russia, Dakota, Canada. We sold the forty acres on the bench for what we could get. In November the two Hofer brothers from Rockport died at Leavenworth, and one of them came home in the uniform he had refused to put on, and we sang for them on the way north. Samuel Kleinsasser came out of Funston thin as a lath in the spring of 1919 and found his brother waiting for him at the depot in Manitoba with a team.

We are not saying we never hated again. We are people. But we knew after that what it was to set it down all at once, a whole field of us, and that it can be done in a day, and that it does not take a lifetime unless you let it.

The new owner of the bench did not sow flax. He put it in wheat. But flax volunteers; the seed lies in the ground a long time. For years, people on the river road said, a few plants came up every summer along the edge by the cottonwoods, in a ring, and opened blue at sunup, and let go before noon.

Background

The verse

As the Vassika plant sheds its withered flowers, men should shed passion and hatred, O ye Bhikshus!Dhammapada 377, tr. F. Max Müller (1881)

In the commentary, five hundred monks take a meditation subject from the Buddha and go to practice in the forest. They see jasmine flowers that opened that morning dropping from their stems by evening, and resolve to be released from lust, hatred and delusion before the flowers are released from theirs. The Buddha, sitting in his Perfumed Chamber at Jetavana, sends forth a radiant image of himself to them and speaks the verse, and all five hundred reach awakening. Read the full commentary story.

The Hutterites in 1918

The Hutterites are communal Anabaptists who trace their origin to Moravia in the 1520s and came to Dakota Territory from Russia between 1874 and 1877. They hold all property in common, eat together, attend a service every evening, and practice Gelassenheit, yieldedness or self-surrender. As German-speaking pacifists they were singled out during the First World War. In the summer and fall of 1918 colonies in the Dakotas and Montana had sheep and cattle seized and auctioned to buy Liberty Bonds, and drafted young men who refused uniforms were held in army camps and prisons. Four men from the Rockport colony, David, Joseph and Michael Hofer and Jacob Wipf, were court-martialed and held at Alcatraz, then moved to Fort Leavenworth, where Joseph died on November 29 and Michael on December 2, 1918; Joseph’s body was sent home dressed in the uniform he had refused. Nearly all the colonies moved to Canada in 1918 and the years after (Encyclopedia of the Great Plains). The colony in this story, its people and its flax field are invented.
