I. The Source

Upton, Long Island · September 1965

Every morning at eight Gene Mroz pressed a black button in a concrete hut in the pine woods, and a hundred yards off a canister of cesium went down a pipe into the ground. The red light over the gate went out and a green one came on. For four hours after that, people could go in.

At noon he counted them out against the clipboard, counted the film badges back onto their board, walked the fence, locked the gate, and pressed the other button. The canister rose to the top of its pole and stayed there twenty hours. It stayed through the afternoon and the night, in rain, on Christmas, and it shone on the trees with a light nobody could see. It had done that every day since the twenty-second of November, 1961.

You could see what the light had done. From the gate you walked in through ordinary Long Island woods, pitch pine and oak with huckleberry underneath. After a while the pines were dead and only the oaks had leaves, and the white oaks there were sticky with aphids, more aphids than anybody had ever counted on a tree. Farther in, the oaks were dead too. They stood gray, with the bark coming off in plates, and nothing was green above the knee but blueberry and huckleberry. Then the bushes stopped at a ring of sedge as fine as hair, like a lawn nobody had planted. Inside the sedge, around the pole, there was sand, gray sticks and a little moss. The pilots out of Westhampton said that from the air it looked like a target.

Gene was twenty-two. He had grown potatoes with his father outside Riverhead until he was eighteen, and gone to Cornell on the State's money, and come home in June with a degree in botany. His mother's brother worked at the Laboratory and had heard that the forest people wanted a technician who knew plants and did not mind mosquitoes. So Gene ran the source. He read the dosimeters on their stakes. He emptied the litter baskets, bagged the leaves, dried them and weighed them. On the first Monday of each month, outside the fence where the woods were whole, the crew took down one tree, a pine or an oak, and Gene carried it out in pieces and weighed every piece, needles and twigs and wood, to the tenth of a gram.

He had a white coat with his name over the pocket. He ate his lunch at a table with men who had doctorates. He had never been so happy in his life.

II. Sunday

Polish Town, Riverhead · the same month

After the ten o'clock at St. Isidore's they ate at his grandmother's on Pulaski Street, eleven at the table. His Uncle Stash sat at the corner by the stove, where he had sat since before Gene was born.

Stash Kozakiewicz had left school at fifteen for the potatoes. In 1942 the Army swore him in at Camp Upton, on the same sand the Laboratory stood on now. His own father had been a recruit there in 1918, and used to tell about a first sergeant from the coal country who said every man's name right the first time, Kozakiewicz included. When Stash came home from Italy he went back out to Upton and asked for work, and they gave him a broom. That was 1948. He was in the glass shop now. At the table it was understood that Gene had gone further in three months than Stash had in seventeen years, and Stash had never said a word against it.

He waited until the women were clearing.

Genek. You do any good out there? One thing?

Sure I do.

What?

First of the month we take a tree. And every day I put the source down in the morning and send it back up at noon.

What for?

Gene had not said it aloud before, and he was pleased with how it came out. That's how you get to be one of them, Uncle. You keep the numbers clean. They say a plot like that ought to run a hundred years, and whoever stays with it will know what a forest is. You do it right long enough and it makes you into something.

Who told you that?

Everybody up there. My professors.

Stash turned his coffee cup a quarter turn on the saucer, and then another quarter.

They're smart men, he said. I blow their glass. But how a man gets made into something, they don't know that, and you don't. They got that way and they couldn't tell you how. I know one that can. You got four hours tomorrow with that fire of yours in the ground. Come over to the shop at ten.

III. The Glass Shop

Upton · Monday, ten o'clock

The shop was a long room with north windows, and gas and oxygen piped along the benches. Glass tubing stood in racks by size, like organ pipes. Mr. Greiner sat at the first bench. He came from Lauscha, in the Thuringian forest, where his father and his grandfather had blown glass. He had come to New York in 1926 and to the Laboratory in its first year, and nearly everything on the site that held a vacuum had been through his hands or through Stash's. He was sixty-three, a small man in a gray shop coat buttoned to the neck, with rose-colored spectacles pushed up on his forehead. On the bench in front of him a burner made a blue cone the length of a finger.

Stash told him about it in four sentences. Mr. Greiner listened and looked at Gene.

It is so? As he says?

Yes, sir.

So. He shut the oxygen, and the flame went soft and yellow. He shut the gas. I know your fire in the woods. It is a strong thing. You may send it up and take it down a hundred years. In a hundred years it will be a tenth so strong, and you will be dead, and it will not have made anything of you. It does not know you are there. Better you sit one minute and look at a man who is already made, and look at him the way you look at your doctors. One minute. It would do more.

What man?

You have eaten Sunday dinner with him since you had teeth.

He opened the drawer under the bench and took out a small brown book with the spine gone. He had bought it on Fourth Avenue in 1927 to learn English from, he said, because he already knew what it said. He had won the same lectures in German as a school prize in Lauscha when he was twelve. He found the place without looking for it.

You have the glittering beauty of gold and silver, and the still higher lustre of jewels, like the ruby and diamond; but none of these rival the brilliancy and beauty of flame. What diamond can shine like flame? It owes its lustre at night-time to the very flame shining upon it. The flame shines in darkness, but the light which the diamond has is as nothing until the flame shine upon it, when it is brilliant again. The candle alone shines by itself, and for itself, or for those who have arranged the materials.

— Michael Faraday, The Chemical History of a Candle, Lecture I

You are the diamond, Mr. Greiner said. It is no shame. Everybody is, at twenty-two. You stand next to a strong thing and you think the shine is yours. Stanislaus. Make him a ring seal.

Stash sat down at the second bench and pulled his own spectacles down. He took two lengths of tubing from the rack, one that would go inside the other. He lit the gas and fed it oxygen until the flame roared and drew down to a point.

Then he put the glass in. He held a piece in each hand, the left hand under and the right hand over, and both hands turned. They turned at the same speed and they did not stop. The glass went soft and orange, and the flame above it flared yellow. He took it out and breathed into the rubber hose he held in his teeth, and put it back. There was nothing on his face.

Gene had watched this man eat for twenty-two years. The hands were his grandmother's hands, thick in the fingers and cracked at the knuckles from the potato fields, and they moved like water going around a stone. Somewhere in the minute he stopped thinking about what he would say afterward. He forgot that he had a coat with his name on it. When the glass came out of the fire for the last time, one tube stood inside the other, joined by a ring of glass with no line in it anywhere, and it went from orange to clear while he looked.

Stash set it in the rack and pushed his spectacles up.

Seventeen years, Mr. Greiner said. The first year I gave him a glass rod to turn in his fingers. No fire. He turned it at the supper table, I think.

He turns his cup, Gene said.

So. You have seen something.

At a quarter to twelve Gene walked back through the woods. He counted the people out and counted the badges and locked the gate, and sent the source up. Then he stood at the fence a while under the red light. He had taken the pencil off the clipboard, and he was turning it in his fingers, the left hand under and the right hand over.

IV. The Candle

Upton · the week before Christmas

Every December Mr. Greiner gave a lecture for the children of the Laboratory. Nobody had asked him to, and nobody paid him for it. He did the whole of Faraday in an hour and a half, with one candle, a bell jar, a balance, and a flask of lime-water that turned to milk when he breathed into it through a tube. Stash stood at the end of the table and handed him each thing before he reached for it, as he had every year since 1950.

That year Gene carried the boxes over from the shop. He sat on the aisle steps among two hundred children, and when Mr. Greiner came to the end he said the end of the book, which he did not need the book for.

Indeed, all I can say to you at the end of these lectures (for we must come to an end at one time or other) is to express a wish that you may, in your generation, be fit to compare to a candle; that you may, like it, shine as lights to those about you; that, in all your actions, you may justify the beauty of the taper by making your deeds honourable and effectual in the discharge of your duty to your fellow-men.

— Lecture VI

Then Stash did the last thing, the one the children waited for all year. He bent and blew the candle out, cleanly, so that a thread of white smoke stood up from the wick. He lit a taper. He held it in the smoke, three inches above the wick and touching nothing, and the flame ran down the smoke by itself and the candle was burning again.

The children yelled. Stash did not look at it. He was already reaching under the table for the box the bell jar went in, and Gene got there first and held it open for him.

Background

The verse

If a man for a hundred years worship Agni (fire) in the forest, and if he but for one moment pay homage to a man whose soul is grounded (in true knowledge), better is that homage than sacrifice for a hundred years.Dhammapada 107, tr. F. Max Müller (1881)

In the commentary, the elder Sāriputta asks his nephew, a young Brahmin, whether he ever does a single good deed. The nephew answers that month after month he kills one animal and tends the sacrificial fire, because his teachers say this is the way to the world of Brahmā. Sāriputta tells him that neither he nor his teachers know the way, and brings him to the Buddha, who says that a hundred years of tending the fire would not come to the worth of one moment's honor paid to his disciple. Brookhaven, 1965

Brookhaven National Laboratory was founded in 1947 in the Long Island pine barrens on the site of Camp Upton, the Army post where draftees were trained in 1917 and 1918 and inducted again in the Second World War. On November 22, 1961, ecologists there began irradiating a stand of oak and pine with gamma rays from 9,500 curies of cesium-137, raised on a pole in the middle of the woods for twenty hours of every day and lowered into a shielded hole for the other four. The experiment, led by George Woodwell, ran into the 1970s. The forest died back in rings: bare ground nearest the source, then a mat of Pennsylvania sedge, then heath shrubs, then oaks without pines, then woods that looked untouched. Pines died at exposures the oaks survived, and aphids multiplied on the stressed white oaks. Cesium-137 has a half-life of about thirty years, so a source loses roughly nine-tenths of its strength in a century. The technician's routine here, the hut, the lights and the monthly tree, is imagined around those facts.

The Laboratory had its own scientific glassblowers from its first years. Many American shops of that time were run by men trained in Lauscha and the other glass villages of Thuringia. Mr. Greiner, Stash Kozakiewicz, Gene Mroz and the children's lecture are invented. Riverhead's Polish Town grew up around St. Isidore's Church, built in 1906 by immigrants who had come to work the potato farms of eastern Long Island. Michael Faraday gave his six Christmas lectures for young people, The Chemical History of a Candle, at the Royal Institution in London in 1860, and the printed text includes the relighting of a blown-out candle through its own smoke and the breath that clouds lime-water. The first sergeant who said every recruit's name right is in The Doorsill.
