As If Living

…and the person fully described, as if living.
— Instructions to Assistant Marshals, Ninth Census (1870)

I heard it told this way, down on Caney Creek.

I. The Portfolio

Brazoria, Texas · June 1870

Moses Gallion was born with a veil over his face, and his mother said that was why he could find things. When he was six he walked straight out into the canebrake below the Ransom quarter and came back leading a two-year-old girl everybody else had been calling for since noon. When he was eleven he told the overseer where the strayed mules were, and they were there. Later he found a drowned man in Caney Creek that three boats had dragged for, and after that the people on the creek stopped asking him to find things and started being a little careful around him, the way you are careful around a well.

He was twenty-six in the summer of 1870. He could read and write, which he had learned in a Bureau school in a church at Brazoria from a Massachusetts woman who kept a switch on her desk and never once used it. He had a good hand. That was why, in June, a man from the marshal's office came down the river and swore him in on the courthouse steps as an assistant marshal of the United States, and gave him a leather portfolio of blank schedules and a printed sheet of instructions, and told him he would be paid by the name, two cents for every living person and two cents for every dead one.

He read the instructions that night by a lard lamp, all of them, twice. Every person in the family was to be written, the head first, with the family name before each given name. The column for color was always to be filled. The entries were to be read over to the party giving the information, that all mistakes may be corrected on the spot. He was to make the enumeration by actual inquiry at every dwelling-house. The duty could not be performed by deputy or proxy. There was a sentence he read four times:

They will approach every individual in a conciliatory manner; respect the prejudices of all; adapt their inquiries to the comprehension of foreigners and persons of limited education; and strive in every way to relieve the performance of their duties from the appearance of obtrusiveness.

— Instructions to Assistant Marshals, Ninth Census (1870)

He did not know what obtrusiveness was. He looked it up in the Massachusetts woman's dictionary, which she had left to the church when she went home, and understood that it meant coming into a house as if it were yours.

There were more than five thousand people in the county who had been owned in the last census. They had been written down in that one by age and sex and color, a number in a column under a white man's name. Nobody had ever asked them anything. Moses went up Caney Creek on a borrowed mule in the heat of June and asked them, at every cabin in every old quarter, in the shade of the galleries, with his portfolio on his knee: the name, the age at the last birthday, the trade, whether they could read, whether they could write, where they were born. Some of them gave him a name they had picked out in 1865 and were trying for the first time. Some of them asked him to read it back twice. An old woman at the Varner place asked him to spell hers out loud so she could hear what it looked like.

There was a second schedule, for the dead. Every person who had died in the twelve months ending the first of June, with the age and the cause. He wrote those too. That year on Caney Creek it was mostly congestion of the lungs in the winter and flux in the summer, and a man kicked by a mule, and a good many children. In March, on the Ransom place, there had been an old woman named Hagar Bell, sixty, of a cough. He had known her all his life. She had been the one who washed him the day he was born and took the veil off his face and dried it on a board and gave it to his mother to keep.

II. Dwelling Number

Caney Creek · August 1870

It was late in August, and he was nearly done. He had come down the west side of the creek since morning and had one more quarter to do before dark, and he crossed at a place he knew, below the old Ransom sugar house, where the bank was low and there was a gravel bar. The mule went into the water to its knees, and to its belly, and then it was climbing out on a bank he did not know.

There was a lane going up from the water between two rows of chinaberry trees. At the end of it were houses. He counted eleven. They were white, with galleries all across the front, and glass in the windows, and green shutters, and every one of them stood in a yard swept clean in the pattern of a broom. He had been up and down Caney Creek his whole life and there was no lane there and there were no houses like that anywhere in the county, not even the Ransoms' before the war.

The mule did not seem to mind. He let it walk.

At the first gate there was a stalk of ribbon cane in full tassel standing in the air by itself, a foot off the ground, with nothing holding it, the silver plumes stirring a little the way they do in October. A man stood on the gallery. He was tall and very black and he was dressed better than the judge, and there was a light about him, the way there is about a man standing in a door with a lamp behind him, except there was no lamp. He came down the steps and out to the gate and stood in front of the mule with his hands folded, as if Moses were somebody.

Moses did what the instructions said. He got down. He took off his hat. He said he was taking the census for the United States and he was obliged to ask some questions at every dwelling-house, and that the man did not have to be afraid of it. Then, because he could not help it, he said: Mister, you have got a fine house. What did you do to get it?

Don't ask me that, the man said.

He said it the way you would ask somebody not to look in a pot that had nothing much in it. Moses asked again. He said please.

I never gave anything, the man said finally. I didn't have anything. I didn't go to meeting much. I didn't sing. All I did, I told the truth.

To who?

To anybody. To them, too. He nodded down the lane, toward the creek and the old sugar house and everything on the other side of the water. Even when a lie would have done me better. That's all. That's the whole of it.

Moses wrote him down. Dwelling number one, family number one. He asked his age and the man told him. He asked the value of his real estate and the man looked at the house as if he had never thought about it. You'd have to ask who gave it, he said. When it was done, Moses read the entries back to him, as the law required, so that all mistakes might be corrected on the spot, and the man listened carefully to every word and said, I'm older than that. Put down four more years. I told them younger once, when they were selling me. I want it right.

•••

He went to the next gate, and the next. At every one a woman came out, and at every one she did as the man had done, came down and stood before him, and when he asked her what she had done to live in a house like that, she would not say. She looked at her hands. She said it was nothing. He asked them all a second time, and he said please every time, and in the end they told him.

At the third house it was Hagar Bell.

She was not sixty. She was not anything he could have put a number to. She had the same gap in her front teeth. She stood at the gate with the light on her, and she looked at him with his portfolio and his pen and the creek water still dripping from the mule's belly, and she laughed and said, Moses. You found your way here too.

Aunt Hagar, I wrote you down in March. I wrote you dead.

I know it. Write me again.

He asked her the question and she would not answer it, and he asked again.

I never had a thing to give, she said. You know that. You know who I belonged to. He did. Everybody on the creek did. Old Ransom would hit a person with whatever was in his hand, a cane knife, a hoe handle, a stick of stovewood, and he had once laid a man's scalp open with a sugar ladle for spilling the strike. Every time it came up in me, Hagar said, I said to myself, Hagar, that man can sell your children. He can sell them tomorrow to Louisiana. You sit on it. And I sat on it. I sat on it like a lid on a pot. Forty years. That's all I did.

The others were short. A young woman at the fifth house said that when she was a girl she had been set to mind the cane by the river road to keep the hogs out of it, and a preacher came walking by on a pass, an old man, and she broke him a stalk. That was all. At the sixth a woman said she gave a persimmon. At the seventh, a cucumber. At the eighth a girl not more than fifteen said a hatful of dewberries, to a runaway they were bringing back tied behind a horse, and she had got a whipping for it. At the ninth, a bunch of radishes. At the tenth an old, old woman who could hardly stand said that she had threaded a handful of chinaberries on a string for a little child that was crying, a child that did not belong to her, so it would have a necklace like the white girl at the big house. That was all I did, every one of them said at the end. That's how I got here.

He wrote every one of them, as if living, the way the instruction said. Name, age, sex. Occupation, which they had trouble with, because nobody there seemed to do anything, and he wrote keeping house, mostly. Where born: Virginia, Virginia, Maryland, Africa, Kentucky, this county. He read every entry back. They corrected him. The girl who gave the dewberries spelled her name for him, letter by letter, slowly, and watched the pen.

When he came to the column for color, he looked up at them standing in their yards with the light on them, and he could not tell, and he did not write anything there. It was the only time all summer he left it empty.

At the eleventh house there was nobody. The door was open and the floor was swept and there was a chair on the gallery. He numbered it, and drew the dash through the column the instructions said to draw for a house standing empty, and turned the mule around.

He crossed back at the gravel bar below the Ransom sugar house in the dark. When he looked back from the water there was no lane, only the canebrake going down to the creek, and a whip-poor-will, and the lamps of the quarter he had meant to do that evening, half a mile off, where people were wondering what had become of the census.

III. The Sugar Maker

The Ransom quarter · the next evening

Absalom Pleasant had been the sugar maker on the Ransom place for twenty-two grinding seasons. He was the one who stood at the last kettle of the train with the ladle in the steam and the whole quarter waiting behind him, and said when. Two hundred hogsheads a year depended on him knowing the moment the boiling syrup would strike and turn to sugar, and he knew it by the sound, people said, a change in the boil no one else could hear. There had been no sugar made on Caney Creek since the war. When Moses had come to him in June, Mr. Pleasant had given his name and his age, seventy, and when Moses asked his trade he had said sugar maker, and Moses had written it down, though there was nothing in the column for a man who was renting four acres of cotton on shares from his old owner's son. The instructions said that if a man could not tell intelligibly what he was, find out what he does. Mr. Pleasant had told him intelligibly.

Moses found him on his step at sundown with a pan of field peas in his lap, shelling. He sat down on the ground in front of him. He did not say good evening. He told him the whole thing, the lane, the chinaberry trees, the cane standing in the air, the eleven houses. He told him about Hagar. He got the portfolio out and showed him the page.

Mr. Pleasant could not read. He looked at it a long time anyhow, holding it out at arm's length, the way he would have held a ladle of syrup up to the light.

Mr. Pleasant, Moses said, can a person get a house like that for a persimmon? For a bunch of radishes? For not saying a lie?

The old man gave him the page back.

What are you asking me for? he said. Didn't they tell you?

Moses sat a while.

Yes, sir, he said. They told me.

Well then.

He went on shelling. The peas went into the pan and the hulls went into his lap. A child came across the yard to them with a gourd of water and he drank and gave the child the rest, and when the child was gone again he said it, plainly, without looking up, the way he must have said strike over the last kettle for twenty-two years: once, and the whole house moving at the word.

Say what's so. Don't keep a mad. When somebody asks you, give him something, if it's no more than a little. That'll carry you there. Any one of them will carry you.

That's all?

That's three. He tipped the pan to the light to see what he had. They told you one apiece.

IV. Family Number

Caney Creek · September 1870 and after

He told it at the brush arbor on the second Sunday in September, because Mr. Pleasant told him to, and he read the names off the page. There were people under the arbor who had known every one of them. The girl who gave the dewberries had a sister there, who had not heard her name said out loud in nine years and put her apron over her face. Afterward people stood around under the trees and did not go home, and talked low, about small things, a dipper of water, a biscuit, a lie they had not told, a blow they had not given back, the way people talk about money they had forgotten they had put away. An old man named Ned Cotton said he had given a stalk of cane once to a preacher on the river road, before the war, and was he the one, and nobody knew.

Moses sent his portfolio up to the marshal at the end of the month, every schedule filled and signed and sworn. He kept back one sheet. He was paid by the name, and he was not paid for those.

By the grinding season of 1871 the kettles on Caney Creek were going again, with convicts leased from the state penitentiary to feed the fires, and the men at the furnace doors were wearing chains. Mr. Pleasant would not go near it. He made molasses in his own yard in a wash pot, from his own two rows of ribbon cane, and the children of the quarter stood around it in the smoke with tin cups, and he would tip the ladle to the light and listen and say now, and they never knew what he heard.

Moses Gallion lived to be eighty-one and taught school at Columbia for forty of those years. He was the enumerator twice more. He kept the sheet in his Bible between the Testaments, and when his granddaughter found it there after the funeral, in 1925, it was brown at the folds and the ink had gone the color of molasses, eleven dwellings on Caney Creek in his good hand, a dash through the last one, the column for color left empty all the way down. Beside the tenth house, the old woman who threaded the chinaberries, he had written something in the margin in a later ink, very small, where the instructions allowed nothing to be written: The child was M. G.

Background

The verse

Speak the truth, do not yield to anger; give, if thou art asked for little; by these three steps thou wilt go near the gods.Dhammapada 224, tr. F. Max Müller (1881)

In the commentary, the elder Mahā Moggallāna, the disciple known for his powers, travels to a heaven and stops at the doors of the shining mansions there. He asks each deity how such glory was earned, and each is ashamed to say, because it was so little. One only told the truth. One, a slave in a former life under a master who would strike off a head with whatever was in his hand, kept back her anger. Others gave a stalk of sugarcane, a persimmon, a cucumber, a berry, a handful of radishes, a handful of nimb fruit. Moggallāna asks the Buddha whether such small things can win heaven, and the Buddha answers, "Why ask me? Didn't they tell you?" and speaks the verse. Brazoria County, Texas, 1870

The lower Brazos, Caney Creek and Oyster Creek were called the sugar bowl of Texas, and before the war Brazoria County made most of the state's sugar on plantations worked by enslaved people. In 1860 the county held 5,110 enslaved people, 2,027 white residents and six free Black residents. Sugar fell away after emancipation and came back, by 1871, on the labor of leased convicts; several of the old plantations later became state prison farms. In 1870 the county's voters helped elect George T. Ruby, a Black former Freedmen's Bureau teacher from Galveston, to the Texas Senate. See the Handbook of Texas on Brazoria County.

The Ninth Census took June 1, 1870 as its day and was gathered by assistant marshals going door to door. It was the first census to list every resident by name; in 1850 and 1860 enslaved people had been counted without names under their owners. The instructions to assistant marshals asked them to approach everyone "in a conciliatory manner," to read the entries back, and to describe anyone who had died since census day "as if living." Under the act of 1850 they were paid two cents for each living person and two cents for each dead one. The appointments went to Republicans, and in the South many of the assistant marshals were Black men; the census superintendent, Francis A. Walker, later judged that the count had missed several hundred thousand Black Southerners.
