Rule Two

As it was passed along to me, at the elevator in Oakesdale.

I. The Gum

Whitman County, Washington · September 1968

Lois Kincaid drove the north route for the Oakesdale school, forty-four miles of gravel and two of blacktop, and she had driven it since the fall of 1961, when the district bought a second bus and nobody else applied. She was out of the yard at ten to seven. She came back at a quarter past eight with forty-one children, or forty if one of the Hoffs had an earache, and she knew every one of them by the back of the head.

The bus was a 1959 Chevrolet with a Wayne body, and it had a long mirror over the windshield, a strip of glass the width of the aisle and a little more, bent so that it held the whole bus at once. In it Lois could see forty-one children and the gray ribbon of road going away behind them out the back door. It was the first thing she looked at after the road and the last thing before it. She did not need to turn around. Children thought she had an eye in the back of her head, and she let them.

The hills that year were already worked down for fall seeding when school began, brown and smooth as the side of a loaf, and so steep in places that the Rademachers ran a combine that leveled itself on the slope like a man leaning into a wind. Out the left-hand windows on the north leg you saw Steptoe Butte all morning, the only thing in that country that was rock instead of dirt. On a clear day she could see Mount Spokane, fifty-odd miles off, and on those mornings she said to the windshield, Well, there it is, as if it might not have been.

Her son Danny had ridden this bus from first grade through his senior year, and then gone a year and a half to Washington State in Pullman and drawn houses in the margins of his engineering notes and come home. The draft board had him by Easter. He went over in July, to a place he spelled out carefully in his first letter so she could find it on the map at the library, in a river delta south of Saigon where he said everything was wet, his socks and his paper and his cigarettes. The letters came every week or ten days. Where the stamp should go he wrote FREE, in the small square capitals he had learned in mechanical drawing, and she kept them in the glove compartment of the bus in a rubber band, in order.

The first week of school she walked down the aisle of the bus after her morning run, as she did every day, to look for mittens and lunch sacks, and she put her hand on the back seat and took it away. The vinyl had been green once. Under the last two benches, where the big kids had sat since Eisenhower, the gum was laid on in layers like the strata in a road cut, pink and gray and black, and somebody had pressed a row of pennies into it.

That afternoon she stood up in the aisle before the first stop.

Those back two seats are a disgrace, she said. Whoever cleans them can have them. For the year.

The big kids got on last, on the Elberton road, and so did not hear it. The little kids got on first, on the long loop past the Pfaff place and down along the creek, and they heard every word. Becky Pfaff was eleven, the oldest of five, and she organized it. On Friday after school seventeen of them, Pfaffs and Hoffs and Lindemans and the Coulter twins and the two little Ottos and some others, stayed in the bus in the school yard with butter knives and a putty knife from the shop and a coffee can of hot water from the janitor, and they scraped. Lois sat in the driver's seat and graded nothing and watched in the mirror. It took them an hour and twenty minutes. The Otto boy who was six found the pennies and kept them.

On Monday the seventeen sat in the back. They sat three and four to a bench, knees up, with the clean green vinyl under them, and they were so pleased that nobody talked.

The big boys got on at the Rademacher mailbox. There were six of them who sat together, Gary Rademacher and his cousin Neil, the two Dorman brothers, Mike Schultz and a tall boy named Pete Varney who did whatever Gary did, and they were juniors and seniors and had sat in the back since they were freshmen, as the big ones before them had, as Danny had. They came down the aisle with their letter jackets open and stopped.

Move, said Gary.

We cleaned it, said Becky Pfaff. She said whoever cleaned it.

We're seniors. It's ours.

We were first, said Becky.

Lois had slowed for the railroad crossing below the Dorman place. She stopped short of the track, as the law said, and pulled the door lever and opened the door to listen, as the law also said, for a train you could not see. What she heard instead came from behind her. It was children screaming, the real kind. She looked up into the mirror. There were letter jackets in the back of it and small arms over small heads, and against the back window one of the Otto boys was holding his face, and there was blood on the glass.

She shut the door and set the brake and turned the key off, and the bus went quiet except for the Otto boy.

She walked back. She had never been a tall woman.

What was that?

They told her, all at once, the little ones. The big boys did not say anything. Neil Rademacher was looking at his own knuckles. Lois took the clean handkerchief out of her coat pocket and tipped Jimmy Otto's head forward and pinched his nose for him and held it.

Nobody hits anybody on this bus, she said. Not ever. Not for a seat. She looked at Gary Rademacher, whom she had driven to first grade. You were six once. Somebody bigger than you sat where they wanted. You remember what that was like. Everybody's scared of that. Everybody is.

She sent the six of them to the middle of the bus and they went. She held Jimmy Otto's nose until it stopped. Then she walked back up the aisle, started the bus, looked both ways at the empty track, and crossed it.

That night she made a card. She did it at the kitchen table after Walt had gone to bed, on the back of a cut-down calendar page, with Danny's T-square from his room so the lines would be straight, because he had left it there leaning against the drafting board and she had not moved either one. She lettered it as plainly as she could. RULES OF BUS 2. And under it: 1. NOBODY HITS ANYBODY. In the morning she taped it up above the windshield beside the mirror, where the whole bus could see it. Nobody said a word about it. It held.

II. Bang

The Elberton road · November 1968

It held through football. It held through the election, which the bus argued about for a week in the voices of its fathers, and through the first hard frost, when she let the engine run twenty minutes in the yard before seven so the heater would have a chance, and the heater still only warmed the first four rows. The big boys sat in the middle where it was warmer and said that was why. The little ones sat in the back in their snowsuits and bounced over the axle on the washboard and liked it.

Danny wrote that they had been moving around a good deal and that he was fine. He asked whether his dad had got the winter wheat in before the rain. He asked whether she was still driving the old crate, and said to tell whoever was in the back seat now that it was the best seat on the bus, because of the bounce.

Gary Rademacher would be eighteen on the ninth of December. Everybody on the bus knew it, because his mother had told everybody at church, the way you tell people about an operation. His older brother Ron was a Marine and had been at a place in the north of the country all spring that had been on the television, and had come home in October and was living in the bunkhouse and did not come up to the house for supper. Gary had stopped going out for basketball. He sat on the aisle in the middle of the bus with his knees against the seat in front of him and pushed, so that the seat moved, and whoever was in it moved, and there was no rule about that.

The morning it happened there was the first real snow, three inches, dry, blowing across the road in long white snakes, and she had the chains on. The six got on at the Rademacher mailbox already in a mood that she could see in the mirror. It started with Neil reaching back over the seat and flicking a Lindeman girl's ear, and it went from there, a knee here, a knuckle, a hat pulled off and thrown, and none of it quite what the card said, until one of the Dormans reached back and caught Becky Pfaff by her braid and pulled her head down hard against the top of the seat.

Then the seventeen stood up.

They stood on the benches in the back in their snowsuits, every one of them, the six-year-olds and Becky with her hand on her head, and they put out their arms with their first fingers straight and their thumbs cocked, and they shot the six. They shot them all together. Bang. Bang. Bang. You're dead. You're dead, you're dead, you're all dead. They had watched war on the television their whole lives and they knew how. They made the noise in the backs of their throats that a rifle makes on the television, and the little Otto boy made the noise of the machine gun on the jeep, and they did not stop.

She was at the top of the hill above the creek. She put her foot on the brake too hard and the bus slewed on the chains and came round a little sideways and stopped, and in the mirror forty-one children went forward against the seat backs and came back. Nobody was hurt.

She did not get up right away. She sat with both her hands on the wheel. The children could see her face in the long mirror, as she could see theirs, and the bus got very quiet, and she let it. Then she set the brake and turned off the key and went back.

We didn't hit, said Becky Pfaff before she got there. You said nobody hits. We didn't touch anybody. Her eyes were wet from the braid. They started it.

I know they did.

We didn't hit, said one of the Coulter twins.

No. She stood in the aisle between the six and the seventeen with her hand on the back of a seat. Sit down. All of you sit down.

They sat down. The Otto boy was still holding his finger out, not pointing it at anybody now, as if he did not know where to put it.

Who goes to catechism? she said.

Seven or eight hands went up, Presbyterians and Lutherans, and a Catholic or two, who had their own. Becky Pfaff's hand went up. Lois nodded at her.

The sixth commandment, said Lois. What is required.

Becky said it in the singsong in which it had been given to her, and Lois had been given it at the same age, in a church basement in 1931, from the same small blue book. Then Lois said the next one herself, the answer to what is forbidden, slowly, so that the Catholics could follow it.

The sixth commandment requireth all lawful endeavours to preserve our own life, and the life of others.

The sixth commandment forbiddeth the taking away of our own life, or the life of our neighbour unjustly, or whatsoever tendeth thereunto.

— Westminster Shorter Catechism, Q. 68–69

What's tendeth? said the Coulter twin.

Leans that way, said Lois. Anything that leans that way. She took the Otto boy's hand, the one he was still holding out, and folded the finger down into it, and the thumb, and gave it back to him. That.

Then she turned around so that she was looking at the six. Gary Rademacher had his eyes on the floor.

Every one of you on this bus wants to get home tonight, she said. Every one. You want it, and so does he, and so does she. It's the same thing in all of you. Look at him and you know what he wants. He wants what you want.

She did not say anything about her son. She did not need to. Every family on the route knew where he was. The Lindemans had sent him cookies.

She went back up the aisle and sat down. She took the card down from over the windshield, and got the pencil off the visor, and wrote under the first rule, as straight as she could without the T-square, against the steering wheel: 2. NOBODY POINTS ANYTHING AT ANYBODY. She taped it back up. Then she started the bus, backed it off the shoulder, and drove the rest of the way to Oakesdale in second gear. Nobody said a word. In the mirror forty-one children sat with their hands in their laps like people in church.

III. The Mailbox

The Rademacher mailbox · May 1969

Gary registered on the tenth of December, the day after his birthday, at the board in Colfax, and his mother told everybody. He went back out for basketball after Christmas and did not make it all the way back in shape, and he sat on the bench and was the one who went in to foul. At some point in the winter he moved up a seat, and then another, until he was sitting in the second row behind Lois, where the heat was, and he talked to her, mostly about the Rademachers' combine and whether a man could get on at the elevator, and once, only once, in March, about his brother in the bunkhouse, who he said had stopped sleeping.

Danny wrote that he was being moved to a desk in a base camp to draw maps, which he called the luckiest thing that had ever happened to anybody, and after that his letters were longer and had drawings in them. One was of the long mirror, from memory, with forty-one heads in it, and a little square up in the corner for the card he had heard about, with two lines in it too small to read. She kept it in the glove compartment with the rest.

The card stayed where it was. Nobody hit anybody on Bus 2 that year, and nobody pointed anything at anybody. By spring it was simply how things were on that bus, as the gum had been before. The seventeen kept the back. In May, when the peas came up in rows so fine you could see them from Steptoe like the lines on a ruled tablet, Becky Pfaff let the two little Ottos have the corner by the window, because of the bounce.

The last day of school was a Friday, warm, with the windows down. She let the seniors off first that afternoon, as she always did, at their own mailboxes, a long way round, and when they got to the Rademachers' she pulled over and opened the door. Gary got off with his cap and gown in a dry-cleaning bag over his shoulder, walked down into the ditch and up the other side to the mailbox, and stood there.

She shut the door. She looked in the mirror before she pulled out, the way she always did, and saw him in it, standing by the mailbox in the long gray ribbon of road behind the bus, and saw him lift his hand.

He held it up with the palm open, high, the way you hold your hand up to a tractor across a field. In the back window, all at once, the seventeen came up off their seats onto their knees and put their hands flat against the glass, all their palms, the six-year-olds and the Lindemans and Becky Pfaff, so many small hands that she could not see out the back for them, and could not see Gary, and then could see him again between their fingers as the road went down toward the creek, getting smaller, with his hand still up.

Lois took her left hand off the wheel and held it up too, open, to the mirror, where all of them could see it.

Background

The verse

All men tremble at punishment, all men love life; remember that thou art like unto them, and do not kill, nor cause slaughter.Dhammapada 130, tr. F. Max Müller (1881)

The Group of Six are the troublemakers of the monastic rule, the monks whose misconduct prompts one regulation after another. In the story just before this one, they come upon lodgings that the younger Group of Seventeen have made ready, say We are older; this belongs to us, and beat them out of it, and the Buddha, hearing the screams, forbids striking. This story is the sequel. The Six strike the Seventeen again, and this time the Seventeen answer with threatening gestures, raising their hands as if to strike back. The Buddha hears the outcry, asks what it is, and forbids the threatening gesture too, saying that a monk should consider that others tremble at the rod and hold life dear just as he does, and so should neither strike nor kill. The Palouse, 1968

The Palouse of southeastern Washington is a country of steep, rolling hills built of wind-blown silt, almost all of it plowed for wheat and legumes since the 1880s; it is now the most important lentil-growing region in the country. Above it stands Steptoe Butte, a quartzite island about a thousand feet higher than the hills around it and some twelve miles north of Colfax, from which Mount Spokane can be seen seventy miles away. Oakesdale, named for a railroad official, is a small wheat town in Whitman County with its own school. Lois Kincaid, her route, Bus 2 and all the families on it are invented.

The Westminster Shorter Catechism, completed in 1647, was written to teach children the Reformed faith in 107 questions and answers, and generations of Presbyterian children learned it by heart. In 1968 the war in Vietnam reached its worst year for Americans: the National Archives counts 16,899 U.S. military deaths with a date in that year, more than in any other. Young men registered for the draft at eighteen with their local boards, and the war came into rural living rooms every night on the television news.
