The Heavenly Road

You may talk of my name as much as you please,
And carry my name abroad,
But I really do believe I’m a child of God
As I walk in de heavenly road.
— “The Heavenly Road”

The way it was raised in the praise house, on St. Helena Island.

I. The Pan of Rice

Sandy Field plantation, St. Helena Island, South Carolina · 1854

There was a praise house at Sandy Field, as there was on every cotton place on the island, a board cabin at the end of the street where the people met three nights in the week. The one who led it was a man called Daddy Titus. He hoed his task like anybody. But it was known from Lands End to Coffin Point that if you were down in the valley, hunting for that thing and not finding it, Titus was the one to carry your dreams to.

On Sunday evenings he went down the street from door to door with a basket, for the sick and for burying, and each cabin put in what it had, a handful of grits, an egg, a copper. At the far end lived an old man named Mingo, who had been bought onto the place in the same lot with Titus’s father and had outlived his strength. He drew half a peck and had no garden anymore. When he heard the basket coming he thought, That is my own boy at the door, and I have not got one thing to put in. He doesn’t know how poor I am. He could not look him in the face. He went round behind the chimney and stood there until Titus had gone by.

The next Sunday he hid again. He told himself, The first thing I get hold of, I will give him. It was a long while coming.

That winter an old woman died on the next place. At the sitting-up they handed round what the family had, something to eat and a bit of cloth, as the custom was, and Mingo was given a pan of rice boiled soft and a square off a new head-handkerchief. He carried both home, and on the way he thought, This is what I will give Titus.

Titus was on his knees in the pines at that hour, where he went by himself to pray. He got up. Mingo has come by something, he thought, and wants me. I had better go. He took his own pan and went and stood at the old man’s door.

When Mingo saw who it was, his heart was satisfied. He made his manners and brought him in and set him on the one stool, and he took up the rice and began to turn it into Titus’s pan. When half was in, Titus laid his hand over the top.

Son, there is but the one helping here. Make me glad in the next world, not in this one. I want to give it all and keep none back. And he turned in the whole of it. Titus ate it there in front of him. When he was through, Mingo gave him the handkerchief, and bowed, and said, Let me come to what you came to.

So be it, said Titus. He thanked him and went home.

There is a saying that what a poor man gives out of an empty hand makes him glad past measuring, and it was so with Mingo. From that night his mind was easy, and he loved Titus for all the days he had left. They were not many. He was laid down in January.

II. Titus’s Name

1855 to the spring of 1862

Ten moons after Mingo was laid down, a boy was born to Rina, wife of Bacchus the boat carpenter, who kept the best cabin on the street and stood by the praise house and by Titus through everything. The women counted on their fingers and looked at one another and let it alone.

While she was carrying him a craving came on her. I want to boil rice soft, with milk and molasses, and feed it to Daddy Titus and every elder on the front bench, and not quit. And I want to tie the seeker’s knot on my head again, the way I wore it when I was a girl in the valley, and sit on the back bench with my pan, and eat what the elders leave. Her people said, That is a praying kind of craving, and they did it for her. She knotted the handkerchief over her forehead and sat on the last bench and ate what was left, and the craving went off. The old women said that knot was the sign the child would seek young.

On the day they named him they washed him before light and laid him in the new blanket. At Sandy Field a pair of blankets came to a house every few years, at Christmas, out of the master’s hand, and for most houses that was the whole of their wealth. This was the new one. The child lay in it and looked at Titus, and it is told that he knew him. When they took him up to carry him to be blessed, he had hooked his little finger in the blanket and it came up with him. His finger is caught, they said, and went to free it, and he screamed. Let him be. Don’t make him cry. They carried him blanket and all. When they held him down toward Titus he let go, and the blanket fell at Titus’s feet.

Nobody said, He is a baby and doesn’t know what he is doing. His people said, Daddy, take what the child gives you. And bless your servant, that has honored you with the best thing in his house.

What is he to be called?

He is to be called for you.

Then he is Titus.

His mother said in herself, Whatever this one wants, I will not stand in his road. There was sweet rice for the elders at his naming, and again when he first ate, and when he was first put in a shirt, and when his hair was first cut.

He was six on the seventh of November, 1861, when the gunboats came into Port Royal Sound. The island calls that day the Gun Shoot. The white people left inside of a day with what they could put in a boat. He was seven the spring after, the first spring anybody at Sandy Field ever planted for himself.

Ma, he said, I want to go in the valley, under Daddy Titus.

All right, son. I said a long time ago I would not stand in your road. Go on.

She had Titus to the house and fed him. Your servant says he wants to seek. I will bring him to you this evening. That evening she carried the boy to the praise house, with a basket on her head for the elders, and gave him into Titus’s hands.

Titus talked to him. Tite, the valley is hard. When you want it warm you get it cold, and when you want it cold you get it warm. Seekers have a weary time, and you have been raised soft.

I can do every bit of what you tell me.

All right, then. He gave him five things to carry into the wilderness and look at until he saw them, and only five, because a leader can spoil a seeker with too much: the hair of his head, the hair of his body, his nails, his teeth, his skin. Then he knotted the handkerchief over the boy’s forehead, and the house raised the one they raised for seekers.

Jesus call you. Go in de wilderness,
  Go in de wilderness, go in de wilderness,
Jesus call you. Go in de wilderness
  To wait upon de Lord.

All dem chil’en go in de wilderness
  To wait upon de Lord.

— “Go in the Wilderness”

For seven nights Rina and Bacchus stayed by the praise house and fed the elders nothing but rice with milk and molasses. By the fifth night the elders were saying to one another, low, A man cannot eat sweet rice every night of his life. On the evening of the seventh the two of them went home, and on the eighth day the boy went out with the basket.

III. Give-It

St. Helena Island and Beaufort · 1862

The island had heard. They say the little seeker is coming round today. Let us do something for him. Women on St. Helena carry everything on the head, on a cloth twisted into a ring for a pad. That morning five hundred of them twisted five hundred new cloths and set five hundred pans of victuals on them, and met him on the shell road. The next day they came into the praise-house yard and did it over. In two days he had a thousand pans and a thousand cloths, and he gave every one of them to the praise house for the old and the sick. The old people said that was the square of handkerchief coming back. They began to call him Give-It.

In July the Government gave up Edisto Island, and a thousand of the Edisto people were brought over and set down at the Village with what they had on their backs. One cold morning in December the boy went through the Village and saw them sitting round lightwood fires in the yards and under the houses.

Why are you all sitting at the fire?

We’re cold, bubba.

When it is cold a body ought to wrap up in a blanket. That keeps it off.

You are a lucky child. Maybe you can come by a blanket. Where are we to get one?

Well, he said, everybody that needs a blanket, come with me. And he had it called through the Village.

A thousand people got up and followed a child of seven down the road. It did not once come into his head to ask himself where blankets were to be had for so many. He took them along and started. He went from street to street across the island and had five hundred before he came to the ferry, and then he crossed to Beaufort.

A sutler on Bay Street, a Maine man, was sitting in his store door with five hundred army blankets stacked behind him. Somebody going by said, Mister, there is a little boy coming up the street with a crowd, collecting blankets. You had better put yours away.

Is he taking them or asking for them?

Asking. As gifts.

Then if I want to give, I will give, and if I don’t, I won’t. Go on about your business.

But when the man was gone the sutler thought it over. A man is ashamed not to give what is lying in plain sight, and nobody can blame him for what is put away. He had two officers’ blankets in the stock that were worth more than all the rest together. He folded them hem to hem and slid them into the middle of the pile.

Then the boy came into the street with the thousand behind him. The sutler looked at him, and something went through his whole body. He thought, For a child like that I would give the meat off my own heart, never mind blankets. He pulled the two out of the pile and laid them at the boy’s feet. Let me have some part in whatever it is you have got hold of.

So be it, said the boy, and thanked him.

That made five hundred on the island and five hundred in town. He gave the thousand to the Edisto people, and after that he was called Blanket. He had let one fall at his naming and a thousand came back to his hand, and all of them went through it. A lady who taught at the Brick Church wrote home about him, and the answer came that there were people in Boston who would pay his passage if he could be carried North and shown in the churches.

IV. Twenty Leagues

January 1863

His mother’s people came to the praise house to see him, and his father’s, every day, and sat and talked. On the first of January the paper was read out at Camp Saxton and ten oxen were roasted, and after that it seemed that everybody who passed Sandy Field turned in to look at him. He thought, As long as I stay here they will come and talk to me, and whether they talk or sit quiet, I will never come through. Suppose I got something to carry from the Teacher and went in the wilderness sure enough.

The one the leaders themselves called Teacher was Daddy Quaco, the well-digger. He had dug most of the wells on that end of St. Helena and a good share of the graves. He watched over all the praise houses there, and he had never been known to raise a song. He stood at the wall and based. The boy went and bowed to him and asked for something to carry.

Quaco looked at him awhile. Every place you lie down, ask who lay there before you. Carry that.

The boy bowed to Titus. He took his pan and his blanket. If I stop anywhere near, he thought, they will send for me. So he went a long way. In the last week of January the colored regiment went down the coast on three steamers, and one of them put in at Fernandina, on Amelia Island, on the edge of Florida, which the Union had held for most of a year. It is told that he was on that one, sitting on a coil of rope. Nobody had put him there and nobody put him off.

At the head of the wharf road an old man named Robin sat mending a cast net.

Uncle, is there a wilderness anywhere here that a seeker could stay in?

There is.

Show me how to get there.

The old man had taken to him at the first sight. He could have pointed. He laid down the net. Come on. I will carry you. They walked south, most of the length of the island. Along the way they passed five or six places that were thick with flowers and fruit, and at each one the boy asked what it was called, and the old man told him. The Orange Old-Field. Sister Pond. The Burnt Mill. Fig Yard. Buzzard Roost. At the last there was live-oak woods with the moss hanging, and behind the woods a hill of sand higher than any ground the boy had ever stood near.

Here is a good place. You stay here. What do they call you? The boy told him. Come to our street in the morning for your victuals. Then Robin went back to the south-end settlement, where some forty people were living on a place the owner had left, and called it out along the street. Wilderness Tite has come to stay at the sand hill. Cook for him.

So he had been Titus, and Give-It, and Blanket, and now Wilderness. That was four names, and he was not yet eight.

V. Two Sayings

Amelia Island · spring and summer

Early the next morning he went into the settlement with his pan. When the people put something in it and made their manners, he said, Be glad. Be free of trouble. He said nothing else. One man who had given could not make himself go back to his house. They all had to stand and look at him. So he was fed without any hardship.

The whole street got down in front of him. If you will stay by us these three months, we will build a praise house and keep it three nights in the week, and leave off what we ought to leave off. Promise us. He saw there was help for them in it, and he promised. After that he came in every morning and only for his victuals. They bowed, and he said the two sayings, Be glad. Be free of trouble, and went back to the sand hill. He kept that up the first month and the second. In the third month he came through.

When the cotton was laid by, Daddy Titus went to the Teacher. I am going down to see my seeker.

Go, Titus.

Titus started with his own people, and on the way he said to Bristol, who led at the next place, I am going to see Tite. I will go too, said Bristol, and brought his. Blind Cupid heard and came with his, and old Kit the barber with his, and so it went until every leader on St. Helena was on the water with a boatload behind him, forty boats.

Robin met them at the landing and bowed.

Uncle, is there a wilderness near here?

There is.

Is there a seeker staying in it?

There is.

What do they call him?

Wilderness Tite.

Then show us the way.

Who are you all?

I have come to see my seeker.

Robin looked them over and knew what they were by how they stood. Joy went all through him. Wait one minute. He ran back to the street. The leaders from Port Royal are here, every one, with their people, to see our boy. Fetch benches and quilts and lamps and oil, and come quick. The settlement took up what it had and fell in behind the leaders and went out to the sand hill. The boy knew them. He took the pans and bundles of the oldest and did for them what a seeker does for his elders.

While he was still showing them where to lie, it got dark. Titus said to the settlement people, Go home now. Night has caught you.

We thought we would hear preaching today. We are not going. We have not heard any yet.

Then light the lightwood and call the meeting. When that was done he said, Tite, your people say they want to hear. Speak to them.

The settlement people stood up all together. Daddy, our seeker doesn’t know a thing to say but two sayings. ‘Be glad. Be free of trouble.’ Let somebody else do the preaching.

Titus turned to the boy. Tite. How does a body come to be glad? How does a body get free of trouble? Tell us what is inside those two sayings.

Yes, Daddy.

He took a palmetto fan in his hand and stood up on a bench, and he spoke until the morning star, the way a storm rains over the whole coast at once and does not slack. He took the body apart piece from piece and the mind after it, and showed them every piece. That is how one that has come through is glad, he said. That is how one that has come through is free of trouble. The rest are not free of being born and dying and what lies between.

Well done, seeker, said Titus. You have it right. Now raise it. And the boy raised it.

At sunup the settlement was in two parties. One was angry. We never saw anybody so close. He could talk like that, and he ate from our pots three months, and two sayings is all he gave us. The other was glad. We are lucky people. We don’t know good from bad, and we fed one like him all summer and never knew it, and now we have heard him.

VI. The Seeker’s Pan

The settlement · that morning

Daddy Quaco, on St. Helena, looked over the world early that day as his habit was, and the people of that settlement came into his mind. Some of them are put out with the boy, he thought, and some are pleased. The ones who hold a thing against a child like my son will come to harm by it. I had better go. If I go, they will all be at peace with him.

The settlement had put up a brush arbor and cooked hominy and fish, and set out benches, and sat down to wait. The leaders came in from the sand hill at the hour for eating. They had asked the boy, Are you coming with us, or after? and he had said, When it is my time I will come. You all go ahead.

Quaco put on his coat at Sandy Field and took up his pan, and between one blink and the next he was standing at the head of the line of leaders as they came into the street. There was one shout. The Teacher is come! The whole place was stirred up. They found him a seat, and the leaders, and served them.

Before the meal was done the boy came in from the wilderness. The people got up and filled his pan with all respect. He took as much as he needed and carried it to Quaco and held it out.

Give it here, Tite. Quaco put out his hand and took the pan and showed it to Titus. Look, Titus. Here is your seeker’s pan.

Titus took it from his hands and gave it back to the boy. Go and sit where you always sit, and eat.

When everyone had eaten, the people asked the Teacher to give the thanks. He said: You are lucky people. On account of one child that came to your doors, you have seen Titus and Bristol and Cupid and Kit and all the rest. I came here my own self on his account and no other. It is your luck. It is your very good luck.

They thought, We were lucky to lay eyes on a child that teachers and leaders think so much of, and to put food in his pan. The ones who had been angry were pleased, and the pleased were more so, and a good many came through that day. Then Quaco got up and started back. The settlement walked a piece of the road with him, and turned home.

VII. The Sand Hill

The same day

He walked with the boy at his side, and he asked him what the places were called that they passed, and the boy told him. Buzzard Roost. Fig Yard. The Burnt Mill. Sister Pond. The Orange Old-Field. When they came to where the boy stayed, Quaco climbed the sand hill, and the boy with him. From the top of it you see the ocean.

Tite, when you stand up here and look this way and that, what do you see?

The big water, Daddy.

What comes to you when you look at it?

That in the times I have cried over my troubles, I must have cried more water than that. More than four of that.

Well said, Tite. It is so. Four oceans is the small water. What one body has cried is the big water. Why would anybody go on sleeping?

He had dug for sweet water all his life, and he stood a good while looking at the salt.

Where do you stay?

In the dug place under this hill.

What comes to you while you lie there?

That there is no end to the times I have died and been laid down in this ground.

Well said. It is so. There is no spot of ground where people have not lain down and died. And he told him one. There was a driver on a place I knew who could not stand to be put in the hands’ burying ground among field people. He said to his boy, When I go, lay me in clean ground, where nobody ever was laid. The boy said, Show me any such. He carried him up a little rise between three oaks. Here. Coming down, they met an old man who dug. Where have you two been? They told him. Let us see how clean it is, he said, and he walked them back up. Son, they have buried your father on this rise fourteen thousand times, and by that same name. You will not find a hand’s breadth anywhere on this earth that has not been somebody’s grave.

Then Daddy Quaco, who never raised a song, gave him one. He did not raise it. He said it over, flat, the way he would tell a man how deep he had gone.

I know moon-rise, I know star-rise,
  Lay dis body down.
I walk in de moonlight, I walk in de starlight,
  To lay dis body down.
I’ll walk in de graveyard, I’ll walk through de graveyard,
  To lay dis body down.
I’ll lie in de grave and stretch out my arms;
  Lay dis body down.

— “I Know Moon-Rise”

It is so for nearly everyone. The old people did tell of one who died where nobody had died before him. He was so old that the two banks of his river both claimed him, and each meant to have his bones. He had himself rowed out at slack water and died sitting up in the boat in the middle of the stream, so that neither side could say it had him, and both could.

Tite, said Quaco, when you hear the cats scream in these woods of a night, and the bull alligators, are you afraid?

No, Daddy. When I hear them, a love for the wilderness comes up in me. And he told over sixty things in it that he loved, the moss and the palmetto and the rest, one after another.

Tite.

Daddy?

I am going. Will you come with me, or turn back?

If my leader wants to carry me, I will go. If he wants me to turn back, I will turn back.

Quaco started down with the leaders. Now it was the boy’s own wish to turn back, and Titus knew it. Tite, turn back if you want to. The boy bowed to the Teacher and to all the leaders, and turned back into the woods. Quaco went home to St. Helena.

VIII. The Other Road

Sandy Field praise house · the fall of 1863

They talked about it in the praise house after meeting. It is a hard thing that child is doing. From the day he was born his people fed the elders sweet rice seven times over. When he went in the valley they fed us seven nights more. The eighth day he went out with the basket, and in two days he had a thousand pans and a thousand cloths. Another day he had a thousand blankets. There were people ready to carry him to Boston. All that came to him here, and he has left the whole of it and gone in the wilderness, and he lives on what is put in his pan.

Quaco came in. What are you all sitting here talking about? They told him.

Yes, he said. There is one road that goes to getting, and there is another road. A seeker that takes to the wilderness with his eye on what the wilderness will bring him has four bad doors standing open in front of him. The one that knows those two roads apart turns away from what they would heap on him, and goes off by himself, and works, and comes through.

Then he gave them the song for it, said over in the same flat way.

You may talk of my name as much as you please,
  And carry my name abroad,
But I really do believe I’m a child of God
  As I walk in de heavenly road.

— “The Heavenly Road”

The next morning he was down a well at Lands End, sending up sand a bucket at a time. They hauled him out at noon, and he asked for the gourd and tasted what was coming in at the bottom.

Background

The verse

“One is the road that leads to wealth, another the road that leads to Nirvana;” if the Bhikshu, the disciple of Buddha, has learnt this, he will not yearn for honour, he will strive after separation from the world.Dhammapada 75, tr. F. Max Müller (1881)

In the commentary, a poor Brahmin who has hidden from the elder Sāriputta for shame at having nothing to give at last gives him a whole bowl of porridge and a small cloth, and is reborn into a family of the elder’s supporters. As a baby he drops a costly blanket at Sāriputta’s feet and is named Tissa after him. At seven he becomes a novice. The town gives him a thousand bowls and cloths in two days, and another day he leads a thousand cold monks out and collects a thousand blankets; he keeps none of it. To get away from his visiting relatives he walks twenty leagues to a forest, where a village feeds him and hears nothing from him but “May you be happy, may you be released from suffering.” In three months he is an arahat. When Sāriputta and the great disciples come to see him, the villagers are astonished, and some are offended, to hear him preach all night; the Buddha arrives to reconcile them, questions the boy on a mountain above the ocean, and lets him turn back to the forest. Port Royal, 1861 to 1863

On November 7, 1861, a Union fleet took Port Royal Sound. The planters of the South Carolina sea islands fled, and about ten thousand enslaved people stayed where they were, on land that grew the finest long-staple cotton in the world; the islands remember it as the day of the big gun-shoot. What followed is called the Port Royal Experiment: Northern teachers and superintendents, wages, schools, and the first Black regiment raised from former slaves, the First South Carolina Volunteers under Thomas Wentworth Higginson, who heard the Emancipation Proclamation read at Camp Saxton on January 1, 1863, and went down the coast by steamer later that month, one transport putting in at Union-held Fernandina on Amelia Island. The superintendents’ Letters from Port Royal record that a slave household’s allowance had been cloth for two suits a year and “a pair of blankets every few years,” and that when the Government gave up Edisto Island in 1862 a thousand of its people were quartered at St. Helena Village, where the next winter they still had “nothing.” Sandy Field, the south-end settlement, the sand hill and all the people in the story are invented.

Every plantation on St. Helena had its praise house, where the Gullah people held meeting and the ring shout, with the best singers standing at the wall to “base” the shouters. Before anyone could be a member, young or old, they had to seek: to go alone into the woods or the swamp to pray, under an older guide, until they had found what they were hunting for. Higginson wrote that “de lonesome valley” was the islanders’ word for it, and that a girl who had entered it “bound a handkerchief by a peculiar knot over her head”; the editors of Slave Songs of the United States (1867) quote a boy who could not join the shout yet because he “hain’t been on my knees in de swamp.” The three songs are given as Higginson took them down from his soldiers at Port Royal in 1862 and 1863 and printed them, in the spelling he used, in the Atlantic Monthly in 1867 and in Army Life in a Black Regiment (1870).
