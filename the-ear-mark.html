Walking the American WayCommentary 22.6 · Issāpakata-itthi

The Ear-Mark

Who seeth us? and who knoweth us?
— Isaiah 29:15

Zo heb ik het horen zeggen, on the Esopus flats.

I. The Mark Book

Marbletown, Ulster County, New York · 1826

Meester Wessel Brink had been voorlezer of the stone church at the Steenkill for forty-one years. He had kept the school beside it for most of those, and for thirty he had kept the town’s books as well, because he wrote the clearest hand between Hurley and the mountains. One of the books was the mark book. Every farmer on the flats turned his sheep onto the commons and his hogs into the oak woods, and a man knew his own in the fall by the cut of their ears, and the cut was the Meester’s to write down. Petrus Oosterhoudt, a halfpenny under each ear. Widow Roosa, a swallow fork in the right. Jacobus Krom, his mark: a crop off the left ear and a slit in the right. Recorded this 2d day of April 1798. For the first pages he had drawn a small sheep’s head beside each entry, with the ears done carefully. After that the town grew and he left off drawing.

He was seventy-nine now. The consistory called him the clerk, and the town had voted in a younger man for its books, and the dominie rode over from Rochester three Sundays in five and preached in English. But on the Sundays between, the old people still came, and the Meester stood at his desk under the pulpit and read them a chapter out of the States Bible with the brass clasps, in the Dutch it was printed in, and lined out a psalm, and they sang it slowly and went home. He had spent his life saying other people’s words aloud, and it had left him with very few of his own. Children who had been his scholars and were grandfathers now could not remember him saying ten sentences together.

Jacobus Krom had a hundred and forty acres of the flat land along the creek, in wheat and flax and Indian corn, and the stone house his grandfather built, with the kitchen in the cellar and the best room, the opkamer, raised four steps above the rest. His wife was Catrina, an Oosterhoudt from Hurley, whom everybody called Trijntje. They had been married twenty-six years. There were three small stones in the churchyard with the name Krom on them and no children in the house.

Trijntje Krom was a woman who counted. She counted the eggs and the candles, and the spoons on Saturday night. She knew to a skein what lay in the linen kas, and she knew it again the next week. It was called good housekeeping and it was. But there was something under it that was not housekeeping. She could not bear for a thing that was hers to be handled by anyone else.

There were two others in the house. Old Kees drove the team and slept in the barn loft in all weathers because of the horses. He had been bought at a vendue in Kingston in 1789 and was a slave for life, or until the Fourth of July next year, when the law said every one of them still held in the State of New York should go free. People on the flats called that day the Freedom, the way they would say the thaw.

And there was Dina. She had been born in the cellar kitchen in March of 1802, to a woman named Bet who belonged to Jacobus Krom, and the law made a difference of that. A child born after the Fourth of July, 1799, was born free, and owed her mother’s master her work until she was five-and-twenty. The master had to carry the birth to the town clerk within nine months, and Jacobus had, and the Meester had written it in a book that was not the mark book. Dina, a female child, born the 9th of March 1802 of Bet, the slave of Jacobus Krom. So Dina was a servant, in the law’s English, and in the Kroms’ Dutch she was what her mother had been. Bet had died in the winter of 1819 and was buried at the wood’s edge beyond the churchyard wall, where they put them.

Dina knew her date. She had ten months left. She did the washing and the milking and the cellar hearth, and she sold eggs at the door to the Irishmen who had come that year to dig the canal along the Rondout, and she kept her English for them and her Dutch for everything that mattered. She had a face that people looked at. The canal men looked, and the miller, and the young dominie, once, and then at his boots.

II. The Cellar Stairs

Winter and spring, 1826

Jacobus began coming down the cellar stairs after New Year, when his wife was asleep.

Dina slept on a straw tick by the cellar hearth. There was no door between her and the stairs and nobody within call. Nobody had ever asked Dina whether she was willing about anything, and Jacobus did not begin then. He did not think of himself as a hard man. He brought her a paper of brown sugar once from Kingston, and a ribbon, which she put in the fire.

By April Trijntje knew. There was the fourth stair, which spoke. There was tallow on the cuff of his nightshirt, and they burned no tallow upstairs. One night she got up after him and stood at the head of the stairs in the dark with her hand on the wall, and listened as long as she could stand to, and went back to bed and lay with her eyes open until the window turned grey.

She said nothing to him. She had been angry with Jacobus Krom for twenty-six years over smaller matters, and she knew the size of that anger and what it could not move. This was something else, and it wanted a place to go. It went down the stairs.

She watched Dina carry the milk. She watched her bend to the fire. She watched the canal men at the door with their hats in their hands, and it seemed to her, by May, that the whole of the trouble was in that face, that it had been set in her kitchen like a lamp in a window to bring men in off the road. She did not once think of the stairs as something Jacobus had walked down. She thought of them as something Dina had drawn him down. This is how it is told, and nobody on the flats has ever said it was sense.

The Saturday before Pinkster, Jacobus and Kees took the wagon to the landing at Kingston with the last of the winter flour, and would not be back before night. It was shearing week. The shears lay on the bench in the cellar kitchen with the little hooked knife beside them, because the lambs are marked when the ewes are shorn. Trijntje had held lambs for the marking every spring since she was a bride. She knew how it was done.

She called Dina up to the opkamer to help her wind yarn.

Hold out your hands.

Dina held them out, a foot apart, palms in, as she had a thousand times, for the skein to be laid over them. Trijntje laid on the skein, and then took a turn of it around both wrists, and another, and drew it tight, and knotted it. It was linen, her own spinning. It would have held a calf. Dina looked at her hands and then at her mistress, and had only begun to understand when Trijntje pushed her down against the foot of the bed and bound her ankles with the rest.

She did it the way it is done to a lamb, kneeling on her, quickly, with the left ear first. A crop off the left and a slit in the right. Then, because it was the face she wanted, she set the point of the knife in the nostril and laid it open to the bone.

Dina did not scream at first, because she did not believe it. Then she did. There was nobody on the flats to hear her but the shorn ewes in the fold, who lifted their heads.

Under the opkamer, reached from the cellar kitchen by a low plank door, was the root cellar, where the turnips and cabbages wintered. In May it was empty. Trijntje dragged her down the stairs and across the bricks and put her in, and shut the door, and rolled the vinegar barrel in front of it.

Then she scrubbed the opkamer floor with sand. She scalded the knife. She burned the ends of the yarn and her own apron. When it was all done she stood in her clean kitchen with her hands hanging and saw that she had not thought past this hour. The girl had run off to the canal with an Irishman. That would do for a day. She had not thought as far as a week.

III. Pinkster

Whitsunday, the 14th of May

Jacobus came home after midnight and went straight up. In the morning there was no fire in the cellar and no milk on the table.

She is gone, Trijntje said. Her bed was not slept in. To the canal, I expect. They have been at the door all spring.

Jacobus stood with one boot on. Something went across his face, and she saw that part of it was relief, and hated him freshly for it.

With ten months left, he said. It makes no sense.

When did that kind ever make sense? And then, with her back to him, taking her black silk out of the kas: Kom, man. It is Pinkster. Let us go and hear the Meester read.

He stared at her. It was an off Sunday, with no dominie. She had not sat through the Dutch reading in ten years. She said the old man’s voice put her to sleep and the psalms ran so slow you could knit a stocking between the lines.

It is Pinkster, she said again. People will look for us.

That was the whole of her reason, and she had found it in the night. Whatever was discovered, and whenever, the entire church should be able to say where Trijntje Krom was on Pinkster morning. She was in her pew with her psalm book, among them, where she belonged.

Kees drove. It was a soft bright day. Along the wood’s edge the wild azalea was out, the one they called the Pinkster flower because it kept that date, pink on bare grey stems before its own leaves came. Pinkster was the holiday the Dutch gave their Black people, three days of it, and already there were men and women on the road in their best, walking toward the green by Sleght’s tavern where there would be a fiddle by noon. They stood aside for the wagon. Trijntje looked at each face as it went by.

The church was more than half full, which for the Dutch reading was a crowd. The Kroms had the fourth pew. Kees went up the stair to the gallery, where the people of his color sat on benches with no backs. Meester Brink stood at his desk under the pulpit in his rusty coat, opened the clasps, and read them the second chapter of the Acts, the rushing wind and the tongues of fire, in a voice like a dry board.

And Trijntje listened. She had never in her life listened so hard to anything. She sat upright in the black silk with her hands folded on her book, and every word came to her small and clear, as if through a keyhole. Every man heard them speak in his own language. She heard that. She thought of the plank door and the barrel, and of how much noise a person could make with her hands and feet tied, and she heard that too.

IV. The Visit

The Krom farm · the same morning

Petrus Oosterhoudt was Trijntje’s brother, and his wife Lena had fried olykoeks on Saturday, a whole crock of them, and said it was a shame to eat them alone on a holiday with a sister three miles off. They drove over from Hurley after an early breakfast, meaning to surprise her. They found the door on the latch, the way every door on the flats was, and nobody at home.

Gone to church, said Petrus. On an off Sunday. My sister.

Lena went down to the cellar kitchen to blow up the fire for coffee. The hearth was cold. She was on her knees with the bellows when she heard it, a slow knocking, like a shutter loose in a small wind, except that there was no wind. It came from behind the vinegar barrel.

It took the two of them to roll the barrel. Petrus opened the plank door and the light went in.

Lena had known Dina since the week she was born. She had held her at Bet’s burying. She did not know her now for the space of a breath. Then she did, and she made a sound and got down on the bricks and began to pick at the knots, and Petrus cut them with his clasp knife, and they brought her out to the hearth.

Petrus was a farmer. He looked once at the ears and knew what he was looking at, the way he would have known it across a pasture. He said his sister’s name, quietly, and went up the stairs, and Lena heard him walking in the yard.

She washed the face with warm water as well as she could and tied it round with a clean linen towel from the kas, under the chin and over the crown, the way they tie up the jaw of the dead. She was crying, and talking the whole time. Lie down, child. Lie here. They will come home at noon and Petrus will speak to her. He will speak to Jacobus. This will be made right. It is a thing for the family. Nobody needs to know.

Dina drank a dipper of water, and another. Her wrists were black where the linen had been.

Where are they?

At church. They must be.

Dina got up. She stood a moment with her hand on the chimney shelf until the room was steady. Then she went up the cellar stairs, past the fourth one, and out at the kitchen door.

You cannot go like that, Lena said behind her. Dina. On Pinkster. Everyone will see.

It was three miles by the King’s road. She walked it in the middle of the track, in the dress she had been cut in, with the towel round her head. The Pinkster flower was out along the wood. The holiday people going down to the green met her and stopped, and stood aside as they had for the wagon. They looked at the towel, and the brown stain coming through it at both sides, and at her bare feet. A woman from the Roosa place asked her a question and got no answer. When Dina had gone by, the woman turned around and walked after her. Then a man did. By the time she passed the tavern there were eleven or twelve behind her, in their good clothes, and the fiddler stood in Sleght’s doorway with the bow in his hand and did not play. Last of all, at a walk, came the Oosterhoudts’ wagon. Petrus did not dare to pass her and could not make himself turn back.

V. The Aisle

The stone church · toward noon

The Meester had finished the chapter and was finding the psalm when the door opened at the back and the light came up the aisle.

She did not take the gallery stair. She came up the middle of the church, past the pews with the names on the doors, as far as the open place under the pulpit where they set the table on communion Sundays, and there she turned around. The men were on her right hand and the women on her left. The elders and deacons sat in their bench along the wall. Over all of them was the gallery, where old Kees had got to his feet. The ones who had followed her stood in the door. So that she had the whole of that country in front of her, as it was sorted.

She undid the towel. It stuck, and she pulled it free.

Nobody in the stone church made any sound at all. She spoke in Dutch, and not loudly. The old people said afterward that they had heard every word in the last pew.

I am Dina. I was born of Bet in Jacobus Krom’s cellar and I owe him ten months. This winter the baas came down the stairs to me at night. I did not ask him to. Yesterday the juffrouw tied my hands with her yarn and did this with the lamb knife, and put me in the root cellar behind the vinegar. Then she came here.

That was all. She stood with the towel in her hand.

The Meester had come around his desk. He was a small man and had to look up at her. He looked at the left ear, and then at the right, for some time, the way he would look at a page.

A crop off the left, he said, and a slit in the right.

He did not say whose. Half the men in the church had driven Krom sheep out of their corn. They turned and looked at the fourth pew.

The Meester went back behind his desk. People thought he would say something about the law, or send a boy for the justice. He stood with both hands on the Bible and looked at Trijntje Krom over the top of it, and when he spoke it was in his school voice, without heat.

You thought, nobody knows this that I have done. And you came and sat down here. Trijntje, if nobody had known it, to the end of your life, if she had died behind the barrel and you had buried her at night, it would still have been better never done. A thing like this does not stay where it is put. It burns afterward. I have seen it burn fifty years in people who were never found out.

Then he turned the heavy pages back from the Acts until he had the place, and read it to all of them.

Wee dengenen, die zich diep versteken willen voor den HEERE, hun raad verbergende; en welker werken in duisterheid geschieden, en zij zeggen: Wie ziet ons, en wie kent ons?

Woe unto them that seek deep to hide their counsel from the LORD, and their works are in the dark, and they say, Who seeth us? and who knoweth us?

— Jesaja (Isaiah) 29:15

He turned again, forward this time, and did not have to hunt for it.

Die zijn overtredingen bedekt, zal niet voorspoedig zijn; maar die ze bekent en laat, zal barmhartigheid verkrijgen.

He that covereth his sins shall not prosper: but whoso confesseth and forsaketh them shall have mercy.

— Spreuken (Proverbs) 28:13

Jacobus Krom stood up on the men’s side. He was grey in the face. He held on to the pew door.

What she says of me is so, he said. I went down. She never asked it.

Trijntje had not moved. She sat with the psalm book shut in her lap and looked at what was standing under the pulpit. In the opkamer she had seen only a face that men looked at. She had cut at that. Now, in the plain light of the church windows, with the whole flats on either side of her, she saw a girl of twenty-four with her ears cut like a ewe’s, holding a bloody towel. She saw that she had made it with her own hands, on her own bedroom floor, and that no scrubbing with sand would ever make it not so. She looked for the anger that had carried her and could not find any of it. There was only the thing done, and herself, who had done it.

She stood. She had to try twice before the words would come.

It is so. All of it. And then, to the old man, in a voice nobody there had heard from her: Meester, what can I do?

You cannot put it back, he said. That is what I have been telling you. He opened the drawer of his desk and took out the ink bottle and a pen, and laid a clean sheet on the closed Bible. But there is a thing left that is better done than not done. You know what the law lets you do. The overseers are sitting there.

They were. Dirck Schoonmaker and Abram Keator, overseers of the poor for the town of Marbletown, were in the elders’ bench in their Sunday black, and a justice of the peace was in the third pew on the men’s side with his mouth open.

The Meester wrote it in English, because the law was English now. He wrote it standing, and it took him some time, and nobody left. Know all men by these presents that I, Jacobus Krom of the Town of Marbletown in the County of Ulster, do hereby release, set free and discharge Dina, born of my woman Bet, from all and every kind of service to me, my heirs, executors and assigns, from this day. Under it the two overseers certified that they had examined the said Dina and found her under the age of fifty years and of sufficient ability to provide for herself, which was the form. Abram Keator’s hand shook so that he blotted it.

Jacobus signed. Trijntje came out of the pew and up the aisle and asked for the pen. The law did not want a wife’s name and the Meester gave her the pen anyway. She signed under her husband, Catrina Krom, and then she turned to Dina, who was a head taller, and lifted her face and looked at the ears and the nose, at each of them, as she had not let herself look in the opkamer. She did not ask to be forgiven. She was a woman who counted, and she had seen what the count was.

The Meester sanded the sheet and shook it and held it out. Dina could not read it. She folded it in the towel.

VI. Whole Ears

Marbletown · 1826 and after

She did not go to the canal. She went that night to Lena Oosterhoudt’s at Hurley, in the wagon, with the crock of olykoeks between her feet, and stayed until the cuts closed, and after that she worked for wages. She had four York shillings a week and her board from a widow at Stone Ridge, and she could leave on any day she chose, and she tried it once, to see, and came back on the Monday.

In September she stood up in the stone church at the communion season and was received on her confession, and the Meester wrote her into the church book. He asked what second name he should put. She had never had one. She said Bet’s. So he wrote it the old way, as a child is written after a father: Dina Bets, a free woman. It was the third time he had written her down.

On the off Sundays that winter, after the reading, when the old people had gone home, she stayed. He taught her letters out of the States Bible. He began with her own name and went on from there, a line a week, his finger under the words, saying very little. She sat in the gallery no longer. She sat on the front bench on the women’s side, with her cap pushed back off her ears, and no one ever asked her to pull it down. When the Meester died, in the hard winter of 1831, the consistory could find no man who still read Dutch well enough to stand at the desk. For two years after that, on the Sundays between, it was Dina Bets who opened the brass clasps and read the chapter to the last of the old people. They said she read it better than the dominie read English, and slower.

Old Kees went free on the Fourth of July, 1827, with all the rest, and walked to Kingston the same day to find a daughter.

Trijntje Krom never again missed the Dutch reading. She sat in the fourth pew every off Sunday for the nineteen years she had left, three benches behind the pushed-back cap, and people noticed that she listened as if through a keyhole. She still counted. But she was seen at the churchyard wall, more than once, on the far side, at the wood’s edge, where Bet was. In May she brought the Pinkster flower there, and she never said to anyone why. Jacobus did not go down the cellar stairs again for any reason. He had a door cut from the yard and went in that way.

•••

The week after Pinkster, Jacobus Krom came to the Meester’s house beside the school and asked for the old mark book. It was in the new clerk’s keeping by then, and the new clerk said a mark could be sold or left to a son but he knew of no way to unwrite one. The Meester sent a boy for the book all the same. He found the page from 1798, with the little sheep’s head he had drawn when he was young, the left ear cropped and the right one slit. He drew his pen through the entry once, and wrote the date in the margin, and blotted it.

The Krom lambs were not cut that spring, nor any spring after. They ran on the commons with the others, and in the fall, when the flocks were sorted, the men of Marbletown went through them ear by ear, the halfpennies and the swallow forks and the crops, and set aside what was left. Nobody was ever in doubt. A Krom sheep was the one with whole ears.

Background

The verse

An evil deed is better left undone, for a man repents of it afterwards; a good deed is better done, for having done it, one does not repent.Dhammapada 314, tr. F. Max Müller (1881)

The commentary story is one of the shortest in the collection. A woman of Sāvatthi learns that her husband has lain with a servant of the house. She binds the servant hand and foot, cuts off her nose and ears, shuts her in a hidden room, and then, to cover what she has done, takes her husband to the monastery to hear the teaching. Relatives come visiting, open the door and release the servant, who walks to the monastery and tells the Buddha what happened in front of the whole assembly. He says that no one should do even a small wrong thinking that others will not know, since a hidden deed still burns afterward, and speaks the verse. Husband and wife are changed by it, free the servant on the spot, and she becomes a follower. Read the full commentary story.

Ulster County, 1826

Slavery in New York ended slowly. A law of 1799 freed no one then living; it declared children born to enslaved mothers after July 4 of that year free, but bound them to serve the mother’s master until twenty-five if female and twenty-eight if male, and required each birth to be reported to the town clerk. A second law in 1817 set July 4, 1827, as the day everyone born before 1799 would go free. Until then a master could manumit by a written paper, with the town’s overseers of the poor certifying that the person was under fifty and able to support herself; the wording in the story follows surviving certificates. More on slavery in New York. Ulster County’s farm country was among the last strongholds. Its slaveholders were largely “that class of people called Low Dutch,” in the words of Sojourner Truth, who was born there about 1797, grew up speaking only Dutch, slept as a child in a slaveholder’s cellar, and walked away from her last master in 1826. Pinkster, the Dutch Whitsuntide, was the great holiday of enslaved New Yorkers in the Hudson Valley, several days free of work. In 1826 it fell on May 14.

The voorlezer was the lay reader of a Dutch Reformed congregation, usually also its schoolmaster and often the keeper of the village records; when no minister was present he read the scripture and led the psalms. The title was fading by 1800 and Dutch preaching with it (New Paltz, nearby, went over wholly to English in 1817), though the language lingered in Hudson Valley kitchens for generations. The Dutch readings in the story, the church at the Steenkill and all its people are invented. The earmarks are not: New York towns kept registers of the cuts by which each farmer knew his sheep and hogs, entered in just such words as “a crop on the left ear and a slit in the same.” The scripture is the Dutch States Bible (Statenvertaling) of 1637, with the King James Version beneath it.
