Four Hundredweight

…here it is the soul itself that is tearing itself in pieces.
— St. Teresa of Jesus, Life, ch. 32

Thus it was told me, at the landing on Nanjemoy Creek.

I. The Landing

St. Thomas's, Charles County, Maryland · November 1717

The Jesuits' house stood on the high ground where Port Tobacco River runs into the Potomac, and the path down to their landing was steep enough that a man carrying oars took it sideways. Peter Cole took it sideways every morning of his life. He was about forty. He belonged to the Jesuits, as the boat did and the cattle did, and he had rowed their priests up and down the rivers of Charles County since he was fourteen, to every house on the Neck where there was a room with an altar in it. He could find a creek mouth in the dark by the smell of it. The men who worked with him said he saw further than other people, and not only on the water.

Matthew Spurling carried the other pair of oars. He was seventeen and had come out of Bristol in the spring, bound for five years to pay his passage, and the Jesuits had bought his time off the ship at Annapolis. He was a Protestant and had been told he would not be made anything else, and nobody had tried. He was still afraid of the river.

It was the grey before sunrise, and the frost was on the path. Father Langworth was to be rowed down to Nanjemoy to say Mass in a house there and come back by dark. Halfway down the hill, where the trees open and the whole river lies below, Peter stopped. Matthew nearly walked into him. Peter stood with the oars on his shoulder and looked down the Port Tobacco to the Potomac and along the Potomac as far as it went, toward the Virginia shore, slowly, the whole length of it. Then he smiled.

Matthew had not seen him smile in seven months.

What is it?

Not here, Peter said. Ask me tonight in the shop, in front of Brother Hollis.

II. The Shop

That evening

Brother John Hollis was a Jesuit who would never be a priest. He had come out from England in 1693 as a lay brother, which meant he took the vows and kept them and was not ordained, and he did the work. He was a joiner. He had made most of the altars on the Neck, and all of the boxes they folded into, so that a priest could close up a chapel in a minute and leave a room that looked like a parlor. He was nearly seventy. He talked less than anyone at St. Thomas's, which was saying something, and men went to his shop in the evenings with things they would not take to a priest.

When the boat was in and Father Langworth had gone up to the house, Peter and Matthew came to the shop. Brother Hollis was planing a cherry board by a rushlight. Matthew asked. Peter sat on the sawhorse.

When we came down this morning, Peter said, there was a great snake lying in the river. His head was at the point under the house, and his body went down the Potomac past Cedar Point, farther than I could see. Thicker than a hogshead. And he was all on fire. The fire started at his head and ran to the end of him and started at the end and ran back up, and more fire came out of his sides and fell on his back. The river was not on fire, only him. I smiled because I have heard that there are such things, and I had never seen one.

Matthew looked from one to the other. Peter was not a man who joked, and neither was the brother.

Brother Hollis set down the plane.

You have eyes, Peter, he said, and you use them. He brushed the shavings off the board with the side of his hand. I have seen him too. The morning I took my last vows, in August, at the five o'clock Mass, I came out of the chapel and he was lying in the river just as you say. I have seen him most mornings since. I did not speak of it. Who would believe it of an old joiner? And the ones who did not believe it would have laughed at it, and it is not good for a man to laugh at a thing like that. I kept quiet for their sake. Now there are two of us.

He looked at Matthew.

You knew him, Peter. He used to sell you fish. It is Kit Lowry.

III. Four Hundredweight

Mr. Thorogood's landing, Nanjemoy · 1715

Mr. Basil Thorogood had three thousand acres on Nanjemoy Creek, and forty people who were his, and a brick house with glazed windows that he had built in the year of the Revolution, when the Protestants took the government and his family had to give up their places in it. He had been a justice of the county then. He was not one now, and would not be again, and he did not seem to mind. He was a short, loud, happy man who could not sit still at a table. He fed everybody. On a Sunday when a priest came, forty and fifty people came up from the creeks to hear Mass at his house and stayed to dinner, Catholics and Protestants both, and the Protestants came back the next time for the dinner.

Since the law of 1704 a priest could not say Mass in any public chapel in Maryland, only in the house of a private family. So the Catholic families built rooms onto their houses. Mr. Thorogood's was the best on the Neck. Brother Hollis had built it for him: a long room off the west end of the house, with its own door to the yard, a plastered ceiling, and an altar of black walnut that folded shut into a press. There was a little window over the altar of colored glass that Mr. Thorogood had sent to London for, and when the sun came through it in the morning it laid red and blue on the floor. The house people swept that room before they swept any other. Mr. Thorogood went into it every morning, priest or no priest, and knelt on the bare floor a quarter of an hour, and came out pleased with the whole world. Of all he owned, it was the thing he loved.

That was the year the new Lord Baltimore swore he was a Protestant and got Maryland back for his family, and every Catholic on the Neck understood that this changed nothing for them. It was also the year the Assembly passed the new law on servants. A runaway servant was to serve up to ten days for every day he had been away, and whoever took him up and brought him before a magistrate was to have four hundred pounds of tobacco from the master.

On a morning in October Mr. Thorogood walked down to his landing before sunrise to meet the boat that was bringing the priest. He had his man Harry with him to carry the lantern. In the tobacco house by the landing, among the hanging leaf, there was a man asleep in the straw, with his legs muddy to the knee and his coat pulled over his head.

Mr. Thorogood was in a good humor. He pointed with his stick.

Look at that, Harry. Somebody's runaway. There's four hundredweight of tobacco lying in my straw.

The man in the straw pulled the coat down off his face and looked at him.

I'll be even with you, he said.

Mr. Thorogood laughed and went down to meet the boat. By the time the priest was ashore the man was gone, and by the time Mass was said Mr. Thorogood had forgotten him.

IV. Seven Times

Nanjemoy · 1715–1716

The man was Kit Lowry. He was not a runaway. He had served out seven years in Prince George's County as a boy and had his freedom dues, a hat and a suit of clothes, two hoes and an axe, and nothing else. He had married a woman who had also served her time, and they had two children and a cabin on rented ground at the head of the creek. He fished. He took a hog out of the woods now and then that was not his, and he had been out after one that night and had lain down in the nearest dry place, and he had been priced in his sleep like a calf.

Ten days later Mr. Thorogood's tobacco house by the landing burned with the whole crop hanging in it.

On the Neck they say it was seven times. Seven times he burned the tobacco, and seven times he went into the woods where Mr. Thorogood's cattle ranged and cut the hamstrings of a steer, and seven times he set fire to the house. Brother Hollis said that seven was a good number for a story, and that he himself had counted three tobacco houses, the kitchen, the dairy, the barn with the corn in it, and eleven head of cattle lying in the woods with their legs cut, which the house people had to finish with an axe. Mr. Thorogood did not know who did it. He did not think of the man in the straw. He rebuilt each thing as it went, and when people came to Mass he fed them as before, and he did not complain, and he did not lose his good humor, and that was the worst of it for Kit Lowry. A man can be revenged on a man who is unhappy.

So Kit went to the landing on a Saturday evening when Harry was down there alone, and he gave him a piece of fish and some of a bottle, and asked him what his master thought most of in the world.

The mass room, said Harry. It isn't close. He thinks more of that room than he does of his wife.

In January the priest went upriver for a fortnight, and Kit Lowry broke the glass cruets for the wine and water, and the candlesticks, and the colored window, and he poured lamp oil on the walnut and set the mass room alight.

Mr. Thorogood was down at the landing when they shouted. He ran. By the time he got around the end of the house the room was gone. The roof had come in on the altar. The brick of the house wall stood black, with the shape of the room printed on it in soot.

He stood and looked at it. The people who had run up from the quarter and the kitchen stood around him and waited for him to fall down. And Mr. Thorogood crooked his left arm against his chest and struck the inside of the elbow with the flat of his right hand, hard, as loud as a pistol, three times, the way a man applauds a horse that has won.

Master, said Harry. Master, that was your room.

I know it, Mr. Thorogood said. I have had all these years to put what God gave me into things for Him, and every time it burns I get to do it again. I am clapping because I am going to build that room twice.

V. The Octave

Nanjemoy · Easter 1717

Brother Hollis built it twice. It took him and two men of the house from February to Lent. The walnut was harder to find the second time and the glass came from London again at twice the price, and Mr. Thorogood paid it. At Easter the priest blessed the new room, and Mr. Thorogood kept the octave. For eight days Mass was said in the room every morning and the whole Neck ate at his house every noon: shad from the creek, and hams, and hominy, and the first greens. People camped in the yard. Protestants came too, for the dinner, and stood at the back of the mass room in their hats out of curiosity.

Kit Lowry came every day. He had a butcher's knife pushed down inside his breeches against his hip. He had understood by now that nothing he could burn would touch the man, and that only one thing was left. For seven days he stood in the yard and in the back of the room, and ate at the long tables, and waited, and Mr. Thorogood was never alone for a breath. People had their hands on him from morning to night.

On the eighth morning, before the priest began, Mr. Thorogood stood up in front of the altar with his hat in his hands.

Father, he said, you know I have had some losses. Seven times a man has burned my tobacco, and seven times he has hamstrung my cattle, and seven times he has burned my house, and I am sure it was the same man who burned this room. I do not know his name. I ask you to offer this Mass for him. Whatever good there is in this room and in this week, I want him to have the first of it.

The priest looked at him a while and then bowed his head, and said the Mass for that intention. Brother Hollis was kneeling at the side wall, and he said that in all his years he never heard a room so quiet.

Kit Lowry stood at the back with the knife against his hip. He thought, I have done all that to him, and he gives me the first of his Mass. He is a bigger man than I am by the whole of it. And he thought too, being Kit, that if a man so forgiving ever found him out and he had not asked first, the sheriff would not be so forgiving. When the Mass was over he went up the room through the people and knelt down on the new boards in front of Mr. Thorogood.

Pardon me, master.

For what, friend?

All of it. I did it all.

Mr. Thorogood did not raise his voice. He asked him about each thing, one by one, in front of everyone. The tobacco house at the landing? The steers in the Long Swamp? The kitchen? The room? And Kit said yes to every one.

But I never saw you in my life, Mr. Thorogood said. What did I do to you?

You priced me, Kit said. In your tobacco house, at the landing. You said there was four hundredweight lying in your straw.

Mr. Thorogood remembered it then. Everyone in the room saw him remember it. His face went red to the hair, and he knelt down on the boards himself, so that they were two men on their knees in front of the altar.

I did say that, he said. I said it for a joke and it was a cruel thing to say. Pardon me for it. And then: Get up, friend. I pardon you. Go your way.

Kit did not get up. If you pardon me, let me serve you. Me and my wife and my two children. Bind us to you. We'll work out the cost of what I burned if it takes my whole life.

Mr. Thorogood shook his head.

Friend, you did all that over one word of mine. If you were in my house, we would neither of us ever be easy. I have no need of you in my house. I pardon you freely, and there is nothing to work off. Go your way.

Kit went his way. In July he took his canoe out on the Potomac after rock with a storm coming up from Virginia, and he did not come back. They found the canoe at Cedar Point. They never found him.

VI. The Fire Inside

The shop at St. Thomas's · November 1717

The rushlight had burned down to the clip. Brother Hollis lit another from it.

He was forgiven, said Matthew. Mr. Thorogood forgave him. The priest said a Mass for him.

He was, said the brother. And he did all of that. Both things. He took down a small worn book from the shelf over the bench, the life of the holy mother Teresa that Father Langworth had brought him from England, and found the place without looking long. Teresa, who saw the place made ready for her own sins, and was let out again.

If I said that the soul is continually being torn from the body, it would be nothing, for that implies the destruction of life by the hands of another but here it is the soul itself that is tearing itself in pieces. I cannot describe that inward fire or that despair, surpassing all torments and all pain. I did not see who it was that tormented me, but I felt myself on fire, and torn to pieces, as it seemed to me; and, I repeat it, this inward fire and despair are the greatest torments of all.

— St. Teresa of Jesus, Life, ch. 32

Nobody is burning Kit Lowry, Brother Hollis said. Mr. Thorogood is not. God is not. Kit did not know what he was doing while he did it. He knows now. It is his own fire.

Peter had not said anything since he told what he saw. He was looking at the board on the bench, which was for a new altar press for a house up the Wicomico, cherry this time, because walnut was getting dear.

He went his way, Peter said.

Matthew looked at him, and then at the brother, and nobody said anything about the forty people at Nanjemoy, or the boat, or the oars.

•••

On Sunday they rowed Father Langworth down to Nanjemoy. Matthew was in the bow. Mr. Thorogood was at the landing in the cold with his stick, as loud as ever, shouting to them about the tide before they were in hearing. Up at the house the people were already going in at the side door, and the morning sun was low enough to come through the little colored window over the altar and lay red and blue across the new boards, the place where two men had knelt.

The new tobacco house stood by the landing, full of leaf. Its door had no lock on it. Matthew asked Harry why, while they were making the boat fast.

Master won't have one, said Harry. He says a man who wants to sleep in it can sleep in it.

Background

The verse

A fool does not know when he commits his evil deeds: but the wicked man burns by his own deeds, as if burnt by fire.Dhammapada 136, tr. F. Max Müller (1881)

In the commentary, the elder Mahā Moggallāna, coming down from Vulture’s Peak with the elder Lakkhaṇa, smiles at something he will explain only before the Buddha: a peta in the shape of a python, many leagues long, with fire running from its head to its tail and back. The Buddha says his disciples have eyes and use them, and that he saw the same being on the night of his awakening but kept silent for the sake of those who would not believe. Then he tells its past. In the time of an earlier Buddha, a generous rich man named Sumaṅgala, walking out at dawn, saw a mud-footed man hiding in a rest-house by the city gate and remarked that he must be a night prowler. The man swore to get even, and burned the rich man’s field, maimed his cattle and burned his house, seven times each; then, learning from a servant what his master loved most, he burned the Perfumed Chamber the rich man had built for the Buddha. The rich man clapped for joy at the chance to build it again. When the thief came for seven days with a knife to kill him, the rich man dedicated the merit of his gifts to the unknown arsonist; the thief fell at his feet, the rich man asked his pardon in turn for the careless word, forgave him, and would not take him into his house as a servant. The thief died with the deed unspent. Read the full commentary story.

Charles County, Maryland, 1715–1717

Maryland was founded as a refuge for English Catholics, but after the Protestant revolution of 1689 the Church of England was established and the old Catholic families were pushed out of office. A 1704 act “to prevent the growth of Popery” forbade priests to exercise their office; Queen Anne then allowed Mass to be said in the chapels of private families, and so Catholic planters built “mass rooms” and “priests’ mass-houses” onto their dwellings, served by Jesuits who traveled between them (Catholic Encyclopedia, “Archdiocese of Baltimore”, which also lists a Jesuit chapel at Port Tobacco by 1697). In 1715 the colony was returned to the Calvert family only after Charles Calvert, 5th Baron Baltimore, declared himself a Protestant; new oaths followed in 1716 and Catholics lost the vote in 1718. Maryland’s labor was by then shifting from indentured servants to enslaved Africans; see Province of Maryland.

The servant law the Assembly passed in 1715 allowed a runaway to be made to serve up to ten days for each day absent (Archives of Maryland, vol. 30, p. 284) and paid four hundred pounds of tobacco to whoever took one up (p. 288); a man servant was owed at the end of his time a hat, a suit of clothes, shoes, two hoes and an axe. Teresa of Ávila’s Life circulated in English among Catholics from the early 1600s; the passage Brother Hollis reads, her vision of the place prepared for her in hell, is quoted here from David Lewis’s translation (1870; 1904 edition). Mr. Thorogood, Kit Lowry, Brother Hollis, Father Langworth, Peter Cole, Matthew Spurling and the house at Nanjemoy are invented.
