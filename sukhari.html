The way I heard it, under the Fremont Bridge.

I. The Heat

Northwest Industrial, Portland, Oregon · June 2021

The village had twenty-two houses, if you called them houses, and everybody did, because that was one of the rules. They were sleeping pods eight feet by ten, framed in two-by-fours and sheathed in plywood by a church youth group from Beaverton and a crew of union carpenters over two Saturdays, each with a door that locked and a window of corrugated plastic and a number painted over the door. They stood in two rows on a gravel lot between a cement terminal and the on-ramp to the Fremont Bridge, and the bridge went over them so high that on clear days its long arch was most of the sky to the east. There was a shipping container fitted out as a kitchen and another for showers, four blue toilets, a chain-link fence, a gate with a sign-in book on a clipboard, and a whiteboard by the gate with the rules written on it in marker. The rules were made at the meeting on Tuesday nights and voted on by everybody who lived there. That summer there were nineteen.

Rose Jim had been chair for two years. She was sixty-eight, a Yakama woman from White Swan who had come down the river at nineteen and spent most of the next forty years on the docks at Terminal 6, lashing and unlashing containers as a casual, never quite getting the hours to be registered. When the big carriers pulled out of Terminal 6 in 2015 and 2016 there was not much left to lash, and the rent on her apartment in St. Johns went up twice in one year after that. She went home to the reservation every August for huckleberries and came back. People asked her why she came back, and she said the village needed a chair. They had elected her because she said the least at meetings, and what she said, people did.

On Saturday the twenty-sixth of June it was a hundred and eight degrees in Portland, the hottest it had ever been. On Sunday it was a hundred and twelve. On Monday it was a hundred and sixteen, and the wires over the MAX tracks sagged in the heat and the trains stopped running. The pods were plywood boxes in the full sun. By ten in the morning you could not put your hand flat on a door.

Rose moved everybody out of the houses and under the on-ramp, where there was shade and a little air coming off the river, and they lay on wet sheets on the concrete with their dogs. Duane, who was seventy-four and had a bad heart, went grey around the mouth on Sunday afternoon, and with the trains stopped they could not get him to the cooling center at the Convention Center until a church van came. On Monday a woman from the county drove by with a pickup full of water and said they were lucky, and Rose asked her lucky how, and the woman said, you have shade.

The one who got them through it was Pavlo, from number fourteen.

He had been in the village since March. He was fifty-eight, a Ukrainian from a village outside Ternopil, a big quiet man with a grey beard cut close and forearms burned on the inside in a ladder of small white scars, the marks bakers get from oven racks. He had come over in 2000 behind his sister, who had married a Pentecostal from Vancouver, and for nineteen years he had worked nights in the bread room of a Slavic bakery in Gresham, and in the first spring of the sickness the bakery had closed and did not open again. Everyone in the village called him Paul. He let them.

All three days of the heat he walked to the gas station on Nicolai Street and back, a mile each way, pulling a cooler on wheels, and filled it with bags of ice, and came back with his shirt black with sweat and the cooler sloshing, and went along the people lying under the ramp and put ice in towels and the towels on their necks. He did not talk while he did it. Rose watched him go out and come back, out and come back, nine or ten times on the Monday, the hottest day there had ever been, and his lips were moving the whole time, and once, when he went by close enough, she heard that it was not English and that it was the same few words over and over.

Seventy-two people died in the county that weekend, most of them alone in apartments with a fan running. Nobody in the village died.

II. Two Streets

Northwest Twenty-third and Twenty-first Avenues · July

In July the heat broke and the village went back to its routines, and so did Pavlo, and his routines were these.

At six in the morning he signed out at the gate and walked up through the industrial streets, past the scrap yards and the bus barn, and under the freeway, and into Northwest, where the old Victorian houses had been turned into shops and the shops sold soap for eleven dollars a bar. On Twenty-third Avenue there was a bakery with a back door on the alley, and at a quarter to seven every morning a young woman with flour in her eyebrows opened it and put yesterday’s bread into a bin for the food bank pickup. The first time Pavlo knocked she had looked at him the way people looked at him now, and then she had looked at his forearms, and asked him where he’d worked.

After that she let him take what he wanted from the bin before the van came. She offered him croissants, and the morning buns with orange peel in them, and the scones, which were the first things people threw out. He thanked her and took two loaves of plain bread. Country bread, rye, a baguette gone hard as a broom handle. No butter in it, nothing sweet. Just bread, please. She stopped offering the pastry after a week.

Then he walked back down to the village, a mile and a half, and ate his breakfast with everybody else, the oatmeal Shane made in the kitchen container in a stockpot, with brown sugar if there was any, and washed his bowl. Then he walked back up again, to Twenty-first, where an old Italian kept a bakery in a storefront and threw out more bread than anybody in Portland, and took two more loaves there, and came home.

Shane, who ran the kitchen and had been a line cook at a hotel downtown until the sickness closed it, said once at the sink: Two trips? For bread? I could get you a whole case of bread from the food bank. Sandwich bread. Sliced.

Pavlo said thank you. He did not want sandwich bread.

In the afternoon, when the sun came over the roof of the cement terminal and lay full on the lot, he took the loaves and his knife, a long serrated bread knife with a handle worn pale where his thumb went, the one thing he had brought from Gresham, and he sliced them thin on a cutting board, every slice the same. He laid the slices out on two oven racks he had found behind a restaurant, and set the racks on the flat roof of the shower container, where the steel got hot enough by three o’clock to make a dog yelp. By evening the bread was dry all the way through, light, pale gold, and hard enough to ring when you dropped a piece on the roof. His grandmother had made them like this in the oven after the bread, from the ends of the loaves, for the road and for the hungry months. Sukhari. You could carry them for a month. You could keep them for a year.

He put them in a white plastic bucket that had held frosting, with a lid that snapped down, and carried the bucket into number fourteen, and on the last Thursday of July he went in after it and shut the door, and nobody saw him for five days.

III. Number Fourteen

The village · August

It was Krystal who noticed first, because she lived in thirteen and she noticed everything that happened in fourteen. She was the village secretary, thirty-one, from Klamath Falls, and she kept the minutes on a laptop with a cracked screen, and she was frightened of rats beyond what anyone thought was reasonable until she told them why, which she did once, at a meeting, and after that nobody said anything about it again.

He hasn’t come out, she said to Shane. Since Thursday. I hear him. I hear him moving around and I hear him talking. Real quiet. All night.

Talking to who?

I don’t know. It’s not English.

It was in the rules that you took your turn on security, four hours on the gate with the clipboard and the radio, and Pavlo had not missed a turn. He had traded all his turns that week ahead of time, to Duane and to a boy named Marcus, and paid them in cigarettes he didn’t smoke. That was allowed. But when Shane went and looked at the gate book he saw that Pavlo had signed in on Thursday afternoon and had not signed out, not for the morning bread, not for anything, and he hadn’t been to the kitchen for breakfast or for dinner, and nobody had seen him at the showers.

The heat had frightened all of them. They knocked.

He opened the door. He was sitting on his cot, in a clean T-shirt, with a cord of knotted black wool wound around one hand, a hundred knots on it, and in the corner on the floor was the white frosting bucket with its lid off, and next to it a gallon jug of water, half gone. There was nothing else in the house that had not been there before: a blanket, a duffel, a small picture of a saint propped on the windowsill, a hook with his good shirt on it. It smelled like bread.

I’m all right, he said. Thank you. I am praying.

He said it the way somebody else might have said I’m reading or I’m watching the game. Then he waited, politely, to see whether they wanted anything else, and when they didn’t, he shut the door.

It was all over the village by dinner. Paul had a bucket of food in his house. Paul had a whole bakery in there. Paul was sitting in the dark eating bread out of a bucket and talking to himself. A man who had come in June said Paul was holding, that you didn’t sit in a box for five days for no reason, and Shane told him to shut up, but the thing had been said and it went around anyway.

And what was underneath it, which nobody said out loud, was something else. Every one of them stood in lines. The line at the church on Burnside for sack lunches, the line at the county for the paperwork, the line at the clinic, the line for the shower, the line for the phone charger, the line for everything. You stood in the line every day and you said please and thank you to people younger than your kids, and that was what it cost. And here was Paul, who had figured out how to stand in line twice and then not stand in it at all for a week. Who did he think he was.

Krystal said it at the kitchen door, with her arms crossed. There’s a rule. There’s supposed to be a rule. You don’t keep food in your house. Everybody knows that. That’s how you get them.

But there was not a rule. Shane went and read the whiteboard at the gate to make sure. There were nineteen rules, and they said no weapons, no hard drugs, no violence, no fires, no guests after ten, take your turn on security, take your turn on the toilets, clean up after your dog, and eleven more, and none of them said anything about bread.

So they took it to Rose.

IV. The Rule

Tuesday night · August 10

Rose did not answer them. She finished the cigarette she was smoking, which was one of four she allowed herself a day, and put it out carefully in a coffee can, and walked down the row to number fourteen and knocked.

He let her in. She sat on the only chair, which was a bucket, a different bucket, upside down, and he sat on the cot.

People are worried about you.

I’m sorry. I am fine.

What are you doing in here?

He thought about it for a while. He did not seem to be thinking of what to tell her. He seemed to be thinking of how to say it in English.

Nineteen years I work in the bread room, he said. At night. Eleven to seven. Mixing, shaping, the ovens. Nobody talks. And all night I say the prayer, the one my grandmother taught me. Lord Jesus Christ, Son of God, have mercy on me. All night. With the hands I shape the bread and inside I say the prayer, and it goes by itself, like the mixer. Like breathing. Nineteen years.

He turned the wool cord in his fingers.

Then the bakery closes. Then I am here. And every day I walk to the doors, and I knock, and I ask, and the girl is kind, and the old man is kind, and I say thank you, thank you, and the prayer goes. It stops. I come back and I have to start again from the beginning, like a child. So I think, my grandmother made sukhari for the road. I walk two streets one day, and I make sukhari, and then I stay. And it comes back. Since Thursday it is like in the bread room. It goes by itself.

What do you eat?

Two pieces in the morning. Two at night. Water.

Rose looked into the bucket. It was a little less than half full of pale dry slices of bread, all the same thickness, and nothing else. No peanut butter. No cans. Not a cracker. Bread that had been in a bin for the food bank van, cut thin and dried in the sun on the roof of a shower.

She nodded and got up off the bucket and went out.

•••

The meeting was in the space between the kitchen container and the on-ramp, on folding chairs, with the traffic going over the bridge above them so that you had to wait sometimes for a truck to pass before you could be heard. Seventeen people came, which was a lot. Krystal had the laptop on her knees. Pavlo came too, because Rose asked him to, and sat at the end of a row with his hands in his lap.

Krystal read the complaint. Shane said what he had seen. The man who had come in June started to say what he thought and Rose looked at him and he stopped. Then Rose took her phone out of her shirt pocket and held it out at arm’s length, the way she held everything she read now.

I went to Chemawa, she said. Some of them knew what that was and some didn’t. Four years. They read the Bible at us three times a day. I kept two or three things out of it, the way you keep a couple of good things out of a bad house. This is one. This is the oldest rule about food there is.

She had found it on her phone in the free Bible, which had different words from the one at Chemawa.

Moses said to them, “Let no one leave of it until the morning.” Notwithstanding they didn’t listen to Moses, but some of them left of it until the morning, so it bred worms and became foul; and Moses was angry with them.

— Exodus 16:19–20

That was bread from heaven, Rose said, and it still drew worms. Ours draws rats. So here’s twenty. Nobody keeps food in their house overnight. Not bread, not chips, not candy. You get a shelf in the kitchen with your name on it, and it goes there, and nobody touches anybody’s shelf. That’s the motion.

It passed fifteen to two. Krystal typed it and then got up and wrote it on the whiteboard at the gate in green marker, in her neat round hand. 20. No food in houses overnight. Kitchen shelf w/ your name.

When she came back, Shane said what everybody was waiting for somebody to say.

So what about Paul?

Rose put her phone in her lap.

What about him? He didn’t break a rule. There wasn’t one till tonight. And you all go look in that bucket before you say another word about it. It’s bread out of a bin that was going to the food bank, that the food bank would have thrown out on Friday. He dried it on the shower roof. He eats four pieces a day with water. He took his security turns, he paid for the trades, and he got every one of you through the heat with ice on your neck.

She looked at the man who had come in June.

Some people get through a day by holding on to everything they can get. He gets through it holding on to about as little as a person can. That’s not the same thing. I don’t know what it is, but it isn’t that.

She picked up the phone again, and scrolled, and found the other one she had kept out of Chemawa, and read it in the new words, slowly, holding the phone out in front of her as a truck went over and shook the bridge and the sound came down around them.

See the birds of the sky, that they don’t sow, neither do they reap, nor gather into barns. Your heavenly Father feeds them. Aren’t you of much more value than they?

— Matthew 6:26

Nobody said anything. Then Krystal typed something, and Shane got up to start the dishes, and the meeting was over.

Pavlo went back to number fourteen and came out with the white bucket, and carried it across the gravel to the kitchen container. Shane gave him a strip of blue painter’s tape and a marker, and Pavlo printed his name on the tape in careful capitals, PAUL, and put it on the lid, and set the bucket on the bottom shelf. Every morning after that, and every evening, he came to the kitchen and took two pieces out of it and put the lid back on.

Krystal watched him do it, the first few mornings, from the door of the container. Then she stopped watching. In the third week of August she came to the meeting with a motion of her own, to set aside a shelf for day-old bread from the bakeries on Twenty-third and Twenty-first, for anybody, if somebody would walk up and get it. Pavlo said he would. Shane said he would go too.

V. The Chimney

Chapman Elementary School · September

Every September since her first one in the village, Rose had walked up through Northwest in the evenings to the school on Twenty-sixth Avenue to watch the swifts go down the chimney.

The school was old red brick, and on the side of it stood a big square brick chimney that had not carried smoke in years. For as long as anyone could remember, the Vaux’s swifts had come to it in the fall on their way south, thousands of them, and spent the night inside it clinging to the bricks, and the whole city came to watch. Before the sickness, the bird people from Audubon had made it an event, every night in September, with volunteers and parking and two or three thousand people spread out on the grass slope above the playing field with blankets and pizza and children sliding down the hill on flattened cardboard boxes. In 2020 the bird people had called it off, to keep the crowds down. In 2021 they called it off again.

People came anyway, a few dozen a night, and sat far apart on the slope with masks around their chins, and nobody stopped them. A volunteer with a clicker counter stood at the bottom of the hill counting the birds, because somebody had to.

On a Saturday in the middle of the month Rose asked Pavlo if he wanted to come. Shane came too, and Krystal, who had never been, and Marcus, who brought a skateboard and did not ride it.

They sat on the slope with the sun going down behind the hills. At first there was nothing, and then there were a few, very high, small and dark and fast as thrown things, flickering, with wings that seemed to beat out of time with each other. Then more came in from the north, and from over the river, in loose bunches, and then there were hundreds, and then the sky over the school was full of them, two or three thousand birds, each the size of a man’s thumb, turning together in a great slow wheel above the chimney. Their calls came down thin and high, like a lot of small doors creaking.

They don’t land, Rose said. Did you know that? They can’t perch. They don’t sit in trees. All day they’re up there catching bugs. They eat flying and they drink flying. They only come down at night, and only in something like this. Used to be the hollow trees, the big old ones. Then they cut the big old ones.

A hawk came and sat on the corner of the chimney, a young Cooper’s hawk, barred brown, and everybody on the hill booed at it, children and grownups together, a long rolling boo across the whole slope, and Marcus booed loudest. The hawk did not care. It sat there with its shoulders up like a man at a bar and when the wheel of swifts came round it reached up and took one out of the air, quick and ordinary, and flew off with it, and the boo turned into a groan.

Then the wheel tightened. The birds came round and round, lower each time, and the circle narrowed over the mouth of the chimney until it was a funnel, a dark turning funnel of birds pouring into the brick, the way water goes down a drain, the way smoke goes up a chimney, only backward. It went on for ten minutes. It went on until it was nearly dark, a thick steady pour of them, and the sound of their wings was like wind in dry grass, and then it thinned, and the last few came round once more, a handful, and went in one by one, and the last one hung for a second over the hole and dropped.

The whole hill cheered. The volunteer at the bottom clicked her counter one last time and wrote down a number.

Pavlo had not said anything the whole time. He was sitting with his elbows on his knees, the wool cord around his hand, and Rose saw that his lips had been moving again, and that he was smiling.

Inside, Rose said, they all hang on the bricks, one on top of the other, like shingles. The bricks keep the heat from the day. In the morning they come out and go eat. Couple days, they’re gone south. Mexico, or further. Nobody knows which ones. Nobody follows them.

They don’t carry nothing, Pavlo said.

Nope.

On the walk back down, under the freeway, in the dark, Krystal asked Pavlo what he said when he said the prayer, the words in his language, and he told her, and she tried to say it after him and couldn’t, and they both laughed, and he said it again slower.

VI. The Shelf

October

The count at the school went down after the middle of the month. Six thousand. Four thousand. A night in the last week of September with a few hundred. Rose went up on the second of October and sat on the slope a full hour and the sky over the chimney stayed empty, and the volunteer at the bottom put her clicker in her pocket and went home.

On the morning of the fifth Pavlo was gone.

Nobody saw him go. Duane had the gate from two to six that night and swore nobody signed out, and the book bore him out; the last line in Pavlo’s hand was from the afternoon of the fourth, when he had come back from the bakeries with the day-old bread for the shelf. The door of number fourteen was unlocked and open a few inches. The cot was folded. The floor was swept. The blanket and the duffel and the picture of the saint were gone, and the wool cord, and the good shirt off the hook, and the bread knife.

Rose walked up to Twenty-third and asked the young woman with flour in her eyebrows, who said she hadn’t seen him since Monday and was he all right, and Rose said she thought so. The old Italian on Twenty-first hadn’t seen him either. Rose called the sister in Vancouver, whose number was on Pavlo’s intake form, and the sister said he had called her Sunday night, which he did not do often, to say he was well and that she shouldn’t worry.

Did he say where he was going?

No, said the sister. I asked him. He said he would be on the road. He said, tell Rose thank you.

At the meeting that Tuesday they voted to give number fourteen to a couple who had been on the list since June. Krystal wrote it in the minutes. Shane said he would keep walking up for the day-old bread, and he did, all that winter, two streets, every morning, and came back with it in a laundry bag over his shoulder and put it out on the shelf for anybody.

The white bucket was on the bottom shelf of the kitchen container. Rose found it there the morning he left, washed out and dried and turned upside down on the shelf, the way you leave a thing you are finished with so that nothing can settle in it. There was nothing in it. The strip of blue tape was still on the lid with his name on it in careful capitals, and nobody took it off, and all that winter nobody put anything in it.

Background

The verse

Men who have no riches, who live on recognised food, who have perceived void and unconditioned freedom (Nirvana), their path is difficult to understand, like that of birds in the air.Dhammapada 92, tr. F. Max Müller (1881)

In the commentary, the elder Belaṭṭhisīsa finds the daily almsround a bother. He walks one street for alms, eats his breakfast, walks a second street, and carries home plain boiled rice with nothing on it. He dries it and stores it, and then spends several days in meditative absorption, eating the stored rice when he needs to. The other monks are offended and report him to the Buddha, who makes the rule against monks storing food. But because the elder acted before the rule existed, and because he was content with very little, the Buddha declares him blameless and speaks the verse about those who have no stores, whose track is as hard to follow as birds in the sky. The commentary's rice becomes the old Slavic sukhari here, bread sliced and dried hard to keep. Read the full commentary story. The prayer Pavlo says is the Jesus Prayer of the Eastern Orthodox tradition, often counted on a knotted wool prayer rope.

Portland, 2021

From June 26 to 28, 2021, the Pacific Northwest heat wave broke Portland's all-time record three days running: 108°F, then 112°F, then 116°F. TriMet stopped MAX light rail when the overhead wires sagged, and the county opened cooling shelters, among them one at the Oregon Convention Center. Seventy-two of Oregon's confirmed heat deaths were in Multnomah County, most of them in homes without air conditioning or cooled only by fans. Portland's self-governed villages for unhoused people, like Dignity Village, govern themselves, with elected officers and rules of their own. Rose's school, the Chemawa Indian School near Salem, is the oldest continuously operating Native American boarding school in the United States.

Since the 1980s, migrating Vaux's swifts have roosted each fall in the chimney of Chapman Elementary School in Northwest Portland, often thousands a night. Swifts cannot perch, and they once roosted in large hollow trees, which logging made rare. Portland Audubon's nightly Swift Watch draws crowds of a thousand or more, who boo the hawks that hunt the birds and cheer when the last one goes in. The event was cancelled in 2020 and 2021 to discourage crowds during the pandemic, but volunteers kept counting the swifts, and in 2021 they recorded most nights between two and seven thousand from late August into early October.
