The Izote

Yea, saith the Spirit, that they may rest from their labours; and their works do follow them.
— Revelation 14:13

Así lo oí contar, on Riggs Road.

I. The Sixteen

Hyattsville, Maryland · 1983–2017

José Alvarenga came up from Suchitoto in 1983 with a pair of work boots, a Bible with a cracked spine and the address of a cousin in Langley Park written on the inside of his wrist in ballpoint, in case they took his papers. They did not take his papers. He did not have any. He hung drywall for thirty-one years, and by the end he could tape a seam so the light went across it like water across a plate, and contractors in three counties asked for him by name, which was Don Chepe.

Marta came two years later with the four oldest. They lived in two bedrooms in the garden apartments on Fourteenth Avenue, then in a basement off Riggs Road with a dehumidifier running day and night, then, in 1996, in a brick rambler with a carport at the dead end of a short street on the Hyattsville side, which they bought with drywall money and the money Marta made cleaning houses in Bethesda, and with a loan Don Chepe paid off eleven months early because he did not like owing. By then there were fourteen children. Seven sons and seven daughters, the oldest born in a mud-brick house in Cuscatlán with a lamp burning, the youngest at a hospital in Takoma Park with a television on the wall. People said it the way you say a bus route. Los catorce de Don Chepe.

Don Chepe gave things away. That was the other thing people said. On Saturdays he set up a griddle under the carport and made pupusas for whoever came: the men from the day-labor corner at the 7-Eleven, the Guatemalan family next door, Mr. Pratt across the street, who was retired from the Post Office and liked the bean and cheese with extra curtido, and anybody's cousin just off the bus from Houston with nowhere to sleep. Marta hemmed pants for free at the kitchen table and cooked for every funeral in the church whether she had known the dead person or not.

The children grew up and took after them, each one in a different direction, the way water takes after a hill. Rigoberto, the oldest, drove a thermos of coffee to the 7-Eleven corner at half past five every morning before his own shift. Ana Gloria did taxes for free every February at the same kitchen table where her mother hemmed. Wilfredo fixed cars in the carport on Sunday afternoons, and you paid for the parts. Iris interpreted at the clinic and the courthouse in Upper Marlboro. Nelson kept a Folgers can on the church's back table for sending bodies home, because a coffin to San Salvador could cost what a car cost, and when the can got too heavy he opened a bank account for it. Morena, the youngest daughter, taught English on Thursday nights in the room behind the sanctuary. Kevin, the baby, born in Takoma Park, ran a Facebook page where people posted what they needed and other people said I have one. There were others: a daughter who cut hair, a son who drove people to Dulles at four in the morning, a daughter who sent the money every year for the roof of the school in the canton, a son who did the TPS forms for half the street every eighteen months. Mr. Pratt counted it up once on the back of an envelope and said it came to sixteen, with the parents. Sixteen ways of giving something away, going out of one house every week, like the spokes of a wheel.

In 1997 Don Chepe planted an izote by the front walk. It was not an izote. The real izote, the national flower, the tree with the white blossoms that grows in every yard back home, will not live through a Maryland winter. The man at the nursery in Beltsville told him so, and sold him a yucca that grew wild from Maryland south, called Adam's needle, with stiff leaves that curled threads off their edges. Don Chepe called it the izote anyway. Every June it sent up a stalk taller than he was, hung with cream-colored bells that turned toward the ground, and Marta picked the petals and fried them in egg the way they did at home, and they tasted almost right.

After the earthquakes in 2001 the government gave the Salvadorans who were here a paper called Temporary Protected Status, and Don Chepe went to the office and was fingerprinted and got a card with his picture on it, and for the first time in eighteen years he drove a car with a license in his wallet. He renewed it every eighteen months for sixteen years. He kept every card in a rubber band in his sock drawer, the expired ones too, and sometimes took them out and looked at them, his own face getting older on each one.

II. The Bed

Hyattsville · January to June, 2018

On the eighth of January it was on Univision. The government was ending the program for El Salvador. About two hundred thousand people, the man on the television said, had eighteen months to arrange their affairs. The last day was the ninth of September, 2019.

Ana Gloria turned the television off. Nobody in the kitchen said anything. Ten of the fourteen had been born in Maryland and were citizens. The four oldest had come up with their mother as children, and two of them were on the same cards as their father, and two were somewhere in between, in a file in an office that none of them had ever seen.

Don Chepe finished his coffee. Eighteen months is a long time, he said. You can make a lot of pupusas in eighteen months.

In February his eyes went yellow. In March a doctor at the hospital showed him a picture of the inside of him on a screen, with a shadow on the pancreas the shape of a thumbprint, and said months, and when Iris pressed her, said not many. Don Chepe laughed out loud in the examining room, and the doctor looked at Iris, and Iris had to explain.

He says good. He says he will leave before they make him.

He kept the griddle going under the carport through April, sitting on a kitchen chair, with Wilfredo flipping. In May the hospice brought a bed with rails and set it in the living room facing the front window, so that he could see the street and the izote and the cars coming down the dead end, and Marta slept on the couch beside it in her clothes.

Hermana Chayo came every Tuesday at five, on her way to work.

She was a short, square woman of seventy-four with her hair pulled back so tight it lifted her eyebrows, and she cleaned offices on K Street in Washington from six in the evening until two in the morning, and had for twenty-nine years. She came from Chalatenango. She had been on the Sumpul River in May of 1980, and she did not talk about it, and nobody asked. At the little church in the strip mall on University Boulevard, up a flight of stairs over a money-transfer place, between a dentist and a tax preparer, she was not the pastor. She had never been anything you could put on a sign. She led the Tuesday prayer circle and she was the one the church sent to the sick, and when somebody's son was in jail or somebody's husband was in a hospital that would not say where, people found that they were calling her before they called the pastor, and that she already knew.

She sat by the bed for twenty minutes every Tuesday in her blue work smock and said very little. Once she held his hand while he slept. Once she asked him about the roof of the school in the canton, whether it had held through the rains, and he talked for the whole twenty minutes and she listened as if roofs were the only thing in the world. Then she looked at her watch and stood and put her hand flat on top of his head, the way you would test a loaf, and went to catch the bus.

In the second week of June the izote put up its stalk. Don Chepe watched it from the bed. It came up a little every day, a green spear with the buds packed tight along it like teeth on a corncob, and on the Wednesday it opened, all at once, a hundred cream bells hanging over the front walk in the evening.

That night he called Rigoberto to the bed.

Call the hermana, he said. Tell her to send me eight of the hermanos. Or sixteen. Tomorrow. Tell her I want to hear the Word.

III. The Reading

The living room · Thursday evening

Hermana Chayo did not come herself. It was Thursday, and Thursday nights she had the whole twelfth floor of a law firm by herself because the other woman was out with her back. But she sent them. Eight came, as he had asked, and then four more, because word goes around, and they stood in the living room with their Bibles, and the ones who did not fit stood in the kitchen doorway. Don Rafa came, who had been a deacon in the church since it was in a basement, and drove the van, and drove Hermana Chayo home from K Street at two in the morning three nights a week because the buses stopped. Two sisters from the Tuesday circle came, and a young man named Ulises who had been out of the detention center in Virginia for six weeks, and a woman with a baby asleep on her shoulder. The fourteen were there too, most of them, and some of the grandchildren, and Marta, and Mr. Pratt standing just inside the front door with his hat in his hands.

Don Chepe opened his eyes and looked at them all for a long time.

Hermanos, he said. It is going to be hard for me to see you after this. I am very tired. Read me just one thing.

What do you want to hear, Don Chepe?

Juan catorce, he said. The one about the way. The one everybody knows.

Don Rafa opened his Bible to the place. It was the old Reina-Valera, the one with the accents on the little words that they don't put anymore, the one his mother had read to him by a kerosene lamp. He had a good reading voice, slow, and he read it the way you would read a letter out loud to someone who cannot see well, so that every word got there.

No se turbe vuestro corazón: creéis en Dios, creed también en mí. En la casa de mi Padre muchas moradas hay…

Let not your heart be troubled: ye believe in God, believe also in me. In my Father’s house are many mansions…

— Juan 14:1–2 (Reina-Valera 1909); John 14:1–2 (KJV)

At that moment six buses came down the dead end.

They came one behind the other with their lights on, slowly, the way buses come into a terminal, and pulled up along the curb in front of the house, though the street was not long enough to hold even one of them, and they fit. They were school buses from up north, the old long-nosed kind that people in El Salvador buy at auction and drive down through Mexico, and they had been painted the way they paint them at home, every inch. One was red with yellow flames along its flanks. One was purple and silver with a picture of a waterfall on the back. One was green and gold with chrome horns along the roof like a row of trumpets. They had strings of colored lights along their roofs and names across the visors over the windshields, the way the buses at home do: Dios Es Amor, El Peregrino, Mi Reina, a girl's name, a saint's name, a name in letters he could not read. Music came out of all of them at once, ranchera and cumbia and an old hymn, and it did not clash. Their engines idled. He could smell the diesel.

In the door of each bus a young man hung by one arm, the way the ayudantes hang at home, half in and half out, calling the route.

Don Chepe! Súbase, súbase! There’s room, Don Chepe. Come with us.

And another: Leave that old cántaro, Don Chepe. When the clay jar cracks you don’t keep drinking out of it. You throw it out and get a gold one. Súbase!

The doors were folded open. Inside, the seats were empty and clean.

Don Chepe lifted his hand off the blanket. He did not want to miss the next verse. Don Rafa was coming to the part about the way, the part he had asked for, and the young men in the doors were calling over it.

¡Esperen! he said. ¡Esperen! Wait. Wait.

Don Rafa stopped reading with his finger on the line.

Nobody in the room moved. Then Ana Gloria began to cry, and then Iris, and then one of the sons who had come in from Houston, a big man who drove a truck, put his face in his hands. Ana Gloria said it out loud, to nobody, in the voice you use when you cannot help it.

He never could get enough of the Word. All his life. And now he asks for it and he stops it himself. She pressed her knuckles against her mouth. In the end everybody is afraid. Everybody.

The hermanos looked at one another over the bed. Don Rafa closed his Bible, carefully, with his finger still in it, then took his finger out.

This is not the time for us, he said quietly, and the others nodded.

They went out one at a time, touching Marta on the shoulder as they passed. They went down the front walk under the izote, with its hundred bells hanging in the dusk, and got into Don Rafa’s van and the two cars and backed out carefully around the end of the street, where there was nothing parked but Mr. Pratt’s Buick, and drove away.

IV. The Izote

The front walk · the same evening

After a while Don Chepe came back to himself and looked around the room.

Why are you crying?

Nobody wanted to say it. Finally Morena did, because she was the youngest daughter, and the youngest daughter in that family had always been allowed.

Papi, you sent for the hermanos, and they came, and they were reading what you asked for, and you told them to stop. You stopped them yourself. So we thought, even Papi. Even him, at the end.

Where are the hermanos?

They left. Don Rafa said it wasn’t the time for them.

Don Chepe closed his eyes. Ay, Rafa, he said. Mija, I wasn’t talking to the hermanos.

Then who were you talking to?

He told them. The six buses along the curb with their lights on. The paint. The music. The young men hanging in the doors calling him by his name, telling him to get on, telling him to leave the cracked jar and take the gold one. They were talking over the Word, he said. I wanted to hear the end. I told them to wait.

Wilfredo went to the window and looked out. The street was empty. A light was on in the Guatemalan family’s kitchen next door. Mr. Pratt had gone back across and was standing in his own driveway with the hose, watering his tomatoes in the last of the light.

Papi, there’s nothing there. Just Mr. Pratt.

Don Chepe nodded as if that was what he had expected. Is the izote open?

Yes, Papi. Since yesterday.

Good. He lay quiet a while, gathering his breath. The buses have names on them, he said. Like at home. Tell me. Which is the best place to go?

They did not know what to say. Then Rigoberto, who was forty-seven and had a grandson of his own, and who remembered the house in Cuscatlán with the lamp burning, and his grandmother in it, said it.

The best place is where your mother and father are, Papi. Where Abuela Toya is.

Don Chepe opened his eyes and smiled at him. One of them has her name, he said. On the glass. Like the girls’ names on the buses. Niña Toya. The blue one. He moved his hand toward the window. Cut a stalk of the izote and throw it in the street. Say: this is for the bus from where my mother is.

Wilfredo got the machete from the carport, the one his father had brought from home and never once used for anything in thirty-five years except hanging on a nail. He cut the stalk low, at the base, with one stroke, and lifted it. It was taller than he was and heavy with flowers, and the bells shook and a few fell on the walk. He gave it to Morena, because it was Morena’s turn, and Morena carried it down to the curb with both arms, the way you carry a child that has fallen asleep, and stood there. Her brothers and sisters came out onto the front walk behind her. The Guatemalan family came out onto their step. Mr. Pratt turned off the hose.

For the bus from where Abuela is, Morena said, and threw it.

It went up. It did not come down. At the height of a bus’s side mirror, about where a man’s head would be if he were standing in the door, the stalk stopped in the air, as if it had caught on something, and hung there. It turned a little on itself. The bells swung. A few petals came loose and drifted down onto the asphalt, and the stalk went on hanging, in the June evening, over the empty street, with nothing under it and nothing holding it.

Everybody on the dead end saw it. The fourteen saw it, and Marta in the doorway, and the Guatemalan family on their step, the grandmother crossing herself the Catholic way, and Mr. Pratt with the hose dripping on his shoes. Yesenia, the Guatemalan family’s fifteen-year-old, had her phone up already, the way fifteen-year-olds do, and she filmed it. Everybody saw the flowers. Nobody saw any bus.

From the bed, through the window, Don Chepe could see it all.

Do you see the flowers? he called. His voice was clear.

We see them, Papi.

They are hanging on the bus from where my mother is. That’s the one I’m taking. Don’t be troubled. If you want to come where I’m going, keep doing what we did. All sixteen. Don’t drop any.

Marta went to him. The children came in from the walk. He looked at all of them, and at the window, and he died with his eyes open and his hand lifted a little off the blanket, the way a man lifts his hand to steady himself on a rail when he is getting on.

And the stalk of flowers moved. It went slowly at first, as if something under it had let off its brake. It went down the dead end at the height of a bus window, turning the way a wide vehicle turns, carefully, around Mr. Pratt’s Buick, and stopped at the corner as if for traffic, and turned right onto Riggs Road toward University Boulevard, and was gone. Mr. Pratt said afterward that he heard air brakes let go. Yesenia’s video stops there, because she started crying and dropped her hand, and the last four seconds are the sidewalk and her own sneakers.

V. The Circle

New Hampshire Avenue · two in the morning

Don Rafa picked Hermana Chayo up at two, as he always did on Thursdays, at the side door of the building on K Street where the cleaners came out. She got in the van in her smock with her purse on her knees, and he drove up Sixteenth Street and across and out New Hampshire Avenue toward Maryland, past the dark embassies and then the dark churches and then the dark car lots, and for a long way he did not say anything.

Did he hear the Word? she asked at last.

Yes, hermana. We read him John fourteen, like he asked. But in the middle of it he called out, wait, wait, and stopped us. Then the children started crying. So we said, this isn’t the time for us, and we left.

Hermana Chayo looked out the window at the orange barrels where they were tearing up the road for the new train.

He wasn’t talking to you, Rafa.

Don Rafa kept his hands on the wheel. Who was he talking to?

To the ones who came for him. They came for him in the middle of the reading, and they were calling him to get on, and he didn’t want to miss the end. So he told them to wait.

He drove a whole block before he said it. Is that true, hermana?

It’s true.

Don Rafa had known her twenty-six years. He had driven her home from K Street maybe two thousand times. He did not ask her how she knew it, when she had been on the twelfth floor of a law firm all night with a vacuum on her back. He had stopped asking her things like that a long time ago.

Where did he go?

Where his mother is.

They came to the light at University Boulevard, and it was red, and there was nobody else at the intersection in any direction, and he stopped anyway.

Last Saturday he was under the carport with the griddle, Don Rafa said. Sitting on a chair. Laughing at Wilfredo for burning them. Happy, with all of them around him. And now.

And now he’s gone where he’s going to be happy, Hermana Chayo said. It’s the same, Rafa. The ones who live like that, they’re glad here and they’re glad there. They don’t have to change anything to go. She turned her purse over on her knees and opened it and took out her own Bible, small, with a rubber band around it, and did not open it, only held it. The apostle wrote it from prison, she said. He knew they might kill him. Listen to what he told them.

Gozaos en el Señor siempre: otra vez digo: Que os gocéis.

Rejoice in the Lord alway: and again I say, Rejoice.

— Filipenses 4:4 (Reina-Valera 1909); Philippians 4:4 (KJV)

The light went green. Don Rafa did not go. She said the other one then, the one they read at funerals, the one he had heard a hundred times in the church over the money-transfer place and in the funeral homes on Route 1, and had never really heard.

Y oí una voz del cielo que me decía: Escribe: Bienaventurados los muertos que de aquí adelante mueren en el Señor. Sí, dice el Espíritu, que descansarán de sus trabajos; porque sus obras con ellos siguen.

And I heard a voice from heaven saying unto me, Write, Blessed are the dead which die in the Lord from henceforth: Yea, saith the Spirit, that they may rest from their labours; and their works do follow them.

— Apocalipsis 14:13 (Reina-Valera 1909); Revelation 14:13 (KJV)

Their works go with them, she said. He’s riding with all of it, Rafa. The coffee at the corner. The taxes. The cars. The coffee can for the bodies. He’s sitting there looking at it out the window, all sixteen. He can see every one.

The light went red again. A Metrobus went by on University with nobody in it, lit up like a fish tank. Don Rafa sat with both hands on the wheel and wept, which he had not done when his own father died in Usulután and he could not go home for the burial, and Hermana Chayo let him, and looked out the window, and did not touch him. When the light went green the third time he drove her home.

VI. The Way Home

Dulles, San Salvador, Suchitoto, Riggs Road · summer 2018

Nelson emptied the Folgers can. For once it was for his own father, and nobody said a word against it, and it was not enough, so the people it had been for came, the ones whose sons and husbands and mothers had gone home in it over twenty years, with envelopes. A man came all the way from Manassas with four hundred dollars in twenties and would not come in the house. The coffin went out of Dulles on a Tuesday in the belly of the plane, taking up the room of twelve suitcases, and Rigoberto and Marta flew with it, upstairs, with the tickets Kevin found on the internet.

Yesenia posted the video that night. By August it had been seen more than two million times. People in the comments said it was fishing line. People said it was a drone. People said it was the CGI. A man in Texas made a video of his own explaining how it was done, frame by frame, with arrows, and that one got two million too. Yesenia stopped reading the comments. Mr. Pratt, who did not have a Facebook, told anybody who asked that he had been standing right there with the hose, and that the only thing in the air on that street was flowers, and that he was seventy-one years old and had carried the mail for thirty-four of them and did not need anybody in Texas to tell him what he saw on his own street.

In the canton outside Suchitoto they say a thing too. They say that on the Thursday night in June, before anyone there knew he was dead, an old woman who had known Niña Toya was sitting outside her door on the road up from the lake, and she saw flowers go by. A stalk of white flowers, she said, going up the hill in the dark, at the height of a bus window, with no bus. She said she heard music. She said it went up past the school, under the new roof, and on up the hill past where the Alvarengas’ house had been, toward the cemetery, and stopped there. The coffin got there eight days later, and they buried him next to his mother, and on the cemetery wall by her grave, people say, there was a stalk of izote flowers lying on top of the wall, a little brown at the edges, the wrong kind, the kind that only grows up north.

In October a judge in San Francisco stopped the deadline, for the time being. Ana Gloria did the forms anyway, for everyone on the street, the way her father would have, and kept a copy of each one in a rubber band.

•••

The fourteen kept the sixteen. They had promised him in the living room, all of them, without saying so. Rigoberto kept driving the coffee. Iris kept interpreting. Nelson bought a new Folgers can, because the old one had gone in the coffin, under the pillow, where Marta put it. The sixteenth, the one that had been Don Chepe’s, Marta took. On the first Saturday in July she set up the griddle under the carport, by herself, at seven in the morning, and stood there, and after a while the men from the corner began to come down the dead end in ones and twos, as if they had been waiting for somebody to say so.

Mr. Pratt came across with a paper plate. Bean and cheese, extra curtido. He stood by the izote while he ate. Its leaves were curling their white threads in the heat, the way they do, and at the base, low down, where Wilfredo had cut the stalk, there was a clean pale stump the width of a man’s wrist, and beside it, already as tall as Mr. Pratt’s knee, out of season, a new green spear was coming up.

Background

The verse

The virtuous man delights in this world, and he delights in the next; he delights in both. He delights and rejoices, when he sees the purity of his own work.Dhammapada 16, tr. F. Max Müller (1881)

In the commentary, Dhammika is a lay disciple of Sāvatthī who, with his wife and fourteen children, keeps up sixteen different kinds of almsgiving. Dying, he asks the Buddha to send monks to recite the discourse on mindfulness. In the middle of it, six chariots arrive from six heavens to carry him away, and he cries “Wait! Wait!” to them. The monks think he means them, stop, and leave, and his children weep that even their father fears death. He explains, has them throw a garland of flowers, and the garland hangs in the air on the pole of the invisible chariot from the Tusita heaven, “the abode of the mothers and fathers of the Buddhas.” He dies and is reborn there, and the Buddha tells the monks that the righteous rejoice in both worlds. Prince George’s County, 2018

Salvadorans began arriving in the Washington suburbs in large numbers during the civil war of 1979–1992, which killed some 75,000 people; among its worst atrocities was the Sumpul River massacre in Chalatenango in May 1980. By 2000, Langley Park, built after World War II as garden apartments around the intersection of University Boulevard and New Hampshire Avenue, had the highest proportion of Salvadoran residents of any place in the United States. Salvadoran Americans are now one of the largest Latino groups in the country. Many belong to hometown associations that raise money for roads, water systems and schools in the towns they came from, and families sending a body home have long relied on collections among coworkers and neighbors, since repatriation can cost many thousands of dollars.

After two earthquakes struck El Salvador in early 2001, the U.S. granted Salvadorans already in the country Temporary Protected Status, renewable every eighteen months. On January 8, 2018, the Department of Homeland Security announced it would end TPS for El Salvador, giving about 200,000 people until September 9, 2019 to leave or find another status. On October 3, 2018, a federal court in California blocked the terminations in Ramos v. Nielsen, and the litigation kept the program alive for years. The buses in Don Chepe’s vision are the brightly painted former school buses of Central America, often called chicken buses. The izote (Yucca gigantea), whose flowers are eaten, is El Salvador’s national flower; it cannot survive a Maryland winter, but its cousin Adam’s needle grows wild there.
