By What Road

Our soul is escaped as a bird out of the snare of the fowlers: the snare is broken, and we are escaped.
— Psalm 124:7

As it has been handed down, in Little Creek Hundred.

I. Thirty

Little Creek Hundred, Kent County, Delaware · Third Month 1855

They came up out of the marsh on the fourteenth of Third Month, a little before light, thirty of them in a single line. They had left three farms below the Choptank on a Saturday night and had been five nights walking. By day they had lain up in pine woods, and once in a tobacco barn. The oldest was a woman past sixty named Sabra, who had a stick she had cut on the road. The youngest was at the breast. Eleven of the children could walk and four had to be carried. A man named Ennals had brought them across the last of it in the dark by the muskrat leads, in water to the knee, each one holding the coat of the one ahead.

Abram Gibbs was standing at the edge of the hard ground when they came to it. Nobody had sent him word. He had a lantern with three sides shut.

Abram was a free man and had been born one. He was fifty or near it. He owned six acres of fast land and a right to the marsh in front of it, and the marsh was his living. He took muskrat from the first frost to the middle of March and cut salt hay in summer, and through the cold months he gunned wildfowl for the Philadelphia market. He sat in a class at the African Methodist meeting and was nobody’s preacher. He did not say a great deal at any time. But for twenty years in that Hundred, when people came up from the south, they were brought to Abram before they were taken on. The Friends who did the taking on never asked what he said to them. They knew only that people who had sat an hour with Abram Gibbs rode differently in the wagon afterward.

His own house would not hold thirty. He put them in Joseph Lindley’s corn house, a quarter mile up the lane.

The Lindleys were Friends, of Little Creek Meeting. Joseph Lindley had a hay wagon with a false floor that would hide six grown people lying flat, or eight if some were small. It had never been asked to do more. He stood in his kitchen that morning working the sum. Thirty was four nights’ hauling to the next house, twelve miles and back, with one wagon passing one tavern four nights running. There were men from Dorchester in Dover already with a printed bill. A Friend caught at this work could lose his farm, and one in the county above had lost his seven years before. A free colored man could be put on the block and sold, and that had been done too.

We’ll begin tonight with the smallest, Joseph said. Amos will drive.

Amos was seventeen.

II. The Gate

The corn house lot · the same day, toward evening

He brought the wagon down the lane as the light was going and stopped at the gate of the lot. The corn house door stood open. He could see them inside, sitting close together on the floor, and Abram in front of them on an upturned bushel with his hat on his knee.

Abram was asking them their names. He went to each one, the babies as well, and asked, and waited for it, and said it back.

Amos had been raised not to break in on a thing like that. He thought he would wait until Abram had done greeting them, and speak to him then about the wagon. He looped the lines over the post and stood by the gate.

After the names there was talk. He could not make it out. Abram’s voice was low and did not rise. Once all of them laughed together, quietly, thirty people at once, and he had not expected to hear that from the building. Then it was still. The mare shifted her feet. A rail called out on the marsh. He waited what he judged to be a quarter of an hour more. Then, since it was coming on dark and there were twelve miles to go, he went up to the door.

Abram was sitting on the bushel alone. Thirty of Hannah Lindley’s quilts were folded and stacked against the wall with the corners squared. There was no one else in the corn house.

Abram, thirty came. Where are they?

Gone.

Amos looked behind him. There was the one lane, and he had been standing in it. On the other three sides lay the marsh with the tide out, and the mud ran smooth and shining all the way to the creek. A heron could not have crossed it without leaving a track.

By what road?

Through the air.

The boy turned that over. He had been taught to look for the plain sense of whatever was said to him, and he could find only the one.

But are they free, then? It is sixty miles to the Pennsylvania line, and Canada beyond it.

They’re free. They heard what I had to tell them, and they were free before they got up off that floor. After that the going was no trouble.

It was the season for it. All that week the geese had been getting up off the bay and leaving. A long line of them came in now over the marsh, so low that Amos heard the wings creak before he heard the voices. They passed over the corn house roof and bore away north where the sky was already dark. Abram came to the door to watch. He had shot geese for thirty winters and sold them by the pair, and he stood with his hat in his hand until the last one was over.

There, he said. Those have a road up there. Nobody cut it for them. The ones that were in here went the same.

He had the verse by heart, from class.

Our soul is escaped as a bird out of the snare of the fowlers: the snare is broken, and we are escaped.

— Psalm 124:7

I’m a fowler, Abram said. I know what a snare is. Take your mother her quilts.

III. The Daybook

Little Creek Hundred · Third Month to Sixth Month

The men from Dorchester came at sunup with a constable out of Dover and two dogs. They had a paper. Joseph Lindley read it through and told them they might look where they liked. The dogs took the scent down the lane with a great noise and into the corn house. There they went once around the floor and lay down. No scent went out of it. The men walked the edge of the marsh for an hour, and one went in to his thighs and had to be hauled. At the last they looked up, as men will when there is nowhere else to look. The sky was empty. They went back to Dover.

In Sixth Month a letter came for Abram Gibbs by way of Philadelphia, from St. Catharines in Canada West. Abram could read print and could not read script, and he carried it up the lane to Amos. It was written in a schoolmaster’s hand. It gave thirty names, the grown people first and then the children down to the youngest, in the order Abram had asked them. A few had signed and the rest had made their marks. It said they were all well and had found work, and that old Sabra wished him told she had the stick yet. It said they had come in on the morning of the fifteenth of March.

Amos read the date over twice. That was the morning the dogs lay down.

Abram took his letter home. He trapped that marsh nineteen years more. It was noticed in the Hundred that he sent no geese to market that spring, or any spring after it. He went on sending ducks. When he was asked, he said he had no quarrel with a duck.

Friends are not people who keep legends, and Little Creek Meeting never set this down in any minute. But Joseph Lindley kept a daybook for the farm, where he entered wages and weather and what was sown, and he could not leave a day unentered. The book is in the family yet. The line for that day is in his hand, and under it, as on every other page, he has ruled across and put down the wind.

3d mo. 14th. Thirty persons lodged in the corn house. Departed at evening. By what road not known.

Wind southerly. Fair.

Background

The verse

The swans go on the path of the sun, they go through the ether by means of their miraculous power; the wise are led out of this world, when they have conquered Mara and his train.Dhammapada 175, tr. F. Max Müller (1881)

Thirty monks from a distant country came to see the Buddha at Jetavana. Ānanda, arriving to wait on him, saw them going in and decided to stay at the gate until the greetings were over. The Buddha greeted them and taught them, and all thirty became arahats, rose into the air and left. When they did not come out, Ānanda went in and asked where they were. “Gone.” “By what path?” “Through the air.” “Have they already rid themselves of the pollutants?” “Yes.” Just then a flock of geese flew over, and the Buddha said that one who has developed the bases of power goes through the sky like those, and spoke the verse. That is the whole of the full commentary story. In America the people who rose and flew away from bondage were told of long before this retelling, in the legend of the flying Africans handed down along the Georgia and Carolina coast.

Kent County, Delaware, 1855

Delaware was a slave state that never seceded, with far more free Black residents than enslaved ones, and the main overland road north from Maryland’s Eastern Shore ran up through it to Wilmington and Pennsylvania. Under the Fugitive Slave Act of 1850 owners and their agents could pursue people into any state. The Quaker farmer John Hunn was fined so heavily for sheltering a family that his farm near Middletown was sold at sheriff’s sale in 1848. Samuel Burris, a free Black conductor born in Kent County, was jailed and put on the auction block for the same work, and was bought and freed by an abolitionist posing as a trader. Much of the guiding was done by free Black Delawareans such as those of Star Hill, near the Quaker village of Camden. Large parties were rare but real: in October 1857 twenty-eight people, many of them children, left Dorchester County together in heavy rain.

Friends dated by number, so March was Third Month, and Delaware counties are still divided into hundreds. The bayside of Kent County is tidal salt marsh, now largely within Bombay Hook National Wildlife Refuge, where geese winter in great numbers and leave for the north in March; marshmen there lived by muskrat, salt hay and market gunning. St. Catharines, in what was then Canada West, was one of the chief places where people who had freed themselves settled. Abram Gibbs, the Lindleys and the thirty are invented, and no daybook records them.
