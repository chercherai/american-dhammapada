— Matthew 10:42

Kida simi‘t, in the back pews of the little church off Pipeline Road.

I. The Ear

Euless, Texas · the winter of 2020

The Mo’alem Fahmy Wassef had been blind since he was three years old, from a sickness of the eyes in a village above Asyut, and he had been a cantor since he was eleven, because in those days that was what a blind boy in a Coptic family became if God had given him an ear. He had sung in the churches of Shubra for forty years and in Texas for twenty-two. He was eighty-four. He lived alone in a second-floor apartment in a complex behind a tire shop on Pipeline Road, four minutes on foot from the church, which he had chosen for that reason over his son’s spare room in Keller, and he walked to every service with one hand on the rail of the outside stair and the other on the shoulder of whatever boy had been sent for him.

He could tell the cars in the church parking lot by their engines. He could tell the boys by their breathing before they opened their mouths. People said, and it was the kind of thing people say, that he could tell you which deacon in the choir had not prayed that morning, and that when he sang the Midnight Praise in the month of Kiahk he heard another choir behind his own and slowed down so as not to get ahead of it. He did not say anything about that. He did not say much about anything. On Saturday mornings he sat in a folding chair at the front of the fellowship hall with the hand cymbals in his lap and taught the hymns to the boys, the long ones that take twenty minutes, the way they had been taught for a thousand years, by ear, one line at a time, his voice and then theirs, his voice and then theirs, until they had it.

He took nothing. That was the other thing everybody knew about him. The church paid him a small salary and he gave most of it to a convent in Egypt. When families tried to give him money after a funeral or a wedding he handed the envelope back without opening it, and if they insisted he gave it to the first boy he could reach and told him to put it in the box. He had been given four winter coats in twenty-two years and had given away three.

The spring before, the church had closed its doors for the sickness, and the Saturday class had gone onto Zoom, and the Mo’alem had hated it more than anything that had happened to him since the death of his wife. You could not sing together on it. The sound came late. Every boy was a half a beat behind the next, and they sounded, he said, like a choir standing in a field a mile wide, each one singing to himself. In the fall they went back to the hall in masks, twelve chairs set six feet apart, and he said it was better than Zoom, and it was.

The boy who most often led him by the shoulder that winter was Bishoy Gerges, who was sixteen and lived with his parents in a new subdivision a mile and a half up the road. Bishoy had been born in Arlington. His Arabic was the Arabic of his mother’s kitchen and his Coptic was the Coptic of the hymns and nothing else, and he was the best voice in the class, and he knew it, and the Mo’alem had told him so exactly once.

II. The Freeze

Pipeline Road · February 14–16, 2021

It began to snow on the Sunday, which was Valentine’s Day, and it went on snowing. By evening the highways were glass. In the night it got colder than anybody in North Texas had felt in seventy years, and in the small hours of Monday morning the lights went out in the apartment complex behind the tire shop and in half the houses in Euless and Bedford and Hurst, and they did not come back. The grid was failing. The gas lines that fed the power plants were freezing at the wellheads, and the plants were tripping off one after another, and the people who ran the grid were cutting power to whole towns at a time to keep the rest of it from going down. On Tuesday morning the thermometer at the airport stood at two below zero.

Bishoy’s parents’ house had a tankless water heater and an electric range and a heat pump and nothing worked. They sat in the living room in their coats around a fire in a fireplace that had never been used, burning the slats of a broken bed frame, and his mother called everybody in the church directory one after another to see who was alive.

The Mo’alem did not answer his phone on Monday. His son in Keller could not get his car out of the driveway. On Tuesday morning Bishoy put on two pairs of socks and his father’s work boots and walked the mile and a half to Pipeline Road on the shoulder of the road, where the snow had a crust on it that broke under every step, past cars abandoned at angles in the ditches with their hazards long dead.

The door of the apartment was not locked. It never was. It was colder in the apartment than in the stairwell. The Mo’alem was lying on his bed under every blanket he owned and the one winter coat he had kept, with his knees drawn up and his rosary of knots in his hand, and he was shaking. Not the way a cold man shivers. A long, deep shaking that came up from his belly every half minute and took his whole body and let it go again. He had eaten nothing since Sunday. He had been sick to his stomach in the night and had cleaned it up himself, feeling his way to the bathroom, and the water in the toilet tank had frozen. He said, in his very small voice, that his insides had turned to wind.

Bishoy wanted to call 911. The Mo’alem said the ambulances had better places to go, and that it was not the kind of thing an ambulance was for. He lay still a while between two shakings, as if he were listening to something.

Go to Dr. Nabil, he said. Tell him the Mo’alem is sick in his stomach from the cold, and asks him for hot water.

Bishoy said Dr. Nabil lived in Bedford, more than a mile off, and wouldn’t have power either.

He has a gas house, said the Mo’alem. An old one. I’ve heard it. Go.

III. The Pilot Light

Bedford · Tuesday, February 16

Dr. Nabil Habashy was seventy-one years old and the most important man in the parish, after the priests, and some said before them. He had come to Texas in 1979 with a doctorate in geology from Cairo University and a job with an oil company in Houston, and he had made his money in the 2000s in the Barnett Shale, the great field of gas that lay under Fort Worth and the towns around it, so that in a sense the gas that heated half of the Metroplex had passed under his hands before it got to them. He knew the Coptic liturgy by heart in Coptic and could explain the Greek of it to the priests. He chaired the building committee for the new church, the real one with a dome, that was going to be built on the land the parish had bought off Highway 10, and the fund stood that winter at four million dollars, and a great deal of that was his. He was a proud man, and a correct one, and people were a little afraid of him.

He lived in the same brick ranch house in Bedford that he had bought in 1986, which had a gas range and a gas furnace with an electric blower that did not work and a gas water heater in the hall closet that was older than his grandson. It had a standing pilot, a small blue flame that burned day and night under the tank and did not need a plug. His son had been after him for ten years to replace it with a tankless one. That Tuesday it was the only hot water for a mile in any direction. Nabil had all four burners of the range lit and the oven door open and was sitting at the kitchen table in his overcoat and his karakul hat, reading the psalms of the First Hour by the blue light of the burners, when a boy knocked on the kitchen door with his face red from the cold and his mask frozen stiff.

Bishoy told him.

Dr. Nabil stood up. Bishoy saw the old man’s face change, and he did not understand it at first, because what came into it was not worry.

He sent to me? said Dr. Nabil. The Mo’alem sent to me, for something? And then, to nobody, in Arabic, the way you would say it in Shubra: What a blessing. What a blessing for this house.

In twenty-two years the Mo’alem had never asked him for anything. Nabil had tried, more than once, to give. He had sent an envelope after his own mother’s funeral, at which the old man had sung so that the whole church wept, and the envelope had come back to him in the hand of a nine-year-old with the seal unbroken. He had offered to buy him a house. He had offered to pay for a surgeon in Dallas who might do something for his eyes, and the Mo’alem had laughed and said that at his age he did not want to start looking at things now.

Nabil emptied the closet of everything that would hold water. He filled the big stockpot they used for the Easter lamb and the two pasta pots and both thermos jugs from the tap at the bottom of the heater, which ran so hot it steamed in the hall, and put lids on them, and wrapped the pots in bath towels. Then he went to the pantry and took down a tin of black honey, the thick dark molasses of the Egyptian sugar mills that his wife bought at the Arab grocery on Collins Street and ate with tahini on bread, and put it in Bishoy’s coat pocket.

Hot water, he said, and put this in it. A spoon in a glass. My mother did it for us for the stomach.

The pots were too heavy for one boy to carry a mile on the ice, and too hot to hold against his body. Nabil’s grandson Mina was staying with him, a large silent college boy from UT Arlington whose own apartment had frozen, and Nabil woke him and put him into his boots. Then the old man went out to the garage and came back with the long wooden handle of a push broom, unscrewed from its head, and hung the two pasta pots from it by their handles with luggage straps, one at each end, and laid it across Mina’s shoulders, and stood back to look at it.

Like the saqqa, he said to Bishoy, who carried the water in the old city when I was a boy. You won’t remember. You never saw it. Go slowly. Don’t spill it. Mina, if you fall, fall forward.

Bishoy carried the stockpot in his arms in its towels, and the thermoses in a backpack, and they went out into the white street, the big boy with the pole across his shoulders and the pots swinging, and the small one in front picking the way. They went a mile and a quarter in an hour. Twice Mina went down on one knee and did not spill. At the corner of Pipeline Road, where the tire shop was, a man none of them knew came out of a white pickup that was idling in the lot, a young man in an orange safety vest, and without a word took the stockpot from Bishoy and carried it up the outside stair for them and set it down at the Mo’alem’s door and went back down, and when Bishoy came out again ten minutes later to thank him the pickup was gone and there were no tracks in the lot but their own.

IV. The Basin

Pipeline Road · Tuesday afternoon

The woman in the apartment below the Mo’alem’s was Vietnamese, a grandmother whom everybody in the complex called Bà Sáu, and she had been looking after him since the lights went out, bringing up rice soup heated on a little butane camp stove she had bought for hurricanes in Houston twenty years ago. She had a statue of the white-robed Quan Âm on the shelf over her television, with an orange and a cup of tea in front of it, and the Mo’alem had told Bishoy once that her stair was the only one in the complex that did not ring when you climbed it, because she swept it. When she heard the boys on the stair she came up with the stove under her arm and set it on the Mo’alem’s kitchen counter and lit it, and put the stockpot on it to keep the water hot, and went back down again without having said a word in any language they shared.

Bishoy had never bathed anybody. He had seen his mother do it for his grandmother before she died. He found the plastic dishpan under the sink and filled it with the water from Dr. Nabil’s heater, cut with a little from the thermos so it would not scald, and knelt on the floor of the bedroom, and took the Mo’alem’s feet out from under the blankets one after the other. They were white and cold as stone. He put them in the water. The old man drew his breath in through his teeth and then let it out very slowly, and his hands opened on the blanket.

Bishoy washed his feet, and his hands, one at a time in the basin, and then wrung out a dish towel in the hot water and washed his face, and his neck, and the backs of his ears, the way you would wash a child, and dried him, and pulled the blankets up again, and Mina went and changed the water and brought more. They did it three times. Mina, who had not been inside a church since his confirmation, stood in the bedroom door with the empty pot and watched with an expression that Bishoy would remember for a long time afterward.

Then Bishoy filled a glass with hot water and stirred into it a spoon of the black honey from the tin, which went down into the water in a dark thread and turned the glass the color of strong tea, and held it, and the Mo’alem sat up against the headboard with Mina’s arm behind him and drank it in small swallows with both hands around the glass.

The shaking came once more while he was drinking, and he waited it out, and drank again. Then it did not come. They sat and waited for it, the two boys, the way you wait for thunder after lightning, and it did not come. The Mo’alem gave Bishoy the empty glass. His color had changed. He said the black honey was from Nag Hammadi, he could taste it, the mills there boiled it longer than the others. Then he lay down on his side and slept for four hours without moving, and Mina sat in the kitchen beside the camp stove with his coat on, feeding it cans of butane from Bà Sáu’s box, and Bishoy sat on the floor of the bedroom with his back against the wall, and toward evening, when it was dark and very cold, he heard the Mo’alem singing, very quietly, under the blankets, the first lines of the Midnight Praise, though it was nowhere near midnight. Arise, O children of the light. Bishoy did not know whether he was awake. He sang the responses anyway, as low as he could.

The power came back to Pipeline Road on Thursday morning. The Mo’alem was in church on Sunday, in the one coat he had kept, with his hand on Bishoy’s shoulder.

V. The Question

Pipeline Road · the Saturday after

Dr. Nabil came to the apartment on the Saturday after the thaw, in the afternoon, when the Saturday class was over and the Mo’alem had been walked home. He brought nothing. He had thought for a long time about what to bring and had decided that the Mo’alem would only give it to a boy. He climbed the stair slowly with his hand on the rail, and it rang under his feet.

The Mo’alem knew him before he knocked.

They sat in the two chairs by the window. The room was warm again and smelled of Bà Sáu’s rice soup and of the radiator. Nabil asked about his health, and was told, and asked about his stomach, and was told it was a stomach again, thanks be to God and to Dr. Nabil’s water heater, and they were quiet a while. Then Nabil said that he had a question he had been carrying around for a year and had not been able to put down.

It was about the building fund. He had given, he said, a great deal of money to the new church, and he would give more. He had given to the monasteries in Egypt, to the bishop’s charities, to the orphans in Upper Egypt, to the family of every man in the parish who lost his job in the oil bust, and he did not say any of this as a boast, he said it as an accountant would read out a column. And he did not know whether any of it had been accepted. He had prayed and he had given and he had given and he had prayed, and he was seventy-one, and in a dark house on Tuesday with the gas burning on the stove he had found he could not answer the question that a child could answer. He said it in Arabic, formally, the way he would have put a question to a bishop.

Mo’alem. To whom should a man give? To whom is a gift given, so that it bears fruit? How does a man make an offering that God will receive?

The Mo’alem turned his face toward the window, where the light was. He was smiling a little.

Doctor, he said, you know it already. You have read it to us yourself, from the Gospel, standing at the lectern. And he said it, not as a teacher says a thing but as a cantor gives the first line for the boys to take up, so that Nabil heard his own voice come in after it.

He that receiveth a prophet in the name of a prophet shall receive a prophet’s reward: and he that receiveth a righteous man in the name of a righteous man shall receive a righteous man’s reward. And whosoever shall give to drink unto one of these little ones a cup of cold water only, in the name of a disciple, verily I say unto you he shall in no wise lose his reward.

— Matthew 10:41–42

It is the one who receives it, he said. Not the size of the cup. You gave hot water, he added, so perhaps a little more.

Nabil did not laugh. He asked, then, the question under the question, which was how a man was to know which one was the righteous man, the prophet, the disciple. Whether it was the new church with the dome, or the monastery, or the orphans, or the priest, or the bishop. How a man was supposed to know where to set the cup down.

The Mo’alem said that every morning of his life, before it was light, Dr. Nabil had said the psalms of the First Hour out of the Agpeya, and so had he, since he was a boy, and that the answer was in the First Hour, a few psalms in, and they could say it together if Dr. Nabil liked. And they did, the old blind man and the old geologist in the two chairs, in Arabic, from memory, and Bishoy, who had come back up the stair for the Mo’alem’s cymbals and was standing in the door, heard it in the English of his own Agpeya in his head while they said it.

O Lord, who shall sojourn in thy tabernacle? and who shall dwell in thy holy mountain? He that walks blameless, and works righteousness, who speaks truth in his heart.

He that does these things shall never be moved.

— Psalm 14:1–2, 5 (Septuagint)

When they had finished Nabil sat with his hands on his knees.

You sang with me once, the Mo’alem said. In Shubra, at the church of the Virgin, in nineteen fifty-nine. You were nine years old. You had a good voice and you came in too early on every line, because you wanted to be first. Your father was a pharmacist on Shubra Street and stood at the back by the pillar on the left. You had a cough that winter.

Nabil looked at him. He had not sung in a choir since he was twelve years old. He had never told anybody in Texas about the church of the Virgin in Shubra, or the pharmacy, or the pillar.

I remember all of them, said the Mo’alem. Every voice. Where they stood. The ones who are in heaven now and the ones who aren’t yet. He put his hand out, and found Nabil’s knee, and patted it twice. You still come in early.

VI. The Back Row

The fellowship hall · Lent, 2021

The building committee met the following week, on Zoom, and Dr. Nabil Habashy proposed that a part of the fund, a large part, be set aside before anything was poured for the dome, for the parishioners who had come out of the freeze with burst pipes and no insurance, and for the apartment complex behind the tire shop on Pipeline Road, where the water had come through three ceilings, and for whatever else the Mo’alem might point at, though the Mo’alem had not pointed at anything and would not. There was an argument. He won it. He was used to winning arguments, and this time he did not enjoy it, and he noticed that.

Then he went to Abouna and asked to be made a member of the Saturday class.

Abouna thought he was joking. The Saturday class was for boys. Dr. Nabil was the chairman of the building committee and the oldest deacon in the parish by rank and knew more Coptic than the priest did. Dr. Nabil said that was true, and that he knew the words to every hymn in the book, and that he had not learned one of them properly since he was twelve years old, and that he would like to learn them now from the Mo’alem, the old way, by ear, one line at a time, before one of them died, and he did not much mind which.

So on the first Saturday of the Great Fast there were thirteen chairs in the fellowship hall instead of twelve, set six feet apart, and in the back row among the boys in their masks, in a white tunic that had been ironed by his wife and was a little short in the sleeves, sat a seventy-one-year-old geologist with a pencil he had been told not to use. The Mo’alem sat at the front with the cymbals in his lap and gave the first line of the long Lenten hymn, and the boys took it up, and the old man in the back row came in half a beat too early, and the Mo’alem lifted one hand from the cymbals without turning his head, and held it there, open, in the air, until he waited.

Background

The verse

Him I call indeed a Brahmana who knows his former abodes, who sees heaven and hell, has reached the end of births, is perfect in knowledge, a sage, and whose perfections are all perfect.Dhammapada 423, tr. F. Max Müller (1881)

In the commentary, the Buddha falls ill with a disorder of the humors and sends his attendant, the elder Upavāṇa, to the brahmin Devahita for hot water. Devahita is overjoyed to be asked. He gives hot water and a jar of molasses and has one of his men carry the water on a shoulder pole. The elder bathes the Buddha in the hot water and gives him the molasses mixed in water to drink, and the illness passes. Devahita then comes to the Buddha with a question in verse: to whom should one give, so that the gift bears great fruit? The Buddha answers that gifts to one who is truly a brahmin, the sage who knows his former lives, sees heaven and hell and has come to the end of birth, bear the greatest fruit, and Devahita takes refuge and becomes a lay disciple. North Texas, February 2021

In mid-February 2021 a run of winter storms, the worst of them named Uri, drove arctic air deep into Texas. Demand for power broke records while frozen gas supplies and unwinterized plants failed, and in the early hours of February 15 the state’s grid operator began cutting power to keep the whole system from collapsing. More than 4.5 million homes and businesses lost power, some for days, in temperatures that reached −2 °F at Dallas/Fort Worth International Airport on February 16, the coldest in North Texas in seventy-two years. Pipes burst across the state and many people faced hypothermia in their own homes; the state later put the death toll at 246. See the 2021 Texas power crisis. Gas appliances with a standing pilot, which need no electricity, were for many families the only heat or hot water left.

The Coptic Orthodox Church of Egypt now has many parishes in American cities and suburbs, including in Dallas–Fort Worth. Its hymns, sung to hand cymbals and triangle, were handed down orally for centuries, and the parish cantor, the mu’allim or “teacher,” taught them to the deacons by ear. Most cantors of the older generation were blind, since the blind were thought to have the finer ear; the most famous modern cantor, Mikhail Girgis El Batanouny (1873–1957), helped preserve the chants. Psalm 14 in the Greek numbering (15 in the Hebrew) is among the psalms of the First Hour of the Agpeya, the Coptic book of hours prayed each morning. The Mo’alem, Dr. Nabil, Bishoy, Bà Sáu and the church off Pipeline Road are invented.
