No Winters

Be ye angry, and sin not: let not the sun go down upon your wrath
— Ephesians 4:26

Mar a chuala mi e, on the Middle Fork.

I. The Chair

Notch Creek, Middle Fork of the Koyukuk, Alaska Territory · autumn 1922

Murdo Morrison kept the roadhouse at Notch Creek, seventy miles north of the Arctic Circle, and the men on the creeks called him the Precentor. In the church on the North Shore of St. Ann’s Bay, in Cape Breton, where he was raised, there was no organ and no hymnbook. There were the psalms of David in Gaelic, and one man who stood under the pulpit and gave out each line for the people to sing after him. Murdo’s father had been that man, and Murdo after him for two years, until he went over the Chilkoot in the spring of ’98 with a sack of oatmeal and a borrowed sled. He had not sat under a minister since. He kept the psalms. On Sunday nights he sang one at the stove, giving out the line to nobody, and the Swedes and the Irishmen and the one Montenegrin in the room let him get on with it, the way you let a man wind his watch.

He was fifty-four years old and had twenty-four winters in. In that country a man’s age was his winters. Summers did not count, because anybody can stand a summer. A man who had seen the ice come and seen it go out again was a sourdough, and a man who had not was a cheechako if he was seventy, and when two teams met on a trail too narrow to pass, the driver with fewer winters turned out into the deep snow. Nobody had written this down. Nobody needed to.

The roadhouse was a log building forty feet long with a sod roof, a stove made from an oil drum in the middle of the floor, a rack of poles over the stove for drying socks and moccasins, and bunks along the walls. A meal was two dollars and so was a bunk, and a man who had no two dollars ate anyway and paid at cleanup, or didn’t. Murdo had one rule he would say out loud. When the door opened, whoever was nearest got up. You took the man’s mitts and his robe. You saw his dogs unhitched and fed. You got him water, and gave him your seat, and had his footgear off him and looked at his toes before he could tell you they were fine, because a man in off the trail at forty below is no judge of his own feet.

Beside the stove stood the one real chair in the house. Murdo had built it of birch in the winter of the deep snow and laced a caribou hide across it, and by custom it belonged to the oldest sourdough in the room, whoever that happened to be on a given night. Murdo never sat in it himself.

His cousin Norman Matheson came up on the last horse scow from Bettles in the second week of September, sitting on a crate of evaporated potatoes with a rug over his knees. He was the son of Murdo’s father’s sister, sixty-seven years old, and stout, so stout that the scow men had trimmed the load around him. In Baddeck he had kept a store and sat as a justice of the peace and been a major in the county militia, and people there had gone on calling him Major after the store failed and after his wife died, until the title was most of what he had left. He wrote to Murdo. Murdo sent the fare.

Nobody on Notch Creek grudged him that. What they noticed was the rest of it. The Major did not cut wood. He had Murdo’s good wolf robe on his bunk. He wore a boiled shirt with a collar, and a black broadcloth coat that he brushed every morning until it shone at the elbows like a stovepipe, and he sent his shirts down the creek to Mrs. Ahlquist with instructions about the starch. And from the first night he sat in the birch chair. He lowered himself into it after supper as if it had been freighted in for him, and folded his hands on his vest, and received the room.

Murdo saw it and said nothing. He had learned from dogs that there is no use correcting anything the first week, and then the first week had gone by.

The sun left Notch Creek on the twenty-ninth of November. It did that every year. The camp lay close under a long ridge called the Sawback, on the south side, and from the end of November the sun could not get over it, though at noon it turned the peaks behind the camp pink for an hour, and a man could read a letter by the sky. There was one low place in the Sawback, cut square like a gunsight, that gave the creek its name. On the ninth of January, at a few minutes before one in the afternoon, if the day was clear, the sun stood in that notch for eleven minutes. Every soul on the creek came out to look at it. Then they had a dance.

II. Mail Day

The roadhouse · January 7, 1923

The mail came once a month by dog team, relayed up from Tanana, and in January it came on the seventh, two days ahead of the sun, so that anybody who could travel was at Morrison’s already or on the way. Billy Dunne drove the upper end of the contract. He was twenty-six and had nine winters in, having come into the country at seventeen, and he was vain of them the way a boy is vain of his first moustache.

Three sleds came in behind him that afternoon, in the blue dusk that passed for the middle of the day. Tom Skaggs drove the first. He had come over the pass to the Yukon in ’86, ten years before anybody heard the word Klondike, and he had thirty-six winters in, and had left the end of his nose and two fingers in the worst of them. Hans Lien had walked off a farm in Goodhue County, Minnesota, at nineteen, and had twenty-five. He carried his mother’s blue china cup in his grub box, the one with the chip, and drank from nothing else. On the third sled were Jonas and Mary, Kobuk people, baptized with those names at the mission down at Allakaket. They hunted sheep and caribou for the camp, and nobody tried to count their winters, since their grandparents had wintered on that river before it had a camp on it or a calendar.

Murdo was out at the cache, sawing a frozen moose quarter. The cook was at the dog pots. The only man in the big room was the Major, in the birch chair, in his collar, with his hands folded on his vest and the lamp behind his white head.

They came in out of the dark in a roll of frost smoke, pulling ice from their beards, and saw him. He did not get up. He looked at each of them in turn and inclined his head.

Tom Skaggs had sat in that chair himself on a good many nights. He looked at the stout old man who had it now, and who plainly expected to keep it, and he did the arithmetic the country had taught him. A man Murdo Morrison would put in that chair over Tom Skaggs would have to be somebody. One of the old Hudson’s Bay men, maybe, or one who had come up the river with Harper and McQuesten in the seventies, that he had thought were all dead.

Old-timer, Tom said, and took off his cap. Skaggs. I don’t believe I’ve had the honor.

The Major inclined his head again.

So they waited on him. It was the natural thing. Tom Skaggs, with his own moccasins frozen to his socks, fetched the old man a mug of tea from the pot and stood holding it until he took it. Jonas brought in an armload of split spruce and built up the stove on the old man’s side. Hans Lien, who had some doctoring, saw how the old man’s ankles swelled over his shoes, and got down stiffly on one knee on the floor, and asked would he like them rubbed, since it helped the blood when a man had sat long. Mary stood by the door with her mitts still on, nobody having offered to take them. She watched all of it.

The Major did not say yes and did not say no. He let Hans Lien take his foot in his hands.

Billy Dunne had come in with the sack and was sorting at the mail table. He had carried the Major’s letters up the river since October, and he watched this with his mouth a little open.

Major, he said. Tell these men how many winters you’ve got in.

The Major looked at him.

None, he said. I came in September. I was a man of sixty-seven when I came.

Hans Lien’s hands stopped.

None, said Billy. You old fraud. You think a great deal of yourself. There’s thirty-six winters standing in front of you holding your tea, and twenty-five on its knees at your feet, and people by the door with their mitts on who were here before this river had a name in English. You never got up. You never took so much as a robe. They offer to do for you, and you sit there and let them. And look at you. You’re not even sorry. He snapped his fingers under the Major’s nose, the way you would to wake a dog.

The Major got up then. He came out of the chair red to the roots of his hair, and he was all at once what he had been in Baddeck on a court day.

Whom did you come here to see?

Murdo, said Billy. Same as always.

You came to see my cousin. And you look at me and say, Who is this? I held the Queen’s commission. I sat on the bench at Baddeck nineteen years. I’ll have your mail contract, boy. I’ll have every one of you turned from this door and every door on the river. I’ll see the whole breed of you finished.

He went out through the kitchen to find Murdo, and they saw before the door closed that he was crying.

Tom Skaggs put his cap back on. We’d better go along, he said. A man in that state will say anything, and it’ll be us he says it about.

III. The Flour Bin

The kitchen · the same hour

Murdo came in from the cache with frost on his eyebrows and the bucksaw in his hand. He found his cousin sitting on the flour bin with tears running into his collar, and four men and a woman standing along the wall.

Norman. What is it? You’re crying.

These people have insulted me.

Where were you sitting?

In the chair. By the stove.

Did you see them come in?

I saw them.

Did you get up?

I did not.

Did you take their mitts from them, or their robes?

No.

Did you see to their dogs? Did you get them water?

No.

Did you bring any of them a seat? Did you look at their feet?

I did not.

Murdo laid the saw on the table.

Norman, every one of those things was yours to do, for every one of them. A man who won’t do them has no business in that chair. The fault here is yours and nobody else’s. Ask their pardon.

They insulted me, Murdo. That boy snapped his fingers in my face. I’ll not ask pardon of the like of them.

Don’t be this way. The fault is yours. Ask their pardon.

I will not.

He’s a stubborn old man, Murdo, said Tom Skaggs from the wall. He said it without heat.

He is that. And it isn’t new.

We can see for ourselves what he is now, Tom said. What do you mean, it isn’t new?

Sit down, the lot of you. Billy, the mail will keep. I’m going to tell you about a man I met the first winter I was in the country. I’ve thought about him every day since September.

IV. Potter’s Cabin

The lower river · January 1899

The first winter, Murdo said, was the winter of the boats. In the summer of ’98 the overflow from the Klondike went up the Koyukuk in little steamboats that drew too much water, and the river dropped and then froze, and there they were. Most of them walked out over the ice before Christmas. The ones who stayed built cabins where the boats had stopped and called them cities.

There was one such place, and he would not say which, up a side river in under the hills, where the sun had been gone for weeks and was owed back at noon on a day in January that every man in camp had marked on the wall. They had chosen a steamboat captain named Rask to be judge for the winter, there being no other law nearer than the Yukon.

The night before the sun was due, a man came in off the hills. He had been eight months up a creek by himself and had come down for salt and matches. He was a big man, heavy, with his hair grown past his shoulders the way it will in eight months, and he was known as a hard one to share a tent with. He asked two boys at the edge of camp where a traveler could sleep. They sent him to a trader named Potter, who had a storehouse cabin, a large one, that stood empty at night. Potter told him he was welcome to it.

He had hardly got his robe spread when a second man came in off the hills by another trail. This one was young, from the North Shore of St. Ann’s, and had been out since spring as well. Potter thought, the first one may not care for company, and I won’t answer for it. So he said, There’s a man in there ahead of you. If he’s willing, it’s all one to me. The young man went to the door and asked. Friend, if it’s no trouble to you, I’d be glad of the floor for one night. And the big man said, It’s a big cabin. Come in and lie over on that side. They spoke civilly enough, and shared out what they had.

When it came time to sleep, the young man took note of where the other lay and where the door was, so that he could find his way out in the night without striking a light. But the big man, when he lay down, did not lie where he had been sitting. He dragged his robe over and lay down square across the doorway.

In the night the young man had to go out. He felt his way along the wall to the door and stepped, and his foot came down on the big man’s hair.

Who’s that treading on my hair?

It’s me, friend.

You come down out of the hills like a tramp and walk on a man’s head.

I didn’t know you were lying there. I’m sorry for it. He went out, and behind him he could hear the big man crying with rage, like a child.

The big man thought, he’ll walk on me coming in, too. Let him. He turned himself end for end and lay with his head where his feet had been. And the young man, coming back, thought, I hurt him the last time, I’ll go in past his feet. He stepped wide to the other side, and his foot came down on the big man’s neck.

Who is that?

It’s me.

First my hair and now my neck. I’ll put a curse on you.

Friend, I meant you no harm. I didn’t know you had turned. I came in this side to keep off you. Forgive me.

I’ll curse you.

Don’t do that.

The big man would hear nothing. He said it.

Tomorrow when the sun comes back over the hill, may your head split in seven pieces.

Now the young man had a temper of his own in those days, Murdo said. He answered, I told you it was no fault of mine, and you have cursed me all the same. Then let it fall on the one who is at fault and leave the other alone. And he said the same words back. Tomorrow when the sun comes back over the hill, may your head split in seven pieces.

His grandmother on the North Shore had the two sights, and some of it had come down to him, though he had never asked for it. The words were hardly out when he saw where they would land. He saw the big man standing in the snow at noon with the sun on his face, and he saw what happened to his head. He was sorry then. He went out on the river ice in the dark and stood facing south, and asked that the sun be kept back.

He never told anyone what words he used. But the sun did not come.

V. The Pool

The same camp · the next day

Noon came. The whole camp stood out on the ice in whatever it had that was clean, looking at the hill, and the sky behind the hill never so much as turned yellow. They waited an hour. Then they went in a body to Captain Rask’s cabin, eighty men, and stood in front of his door and set up a noise.

Captain, the sun hasn’t come back, and you’re the judge here. Make it come up for us.

Rask was a fair man. He went back over the winter in his own mind, what he had ruled and said and thought, the claims, the split of the flour, and he could find nothing crooked in it. Then he thought, there is a quarrel in this camp that I don’t know about. Has anybody come in lately?

Two men last night, Captain, off the hills. They’re in Potter’s cabin.

He went there with men carrying lanterns ahead of him at one o’clock in the afternoon, and found the two of them sitting against opposite walls. He took off his cap to the young one and sat down on a crate.

A man can’t see to work, Rask said. He can’t see to cut his wood. I’m asking you. Why has it gone dark on us?

The young man told him all of it. He cursed me, and I cursed him back. I said, let it fall on whichever of us is to blame. Then I looked to see where it would fall, and I saw that the minute the sun touched him, his head would go in seven pieces. I didn’t want that. So I’m keeping it back.

How does he get clear of it?

He asks my pardon. That’s all.

Then ask it, said Rask to the big man.

This tramp stood on my hair and he stood on my neck. I’ll not ask his pardon.

Ask it, man. Don’t be like this.

I will not.

Your head will split.

Let it. I will not ask his pardon.

No, said Rask. I can see you won’t. Not if it’s left to you.

He called four men in. They took the big man by the wrists and the ankles and the belt and the back of the neck and carried him across the floor, and bent him down until his forehead rested on the young man’s moccasins.

Get up, friend, the young man said. I forgive you.

But to Rask he said, He didn’t ask it of his own will, so it isn’t finished. There’s the warm spring above the camp, where the pool stays open. Take him there. Put a lump of clay from the bank on the top of his head, and stand him in the water up to his neck.

They did that. The clay at that spring is blue, and it froze on his head like a cap. The young man came and stood on the bank in the steam.

Friend, I’m going to let the sun come now. The moment it does, go under. Come up somewhere else, and go where you like.

The sun came over the hill a day late, at five minutes past one. The first of it struck across the pool and touched the lump of clay, and the clay cracked into seven pieces and fell in the water. The big man went under. He came up on the far side in the steam, where somebody had left a parka, and he was seen running down the river trail with it on, and nobody on the Koyukuk laid eyes on him again.

Some of the men from that camp are still in the country. They don’t agree about much. They agree that the sun was a day late in ’99.

VI. The Notch

Notch Creek · January 7 to 9, 1923

The kitchen was quiet. The cook had come to the door with the dog ladle in his hand and stayed there.

Who was the young one? said Billy Dunne.

A boy off the North Shore with more temper than sense. He has kept a roadhouse since. Rask died at Nome. The big man I never learned the name of. Murdo looked at his cousin. But I knew him, Norman, when you came off the scow. I knew the way he lay down.

Then he spoke to his cousin and to nobody else, though the rest of them heard it.

A man who goes on telling it over to himself, they insulted me, they laid hands on me, they got the better of me, they took what was mine, that man’s quarrel never gets done. It will be with him in the ground. And the man who leaves off telling it over, his gets done. That’s the whole of it, Norman. There is a text you know as well as I do. It was written for a country where the sun goes down every night.

Be ye angry, and sin not: let not the sun go down upon your wrath

— Ephesians 4:26

Up here it has been down since November. A man can keep his wrath a long while on this creek.

It was a Sunday. He sang then, standing by the flour bin with his cap in his hand, the way he had stood under the pulpit on the North Shore. He gave it in the English of the Scottish book so the room would have it. He gave out each line and waited, as if for the people to sing it after him, and the second time he waited, Hans Lien, who had been raised on other tunes entirely, hummed it back.

Do thou from anger cease, and wrath
see thou forsake also:
Fret not thyself in any wise,
that evil thou should’st do.

— Psalm 37:8, Scottish Psalter (1650)

The Major sat on the flour bin with his hands on his knees. He asked nobody’s pardon. Tom Skaggs, who had expected nothing else, went back into the big room and sat down on a bench, and after a minute Murdo went in and moved him to the birch chair.

•••

The ninth was clear, and fifty-one below. A little after noon they all went out and stood in the creek bed facing the Sawback, some forty people, with their breath going straight up. Billy Dunne held Murdo’s watch. The sky in the notch went from green to brass. At eleven minutes to one a point of light too bright to look at stood in the bottom of the gunsight, and their shadows fell out behind them on the snow, long and blue, the first shadows any of them had cast in six weeks. Nobody cheered. Mary laughed out loud, once. Tom Skaggs took off his cap to it.

The Major had come out last and stood apart, at the corner of the roadhouse, in his sealskin cap. When the light reached him, a few of them saw him put his hand up to the crown of his head and hold it there. Then the sun went behind the ridge, eleven minutes after it had come. The cold shut down again, and they went in to get ready for the dance.

Late that night a cousin of Jonas’s came in off the upriver trail, forty miles since morning, a man nobody had expected, with his eyelashes frozen together. The door opened on the fiddle and the frost smoke rolled across the floor. Murdo was at the far end of the room with a tray. Billy Dunne was dancing with Mrs. Ahlquist.

The Major was nearest. He was sitting on the bench by the door, because Tom Skaggs had the chair. He looked at the man in the doorway. He looked down the room at his cousin, who was not looking at him.

Then he got up. It took him some time. He said nothing to the man, no welcome and no word about the cold. He took the mitts off the man’s hands, one and then the other, and carried them across to the stove, and hung them on the drying pole with the palms turned to the heat, the way he had seen it done.

The verses

"He abused me, he beat me, he defeated me, he robbed me,"--in those who harbour such thoughts hatred will never cease.Dhammapada 3, tr. F. Max Müller (1881)
"He abused me, he beat me, he defeated me, he robbed me,"--in those who do not harbour such thoughts hatred will cease.Dhammapada 4, tr. F. Max Müller (1881)

In the commentary, Tissa is the Buddha’s older cousin, his father’s sister’s son, who becomes a monk as a fat old man and takes to sitting in the middle of the monastery hall in well-pressed robes. Visiting monks mistake him for a great elder and offer to wait on him. When a young monk learns that he has not kept a single rains retreat, which is how monks count seniority, he rebukes him and snaps his fingers. Tissa goes weeping to the Buddha, who asks whether he rose, took the visitors’ things, brought water or seats, or rubbed their feet, and then tells him to ask their pardon. He refuses. The Buddha then tells of the ascetic Devala, who lay down across a doorway, was stepped on twice by the ascetic Nārada, and cursed him: at sunrise his head should split in seven. Nārada, seeing that the curse would fall on Devala himself, kept the sun from rising until the king forced Devala to bow, and then saved him with a lump of clay and a pond. The upper Koyukuk, 1923

The Koyukuk drains the south slope of the Brooks Range, north of the Arctic Circle. In the summer of 1898, overflow from the Klondike rush went up it by steamboat; one stampeder later remembered sixty-eight small steamers frozen in that autumn with nine hundred people aboard, most of whom walked out over the ice (the account is quoted in the National Park Service history of the region, Gaunt Beauty, Tenuous Life). The camps that lasted were far upriver at Coldfoot and Wiseman, supplied by horse-drawn scows from Bettles in summer and by a monthly dog-team mail in winter. By the early 1920s only about a hundred white miners remained, living beside Koyukon Athabascan and Kobuk Iñupiaq families, many of whom had ties to the Episcopal mission of St. John’s-in-the-Wilderness at Allakaket. The forester Robert Marshall described the district a few years later in Arctic Village. A later chapter of the same history covers the 1920s. Notch Creek, its roadhouse and its ridge are invented. In a camp under a ridge at that latitude the sun stays hidden for weeks in midwinter, and its return was the great day of the year. A newcomer, or cheechako, became a sourdough by staying through a winter.

Many of the Gaelic-speaking Presbyterians of Cape Breton came from the Highlands and Hebrides in the early nineteenth century and kept the old way of singing the metrical psalms without instruments, a precentor giving out each line and the congregation singing it back, a practice called lining out. The psalm Murdo sings is from the Scottish Psalter of 1650, the English metrical version approved by the Church of Scotland and still in use. The verse from Ephesians is given in the King James Version.
