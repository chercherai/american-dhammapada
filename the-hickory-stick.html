The Hickory Stick

we must not inflict pain, harm or sorrow upon any one
— Dordrecht Confession (1632), art. XIV

Wie ich’s gheert hab, at the wall of the Salford burying ground.

I. The Wall

Lower Salford, Montgomery County · March 1828

They have put you in the row by your father, Velte, and the clay is so wet it shines. I am standing outside the wall because I have stood outside it for fifty-two years and my legs do not know another place to stand. Your brother Abraham’s widow is inside, by the hole, with her hands in her apron. She saw me come up the lane. She knows what I have under my shawl. She is the only one living who does.

Nobody in there will say anything over you that is about you. That is your people’s way, and it was my mother’s, and it was mine until I was nineteen and married Philip Schlosser at the Reformed church on the Skippack Road. A preacher read. They sang a long hymn out of the thick book, lined out, one line and then all of them after it. Then the men took up the shovels. The boards they let you down on were planed. That is the whole of it. You were seventy-three.

I have things to say to you that I did not say on the road, because on the road you never once turned around.

II. The Road

The Great Road below the Swamp · April 1778

That spring the English had the city and our army was starving at the Valley Forge, twenty miles off, and the militia under General Lacey was riding the roads between the two rivers to stop farmers from carrying anything into Philadelphia, where the English paid in hard money. Philip was a sergeant in it. He was a smith by trade, and he shod the militia horses for nothing, and on his nights home he talked about the Mennists.

Non-jurors, he called them, because they would not take the Test oath, and Tories, which they were not, and other things. Your people paid the fines for not mustering. Your people would not take Continental paper if they could help it. Most of them would not pay the war tax either, and up at Franconia there was trouble over Preacher Funk, who said they should. Philip had all this by heart. He said it at the table with his mouth full.

On the fourteenth of April he came home and told me, as a good joke, that he and four men had stopped a wagon on the Salford road that morning going down to the city with butter and eggs, and the wagon belonged to an old Mennist widow, and they had made her stand and watch while they broke every egg in the road and tipped the butter in the ditch, and the dogs had come.

It was my mother’s wagon. He knew it. That was the joke.

I don’t remember what I said. I remember he went out to the forge, and I put on my cloak and walked out the other door, and I set my face toward Salford, eleven miles, because I meant to go home to her and not come back.

It was not a road for a woman alone that spring. There were deserters from both armies in the woods, and foragers, and men who said they were one and were the other. A mile below our place I saw a man walking ahead of me with an axe on his shoulder and a bundle hung from the helve. A broad hat. A plain coat with nothing on it worth stealing. I knew what he was by his back, the way you know your own people by their backs even after you have left them: he walked without looking to the side at anything, as if the road were a row he was hoeing.

So I kept behind him. Thirty steps, then twenty. I thought, if anything comes out of the woods, it will see a man with an axe first.

He never looked around. I want you to know that I watched for it. Six miles, and you never turned your head, not when my shoe scraped, not when I coughed to see if you would. I have thought since that you knew I was there the whole way, the way you know the weather is behind you, and that it was nothing to you either way, and I was sore about that for a while, if you want the truth. A woman likes to be looked at, even by a Mennist.

III. The Axe

The same road, toward evening

Philip came up behind us at the ford below the mill, riding one of the militia horses. He had his hickory stick, the one he carried for dogs, as thick as a woman’s wrist at the knob end and worn dark where his hand went.

He did not say anything to me. He rode past me and pulled the horse across the road in front of you and got down.

Where are you taking her.

You looked at him. You did not look at me even then.

I said, Philip, he never looked at me. He never said one word. I walked behind him on my own. Leave him be.

So you took yourself off, he said, behind a Mennist. And then, and I have heard it every day since: Then he can have what you have coming.

Velte, I have told this to nobody but Barbara and my own daughter, and now you.

You took the axe off your shoulder. Philip stepped back, and I thought, God help us, now. And you bent and laid it down in the road. You did not drop it. You laid it down the way my mother laid a loaf on the board, with the bit turned toward the ditch and the helve along the wheel rut, where no hand could come at it quickly, not his, not yours. You put the bundle beside it. Then you stood up and let your hands hang.

He beat you across the back and shoulders. Then the arms. You went down on one knee once in the mud of the ford and got up again, and you did not put up your hands, and you did not make a sound I could hear over his breathing. I screamed at him. He hit you until he was tired, which was a long time, because he was a smith.

Then he put me up on the horse in front of him like a sack and turned for home. I looked back. You were standing in the road bareheaded, looking at nothing, the way a man looks at the sky to see if it will rain. Then you bent and picked up your hat and your bundle and your axe, and went on south.

Philip and I did not talk about it. Not that night, not in thirty-three years. I stayed. I am not telling you this to be pitied. I had one child by then and another coming. I stayed, and I learned the price of butter, and I learned which way to stand at the table.

IV. What Barbara Told Me

Abraham Hunsberger’s kitchen, Lower Salford · that night

Barbara told me the rest in 1811, at my mother’s burying, when I had come back for the first time and stood at this same wall. She came out to me through the gate. She said, You are the one from the ford, and I said I was, and she told it to me the way you would hand a woman back something she had dropped.

You came in after dark. Abraham was at the table, and your brothers Jacob and Christian, and Barbara at the fire. You had been the whole winter in the Swamp, cutting cordwood for your aunt Anna, a widow on a timber lot past the Milford line with nobody to cut for her, and they had not seen you since the first snow. You set the axe in its corner. You said you would eat later. You said you had to go over to the old preacher’s in the morning and tell him something.

Barbara saw the blood come through your shirt when you sat.

She got the shirt off you, and it was stuck. She said the weals stood up on your back like the ribs in corduroy, so many that she started counting to keep her hands steady, and stopped at thirty because there was no more back to count on. She warmed goose grease in a saucer and put it on with two fingers while the boys held the candle.

Who did this.

You told them. The ford, the woman behind, the smith with the stick. You told it plainly, as if you were telling where you had seen a fox.

Jacob said, And what did you say to him?

There was nothing to say.

But were you angry? Velte. When he was doing it. Were you angry at him?

No.

Barbara said the kitchen went still. You have to understand, she said to me, it was worse than the beating. In that house a man’s yea was yea and his nay was nay, without any oath to hold it up. That was the whole of why your people would not swear to the Test, why they were fined and called Tories and had their eggs broken in the road. Their word was the oath. And here was Velte, home from the woods, saying No to something any child in Salford knew could not be no. Abraham said it first. He is lying. Christian said it. And Jacob, who loved you best, said, Velte, it is a sin to say that if it is not true. Say you were angry and be done.

You said, I was not angry, and went up to bed.

In the morning the three of them went across the fields ahead of you, to old Hannes Detweiler.

V. The Loom

The weaver’s house on the Indian Creek road · the next morning

Barbara went with them, because she had the saucer of goose grease in her apron and meant to take it on to you after. So she heard it.

Detweiler was past seventy. He had been a weaver all his life and a preacher since 1742, when the lot fell on him: the slip of paper in the hymnbook, the one with the verse on it, The lot is cast into the lap. He used to say the lot had made a mistake and been too proud to take it back. He was at his loom when they came in, throwing the shuttle, and he did not stop.

Abraham told it. The smith, the ford, thirty weals and more. And then: He says he was not angry. We think he is lying. We have come to you because it is a lie in the face of the whole congregation, and we do not know what to do with a brother who lies about a thing like that.

The old man threw the shuttle and beat the weft up with the batten and threw it back.

Was it you, he said, who sent him to the Swamp? Or was it me?

It was you.

It was me. In November. He came here with his fists like this. He showed them, without letting go of the shuttle. He had been in the road when the militia took the Bergeys’ horses, and he wanted to hit somebody, and he was frightened of how much. So I gave him your aunt’s cordwood and one thing to do with it. I said, Velte, when you lift the axe, know that you are lifting it. When you lay it down, lay it down. Every stroke. Not one stroke without knowing it. He said, That is all? I said, That is enough for a winter.

He threw the shuttle.

He laid it down in the Swamp, he said. Somewhere in March, I would think. Ask him the day. He has come home to tell me, and I can see from here he does not need to. What he picked up off the road yesterday was an axe. It was only an axe.

Then he stopped the loom, Barbara said, which he did not do for anybody, and got down the little book of the Confession from the shelf where it lay with his Testament, the confession the ministers of this country had owned for theirs in 1725, and found the place without looking long, and read it out to the four of them in the German, standing, as if they were all in meeting.

From this we understand that therefore, and according to His example, we must not inflict pain, harm or sorrow upon any one, but seek the highest welfare and salvation of all men … that we must not harm any one, and, when we are smitten, rather turn the other cheek also, than take revenge or retaliate.

— Dordrecht Confession (1632), art. XIV, “Of Revenge”

We all say that, he told them. Sundays. We have it by heart and we pay the fines for it. Your brother has got to where it is true of him, that is the only difference, and it is a hard thing to see in somebody you grew up with, so you call it a lie. He did not lie. Go home. Abraham, put him on light work until his back closes. Barbara, you have something in your apron for him.

They went home across the wheat. You were in the yard splitting kindling with your shirt off because of the grease, and the weals were turning the color of plums, and Barbara said you lifted the hatchet, and brought it down, and set it on the block, and lifted it, so slowly that the boys, who had come to say something to you, stood at the fence and could not think what it had been.

VI. The Stick

The Salford burying ground · March 1828

Philip died in the fall of 1811 at the forge, of his heart, with the tongs in his hand. I buried him in the Reformed ground on the Skippack Road under a stone with his name on it and a willow, because that is what we do.

The stick hung behind the forge door for thirty-three years. He never carried it again after that April. I do not say that means anything. He did not say. Some days I thought he was ashamed of it, and some days I thought he was keeping it the way a man keeps a gun he has used. When he died I took it into the house. My daughter wanted it for the fire. I said no. I have had it in the corner by my bed for seventeen years, and every winter I have asked myself why, and the answer I have come to is that I was waiting to give it back to somebody, and there was only ever one person it could go to.

The men are filling you in now. Barbara is looking at me. She is near eighty and she still holds her apron the same way, as if there were a saucer in it.

I am going to go in at the gate, Velte, the first time since I was nineteen. Nobody will stop me. Your people do not stop anybody; that was always the trouble with them, Philip said. I am going to walk up the row to the new clay and lay this down on it, the knob end toward your father’s stone and the rest along the length of you, the way you laid your axe along the wheel rut at the ford. Then I am going to walk back out and down the lane and home, and there will be nobody in front of me, and I will not need anybody to be.

There are no flowers in this ground and no willows, and the stones are all one height. It will be the only thing lying on any grave here, a dark old stick worn smooth at one end by a hand, and I do not think anybody will pick it up.

Background

The verse

Him I call indeed a Brahmana who finds no fault with other beings, whether feeble or strong, and does not kill nor cause slaughter.Dhammapada 405, tr. F. Max Müller (1881)

In the commentary, a monk takes a meditation subject from the Buddha into the forest and becomes an arahant. Walking back to tell the Teacher, he passes through a village where a wife has just quarreled with her husband and set out for her family’s house; she follows close behind the monk, who never once looks at her. The husband catches up, decides the monk has taken her, and beats him badly while she protests that the monk never looked at her or spoke. Back at the monastery, the monks who rub his weals ask whether he got angry. He says he did not, and they go to the Buddha to report that he is lying. The Buddha answers that those free of the pollutants have laid down the rod and feel no anger even toward those who strike them. Read the full commentary story.

The Mennonites of the Skippack and Salford, 1777–1778

The Swiss and South German Mennonites who settled the Skippack, Salford and Franconia country north of Philadelphia had in 1725 adopted the Dutch Dordrecht Confession of 1632 as their own; its articles on revenge and on oaths bound them to refuse both the sword and the swearing of oaths. The Revolution tested both. Pennsylvania’s Test Act of June 1777 required an oath or affirmation of allegiance and stripped those who refused of many civil rights, and men who would not muster paid fines. The Franconia Conference divided in 1778 when the minister Christian Funk, who argued for paying the war tax, was put out of office. The quotation in the story is from the English translation of the Confession by Joseph F. Sohm, printed with the Martyrs Mirror in 1886. In Franconia practice, ministers were chosen by lot, with a slip of paper hidden in one of several hymnbooks.

In the fall of 1777 the British took Philadelphia, and Washington’s army spent the winter at Valley Forge. In January 1778 the Pennsylvania Supreme Executive Council made John Lacey a brigadier general of militia, and his men patrolled the country between the Delaware and the Schuylkill to keep farmers from carrying produce into the occupied city, where the British paid in hard money. On May 1, 1778, British troops surprised Lacey’s camp at the Battle of Crooked Billet. The Hunsbergers, Detweiler, the Schlossers and the egg wagon in the story are fictional.
