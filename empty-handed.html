Empty-Handed

So long as a man clingeth unto the elements and fragments of this world … and holdeth converse with them … he is deceived and blinded.
— Theologia Germanica XIX

Zo heb ik het gehoord, on the Bohemia River.

I. The Stoep

Midwout (Flatbush), Long Island · spring 1702

Teunis Joosten was a cooper at Midwout, and he and his wife Marritje had buried four children in the churchyard there and kept one. The one was Joost, seventeen, who could already raise a tight barrel and who had never in his life been out of sight of both of them at once.

In April two men came up from the Bohemia River in Maryland to sell linen thread in New York, and a neighbor who had dealings with them asked Teunis to give them a bed. They were brethren of the Labadie house on the Manor, plain men in coarse cloth. Marritje fed them. When they had eaten, the older one took off his hat and sat. After a while the younger took off his. Neither said a word for half a quarter of an hour, and the family, not knowing what was wanted, sat too. Then the older man put his hat on and said a few words to the table, low, as if they were for the bread. He said that everyone under this roof had been lent to the others for a little while, that the Lender was good, and that a heart could be set where nothing was ever called back.

Teunis thought it a long grace for after supper. Marritje thought they looked underfed. Joost did not sleep, and in the morning, when the two men had gone down to the ferry, he asked leave to follow them and go in.

No, said his father.

They put the men on one side and the women on the other, said his mother. They take your goods and your name. Old Herrman's son went in to them and left his wife, and his father cursed him for it, and he was dead inside two years.

Joost said no more about it, which frightened them worse than if he had argued. After that, whenever Teunis went to the shop he said to Marritje, Keep him where you can see him, and whenever Marritje went to a neighbor's she walked the boy to the shop door and said the same to Teunis.

On a morning in May Teunis had staves to buy at the landing. Marritje said to herself that she would keep the boy, and keep him well. The house had a back door that had been nailed up since the winter and a front door that gave on the stoep. She sat down in the front door, on the sill itself, set one foot against one doorpost and the other foot against the other, took up her distaff and began to spin.

Joost stood behind her a while watching the spindle drop and climb.

Moeder. Draw your foot back a little. I have to go out to the necessary.

She drew her foot back. He stepped over her thread and went around the corner of the house. She set her foot against the post again and spun perhaps two ells before it came to her that the necessary was on the other side.

II. Gone In

Bohemia Manor, Cecil County, Maryland · summer 1702

Where is he? Teunis said, with the staves still on his shoulder.

He was here but a moment ago.

He looked in the loft and the shop and the church. Then he said, The Manor, and he found a sloop bound for the South River, and at New Castle he took the cart road that old Herrman had cut across to the Bohemia, twenty miles through oak woods, on foot.

The Labadie house stood on a neck of good land above the river, a great plain dwelling with its barns, and around it the plantation: corn, flax and hemp, cattle, and tobacco. The tobacco was worked by Africans the house had bought. The two brethren who had first come spying out the country had written down their horror of that trade, and of tobacco too, and their house now did both.

He found his son in a row of men pulling flax, in the coarse dress of the house, with his own good coat gone into the common chest. Teunis stood at the end of the row and wept like a child. Joost. Joost, why do you undo me?

The boy went red to the ears and went on pulling. Nobody stopped work. Teunis wept until he was done, and sat down on the headland, and looked at his son's back going away from him up the row. After a time a thought came to him that he had not expected.

With the boy in there, what is a cooper at Midwout? Who am I hooping casks for?

He went up to the house that same day and asked to be received. The rule was known to everyone on the river. Whoever came in, rich or poor, put what he had into the common stock, and whoever afterward had a mind to leave must leave what he had brought and go out empty-handed. Teunis signed over the shop, the tools and the house at Midwout by a letter carried north with the next skipper. They gave him the cooper's shed, where the hogsheads for the tobacco were made, and a place on the men's side.

Marritje waited at Midwout. A week went by, and another. Then a stranger came with a paper and looked at her house the way a man looks at a house he means to buy, and she understood where both of them were.

With my son gone in and my husband gone in, she said to the stranger, who had not asked, what would I keep a door for?

She took the distaff, since nobody had bought that, and went south, and was received on the women's side.

III. The Retting Pond

The same · August and September

They were upwards of a hundred in that family, men, women and children. The men ate at one long table and the women at another, in a different room. An English Friend who was given supper with the men about that time wrote down how it went: when the food was in, one man pulled off his hat, and after a space another, and so on down the benches until all sat bare-headed and silent, each praying when he was moved to and not before, and then the hats went back on one by one and each man fell to eating without regard for those still uncovered. The children were taught together under a tutor. It was told of the house in its first years that a mother who kissed her own child there had been rebuked for showing so naturally her fleshly cleavings, and that the cells had been kept without fire in winter while wood was burned in the fields to be rid of it. Some of that had slackened. The tables had not.

For three days Teunis, Marritje and Joost kept the order of the house. On the fourth, at the hour after the noon meal when a brother might walk by himself and examine his heart, the three of them walked by themselves to the same place.

The place was the bank of the retting pond. The pulled flax lay sunk there in bundles under stones, rotting loose from its own straw, and the stink of it carried to the tobacco barns, so that nobody in the house went near the pond who was not sent. They sat down on the bank in a row with Marritje in the middle and they talked. They talked about who had bought the house at Midwout and whether he would keep the pear tree. About the dominie's wife. About whether the gray cloth itched Joost as it itched his father, and whether he had enough at the men's table, where his mother could not see his plate, and whether that cough was the flax dust or something on his chest. Marritje took his head in her two hands and kissed him on the hair, there in the open, and looked him over for ticks.

They were there the next day at the same hour, and at dusk too, and after that at every hour that was not counted. When they were set to work in three different places they found errands that crossed. Teunis would look down the men's bench at supper to see whether the boy had bread, and so his hat was always the last one off.

The house could not bear it. Some were angry for the rule's sake. But there were women at the sisters' table who had left husbands in Amsterdam to come out of the world, and men who had not been let to carry their own children across the yard, and they had made what peace they could with that, and now three people sat on the pond bank every afternoon holding on to each other as if nobody had ever asked them for anything. A widow from Friesland, with two sons dead in the colony at Surinam, stopped eating.

Pieter Sluyter had the rule of the house, and by then the best of its land in his own name, and most complaints went up to him. This one the brethren and sisters carried to old Geertje Wybes instead. It was not a matter that mortifying would mend, and they knew it.

IV. The Cell

The sisters' side · an evening in September

Geertje Wybes was past seventy. As a young wife in Amsterdam she had heard Jean de Labadie preach, and she had followed his people to Herford and Altona and at last to the castle at Wieuwerd in Friesland, where she kept the sickroom of Juffrouw van Schurman, the most learned woman in Europe, until that lady died. Then she had crossed the ocean with the first company. It was said in the house that the Father's hearth was never cold. Hers had never been lit, and nobody had told her to keep it so. She spun flax all day by the one window and owned the stool she sat on only in the sense that no one else wanted it.

She sent for the three of them. They stood in her cell in a row.

They tell me you sit on the pond bank every day and talk, the three of you. Is it true?

It is true, moeder, said Teunis.

Why do you do it? It is not the way of people who have come out.

Teunis turned his hat around in his hands.

We had five. Four are in the churchyard at Midwout. This one we counted every night of his life, before the candle was put out, the way you count the one coin you have. Moeder Geertje, we cannot live apart. We would keep your rule. We do not know how to be where we cannot see him.

Nor he us, Marritje said. Joost looked at the floor and did not say she was wrong.

The old woman let the wheel run down.

I left a husband in Amsterdam in the year 'sixty-nine, because he would not come, and a girl of nine with him. I have not seen either one since. I know what it is not to see them. I know it this evening. And I have sat thirty years at a table with women I would not have chosen for neighbors, and to have to look at those is a pain as well. It took me a long time to find out that it is the same nail. Whether you must look or cannot look, the heart is hung on a creature, and it tears when the creature moves, and they all move.

She had one book besides the Scriptures, the little German Theology in a Dutch printing with the boards gone, which had come with her from Amsterdam to Herford and from Friesland over the sea. She found the place without her spectacles. It was where the book takes up the hard saying about hating father and mother.

He meaneth it thus: “He who doth not forsake and part with everything, can never know My eternal truth, nor attain unto My life.”

But so long as a man clingeth unto the elements and fragments of this world (and above all to himself), and holdeth converse with them, and maketh great account of them, he is deceived and blinded, and perceiveth what is good no further than as it is most convenient and pleasant to himself and profitable to his own ends.

— Theologia Germanica XIX

Elements and fragments, she said. A husband is one. A girl of nine is one. That is all I have to tell you. And she set the wheel going.

In that house anyone moved to it might speak at the evening exercise. Geertje Wybes almost never did. That night she stood up among the sisters and said to the whole family what she had said in the cell, the husband and the girl of nine and the table and the nail, and read the passage out. It is told that the widow from Friesland ate her supper afterward, and that a good many in the room, who had given up as much as she had and had only gritted their teeth over it, dated their peace from that evening.

V. The Cart Road

The road to New Castle · the next morning

The three sat on the pond bank one more time, before light, and nobody had to say much.

She is right, Teunis said. I heard it and I knew she was right. And at supper I was looking down the bench again to see had he got bread.

I watched her spin, said Marritje. She has a daughter somewhere who is past forty and she does not know if the girl has a tooth in her head. I could not do it. I do not want to be able to do it.

Joost had been in the house four months. He looked a long time at the row of men going out to the hemp with their hoes on their shoulders, not one of them turning his head to see who was beside him. Then he looked at his mother's hands.

Nor I, he said. Not yet.

They went up and told the house they were leaving. The rule held for going as it had for coming. The shop at Midwout, the tools, the money from the house and forty years of barrels stayed in the common stock. They were given back the clothes they had walked in with. Marritje asked for her distaff, and was told it had been put to use on the sisters' side, and she let it go.

Geertje Wybes did not come to the door. She had flax on the wheel.

They went down old Herrman's road toward New Castle with nothing to carry, three abreast where the track was wide enough, Marritje in the middle with a hand in each of theirs. Walking that way, the three of them had only two empty hands among them, the two on the outside.

The verses

He who gives himself to vanity, and does not give himself to meditation, forgetting the real aim (of life) and grasping at pleasure, will in time envy him who has exerted himself in meditation.Dhammapada 209, tr. F. Max Müller (1881)
Let no man ever look for what is pleasant, or what is unpleasant. Not to see what is pleasant is pain, and it is pain to see what is unpleasant.Dhammapada 210, tr. F. Max Müller (1881)
Let, therefore, no man love anything; loss of the beloved is evil. Those who love nothing and hate nothing, have no fetters.Dhammapada 211, tr. F. Max Müller (1881)

In the commentary, the only son of a household at Sāvatthī hears monks give thanks after a meal and wants to go forth. His parents refuse and guard him by turns. One day his mother sits in the doorway spinning, a foot against each doorpost; he asks her to move her foot so he can relieve himself, and runs to the monastery. His father follows, weeps, and becomes a monk himself. His mother follows and becomes a nun. But the three cannot stay apart, and spend their days sitting and talking together, until the monks and nuns complain. The Buddha tells them this is not how those who have gone forth should live, and speaks the three verses. Many who hear them are helped. Bohemia Manor, 1702

The Labadists were followers of Jean de Labadie, a former Jesuit turned Reformed preacher, who gathered a community of the "elect" that held all goods in common and lived apart from the world. Its best-known member was the scholar Anna Maria van Schurman, who died in the mother house at Wieuwerd in Friesland in 1678. In 1683 two envoys, Peter Sluyter and Jasper Danckaerts, obtained some 3,750 acres of Augustine Herrman's Bohemia Manor in Cecil County, Maryland, and founded a daughter colony that grew to a little over a hundred people, many of them Dutch converts from New York. Members surrendered their property on entering and left empty-handed; men and women ate at separate tables; marriage and parenthood were subordinated to the house. The supper with the hats is described by the English Quaker minister Samuel Bownas, who visited in the first years of the 1700s. The rebuke for "fleshly cleavings" and the fireless cells come from a hostile former member, Peter Dittelbach, writing in 1692. Both are quoted in Bartlett B. James, The Labadist Colony in Maryland (1899).

Danckaerts's journal of 1679–80 condemns both tobacco and slavery, but the Maryland house grew tobacco with enslaved African labor. In 1698 Sluyter divided most of the tract among the leading members and kept a neck of land for himself; he died wealthy in 1722, and within a few years the community was gone. The cooper's family and Geertje Wybes are invented. The Theologia Germanica, an anonymous German treatise of the fourteenth century that Luther sent to the press, was read by Protestant pietists for centuries afterward; it is quoted here in Susanna Winkworth's English of 1854.
