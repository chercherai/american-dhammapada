Walking the American WayCommentary 6.7 · Kāṇamātā

Kaneez’s Mother

Verily in the remembrance of Allah do hearts find rest!
— Qur’an 13:28

Main ne yun suna hai, under the 7 train.

I. Malpure

Corona, Queens · May 1989

On the block nobody called her Zubaida. Since the day her daughter was born in Gujranwala twenty-three years before, she had been Kaneez ki Ammi, Kaneez’s mother, and when she came to America in 1975 with her husband and the girl and two suitcases tied with rope, the name came with her, the way names do. Her husband drove a cab for eleven years and died of his heart in the front seat of it on the Grand Central Parkway. She kept the two-family house off Roosevelt Avenue that he had bought with the cab money, and rented the top floor to a family from Guayaquil, and sewed shalwar kameez for half the Pakistani brides in Jackson Heights at a machine in her front window, where she could see the street.

Her rule about the door came from her own mother. Nobody who knocked at it went away with empty hands. Not the Jehovah’s Witnesses, not the boys selling candy for their basketball team, not the man who came around every month for the Con Edison meter. She would give them a samosa in a paper napkin or a cup of tea or an orange from the bowl. Kaneez had been embarrassed by it all through high school.

In February Ammi slipped on the ice on the front steps and broke her wrist, and Kaneez came home from Brooklyn to help her. Kaneez had been married two years by then, to a boy named Tariq whose parents had a house in Brooklyn and who drove a cab, as her father had, out of the same garage in Long Island City. It was supposed to be two weeks. Then the cast came off, and the wrist was weak, and there were brides waiting for their clothes, and then it was Ramadan and you could not leave your mother alone in Ramadan, and then it was Eid, and somehow it was May.

After Eid Tariq called. Enough. Come home.

She can’t go back to her in-laws empty-handed, Ammi said to him on the phone, after three months. What will your mother say? Give me one day. I’ll make malpure. Tariq’s mother was from Gujranwala too, and she was famous in two boroughs for loving malpure, and for complaining that nobody in America knew how to make them.

So on Monday morning Ammi made the batter the way her mother had: flour and milk and sugar and a little semolina, crushed fennel, cardamom, left to stand an hour. Then she fried them one by one in ghee in the big karahi, each one a lacy brown disc the size of a saucer, crisp at the edges and soft in the middle, and lifted them out and dipped them in syrup and laid them in rows on her best steel tray, and the smell went out through the screen door and down the block and under the El.

The doorbell rang.

It was five men in white. They wore long kurtas and loose trousers that stopped above the ankle and white caps, and four of them had beards. One was an old Guyanese man with a cane. One was a Bangladeshi boy who could not have been nineteen. They were a jama’at, a party of travelers, staying three days at the new masjid on the other side of Corona, the one that had been built from the ground up to be a masjid, the first in the city. Every afternoon they went out on gasht, door to door through the Muslim streets with a man from the neighborhood to show them the way, to invite the brothers to come and pray Maghrib with them.

Assalamu alaikum, Ammi-ji, said the young one. Is there a man of the house, to come to the masjid tonight?

There was no man of the house. But there was the tray.

Ammi stood in her doorway with her hand on the frame and the smell of the malpure coming out past her into the street, and the men in white standing on her steps in the heat, and she could no more have closed that door on them than she could have flown. She went to the kitchen and wrapped the whole tray in a clean cloth and brought it out and put it in the young one’s hands.

The jama’ats had a rule, which the old ones taught the young ones. You ask nobody for anything. You pay your own way and carry your own bedding and cook your own food. But if something is offered with an open hand, you take it, and you thank God for it, because to refuse a gift is to refuse the giver. So they took it. The old Guyanese man made a long dua on the steps for her and her house and her daughter, and they went off down the block under the El with the steel tray going ahead of them like a lantern.

Ammi, said Kaneez from the kitchen.

I’ll make more tomorrow, said Ammi. Call Tariq. Tell him Wednesday.

II. Four Trays

Two weeks in May

On Wednesday she made them again. The batter, the hour, the ghee, the syrup, the rows on the tray. A second tray, her other good one, the one with the handles. The doorbell rang at half past four. It was a different jama’at, from Toronto this time, six men, staying the week, and their guide was the same young man from the neighborhood who had brought the first ones, and he looked at Ammi and at the tray in the kitchen behind her with a face that said he was very sorry and also that he had smelled it from the corner.

She gave it to them.

Tariq’s mother called on Thursday and talked to Ammi for half an hour in a voice Kaneez could hear from across the room.

The next Monday it was four men from Chicago. The Friday after that it was a jama’at of students from Long Island, boys, with their caps on crooked, and one of them had a Walkman around his neck that he had forgotten to take off, and he put his hand over it when he saw Ammi looking. She gave each of them the whole tray. She could not help it. Each time she stood in the door and meant to say, Brothers, I’m sorry, these are for my daughter’s in-laws, and each time the smell went out past her into the street and the words did not come, and she went and wrapped the tray.

Kaneez did not say anything the fourth time. She stood in the kitchen doorway with her arms folded and watched the tray go down the steps.

On the first of June Tariq got on a plane to Lahore. Kaneez heard about it from her cousin in Flushing, who had heard it from his cousin in Brooklyn. He was going to marry his father’s brother’s daughter, a girl of nineteen from Gujranwala, whom the families had talked about for him before Kaneez, and he would bring her back to Brooklyn when the papers came through, and Kaneez could stay at her mother’s, since she liked it there so much.

Kaneez put the phone down very carefully, as if it might break.

Those beards, she said. Those beards ate my marriage.

III. Roosevelt Avenue

June and July 1989

After that Kaneez went a little crazy, the block said, and the block was not wrong.

She cursed them in the street. Any man in a white kurta and a white cap, any party of them going by in a line on gasht, any single brother coming down the stairs from the 103rd Street station with his sleeping bag on his back, she would go to the edge of the sidewalk and let him have it in Punjabi, the kind of Punjabi her mother had never let her learn and she had learned anyway. She called them beggars and thieves. She called them crows. She said they went door to door eating other people’s weddings. She said it so loud that the Colombian women at the bus stop turned around, and the man at the fruit stand stopped weighing, and the train went over and drowned her out, and she waited till it passed and said the rest.

The jama’ats got so they would not come down her street. The young man from the neighborhood who guided them on gasht would stop at the corner of Roosevelt and say, Not this block, brothers. We’ll go around, and they would go around, five or six men in white walking a long way out of their way in the July heat, and Kaneez would watch them from the front steps.

Ammi said nothing. She sewed in her window. Sometimes at night Kaneez could hear her in the kitchen, standing at the stove with nothing on it.

At the masjid the elders talked it over after Isha, as they talked over everything. She is grieving, said one. She is shaming us in the street, said another. Somebody should talk to her, said a third, and they all agreed, and nobody went, because none of them wanted to stand on that sidewalk and hear it.

Bhai Yusuf went.

IV. Bhai Yusuf

The house off Roosevelt Avenue · a morning in August

He was seventy-one years old. He had worked the A train as a conductor for twenty-nine years, from Inwood to Far Rockaway and back, and retired with a pension and a gold watch he gave to his grandson. He had taken his shahada in 1962 at the old mosque on State Street in Brooklyn, where the imam was a man from Grenada and the people praying were from Harlem and Yemen and Bengal and Trinidad, and since then he had been going out in the path of God with the jama’ats, three days a month and forty days a year, for longer than most of the brothers had been alive. He had been to Raiwind. He had been to Dewsbury and to Durban and to a mosque in a strip mall in Texas. His Urdu was terrible and he knew it, and the young men from Lahore loved him for it. They called him Bhai Yusuf, Brother Yusuf, or Chacha, uncle, and the Bangladeshi boys called him Nana.

He did not say much. When he did, it was short.

He rang the bell at nine in the morning and Ammi opened the door and knew him, everybody knew him, and put her hand to her chest. Then she brought him in and sat him at the kitchen table in her husband’s chair, and because there was no question of anything else she made him breakfast: parathas fried in ghee, an omelette with green chilies and onion, tea boiled with milk and sugar and cardamom in the pan. He ate all of it slowly and said alhamdulillah at the end and wiped his mustache with the napkin.

Where is Kaneez?

Chacha-ji, she saw you from the window, said Ammi. She is in the back room. She is crying.

Why is she crying?

Because of what she has been saying to the brothers in the street. All summer. She saw you come up the steps and she knew why you came.

He nodded. He sat a while. A train went over on the El at the end of the block and the glasses on the shelf rattled and went still.

Ask her to come, he said.

Kaneez came out after a long time with her dupatta pulled down over her forehead and her eyes red and stood by the refrigerator, and would not sit, and would not look at him. So Ammi told it. She told it all, the malpure and the trays, the four jama’ats, Tariq’s mother on the phone, the plane to Lahore, the girl of nineteen, all of it, standing by her own stove.

Bhai Yusuf listened to the whole thing. Then he turned to Ammi.

Sister. Those trays. Did the brothers take them from you? Or did you give them?

I gave them, Chacha-ji.

The brothers came to your door to invite people to the prayer. You put a tray in their hands. They said thank you and made dua for your house. What did they do wrong?

Ammi was quiet a moment. Then she said, Nothing. They did nothing wrong. It was me.

He turned to Kaneez by the refrigerator.

Daughter. You heard her. The brothers came to the door, and your mother gave, and they took what she gave them. What did they do wrong?

Kaneez opened her mouth to say it, the thing she had been saying all summer on Roosevelt Avenue, and it was not there. She looked for it and it was gone. She stood by the refrigerator with her hands open.

Nothing, she said. They did nothing. It was me.

Then she began to cry again, differently, and came and sat down at the table across from him, and asked him to forgive her, and to ask the brothers to forgive her, every one. He said that he forgave her and that they would. Then he told her a few things, not many. He said what her mother had done was the thing the Book praises, and he said it from the Book in English, in his conductor’s voice, the voice that had called out the stations from 207th Street to the Rockaways.

And feed with food the needy wretch, the orphan and the prisoner, for love of Him, (Saying): We feed you, for the sake of Allah only. We wish for no reward nor thanks from you;

— Qur’an 76:8–9

Your mother fed travelers for love of Him, he said. Four times. It cost her everything she had in the kitchen and her daughter’s marriage. And she didn’t want thanks. They gave her a dua on the steps and went off down the street. That’s the whole story. Nobody in it did anything wrong except the one who threw a marriage away for a tray of sweets, and he’s in Lahore.

Kaneez laughed, in the middle of crying, before she could stop herself.

He talked a little more. About what a person keeps and what a person doesn’t. About the three things that go on working for you after you are gone, which are what you gave away, and what you taught, and a child who prays for you, and nothing else, not a house, not a husband. Ordinary things. She had heard them all before, in the mosque and from her father, a hundred times. But a train went over at the end of the block, and the glasses on the shelf rattled, and then they stopped, and she sat there and felt herself go still the way the glasses did. All the way down. It was like looking into water that has been stirred up all summer and has finally settled, and seeing, for the first time, that it goes much deeper than you thought, and that it is clear all the way to the bottom.

Bhai Yusuf saw it. He did not say anything about it. He finished his tea and stood up and thanked Ammi for the breakfast, and went out and down the steps.

V. Chaudhry Sahib

Seventy-fourth Street, Jackson Heights · the same morning

He walked back toward the express stop under the El, the long way, because it was a fine morning and he liked to walk, and so he went past the office of Chaudhry Sahib.

Everybody knew Chaudhry Sahib. He had come in 1968 with a degree in engineering that nobody in New York would honor, and driven a cab, and bought a medallion, and then another, and now he owned forty of them and a travel agency on Seventy-fourth Street that sold more tickets to Karachi and Lahore than any other in the city, and two buildings, and a share of a sweet shop, and he paid for half of everything at the masjid and did not let anybody forget it. He was sitting in his office window doing the accounts when Bhai Yusuf went by, and he said to his nephew, Is that Bhai Yusuf? Go and tell him I’m coming out.

He came out onto the sidewalk in his shirtsleeves and embraced the old man and kissed his hand.

Chacha-ji. Where are you coming from so early?

From the house of Kaneez’s mother, in Corona.

Kaneez? The one who curses the jama’ats on Roosevelt Avenue? What were you doing there?

I heard she was cursing the brothers. So I went.

And? Did you stop her?

She stopped, said Bhai Yusuf. She’s rich now. Richer than you. She has the kind of wealth nobody can take from her.

Chaudhry Sahib looked at him a long time, there on the sidewalk under the El, with the people going around them. He was a proud man and not a fool.

All right, he said. You made her rich in what can’t be taken. I’ll make her rich in what can.

That Friday he sent his own car, the long grey Lincoln, to the house off Roosevelt Avenue for Kaneez and her mother. He had his wife dress Kaneez in a red and gold suit from the best shop on Seventy-fourth Street and put his own mother’s gold bangles on her wrists, and he brought her into the hall under the masjid after Jumu’ah, in front of everybody, and declared her his daughter. His munh-boli beti, his daughter by his own word, the eldest, with everything that went with it. Then he said, in front of everybody, If there is a man here who thinks he can keep my daughter, let him come and talk to me.

Tariq signed the papers from Lahore within the month. Chaudhry Sahib’s lawyer saw to that.

The man who came to talk to him was Rana Sahib, a widower of forty with grown sons, who owned the halal meat wholesaler in Woodside and the garage in Long Island City and a piece of most other things in Queens that had a Pakistani name on them, and sat on every committee, and had a hand in everything. He married Kaneez in October. On the night of the wedding he gave her the keys to the big house in Fresh Meadows, with its two kitchens, and the checkbook, and said, Do whatever good you like with it. I won’t ask.

VI. Four Doors

Fresh Meadows · 1990

She did whatever good she liked.

She hired two cooks from Lahore and kept both kitchens going from Fajr to Isha. She paid four boys from the neighborhood, students, five dollars a day each, to stand at the doors of four masjids, in Corona and Jackson Heights and Astoria and Flushing, and ask every jama’at that came through whether they had eaten, and if they had not, to bring them to her house. Five men, ten men, twenty. Brothers from Leicester and Johannesburg and Trinidad and Dhaka, sleeping bags on their backs, sitting on the floor of her living room on white sheets she had spread over the carpet, eating biryani and nihari and her mother’s malpure, and the food went out of her door and down the walk and onto the sidewalk in foil trays for anybody passing, like a river that had come up over its banks.

And it was never enough. That was the thing she said to the cooks, and to Rana Sahib, and to her mother, who had moved in with them and sat at a sewing machine in the front window where she could see the street. There aren’t enough of them. I can’t find enough of them. She sent the boys out again. She called the masjid in Corona and asked when the next jama’at was coming from Toronto.

At the masjid the brothers talked it over after Isha, as they talked over everything.

Last summer four jama’ats ate her mother’s trays and she cursed the whole lot of us in the street. We had to walk around her block. Now she can’t find enough of us. Chacha went to her house one time and ate breakfast.

Bhai Yusuf was sitting against the back wall with his legs out in front of him and his cane across his lap.

It’s not the first time, he said. They all turned around. Not the first time those four got her into trouble. Not the first time I got her out.

So they asked him, and he told them.

•••

When he was twenty-two, he said, before the army, he swept the floor for an old diamond cutter on Forty-seventh Street, a Jewish man just over from Antwerp, in a room four flights up with a north window. And in the wall of that room there lived a mouse. She was an old mouse, and she had come with the building, and her people had been rich once, and she was sitting on what was left of it somewhere behind the plaster, all by herself. One day she came out across the floor with a nickel in her mouth and put it down by his broom.

What’s this, Mother? he said.

Buy yourself something, said the mouse. And buy me a little meat.

So he went down to the deli on Sixth Avenue and bought her a slice of pastrami, and every day after that she brought him a nickel and he brought her a slice, and they were friends. Then one day a cat from the alley caught her. Don’t eat me, said the mouse. Are you hungry just today, or every day? Every day, said the cat. Then let me go, and I’ll feed you every day. So she gave the cat half her pastrami. Then a second cat caught her, and a third, and a fourth, and by the end she was cutting one slice of pastrami five ways and she was nothing but fur and bones.

He saw it, and asked her, and she told him. Why didn’t you say so? he said. The diamond cutter had a block of rock crystal on the windowsill, big as a loaf of bread, that he used to hold the door. Bhai Yusuf borrowed the old man’s tools one night and hollowed it out and put the mouse inside. Now you stay in there, he said, and when they come, you tell them about themselves.

The first cat came. Where’s my meat? Meat? said the mouse. Go home and eat your kittens. And the cat was so mad he jumped at her, and he never saw the crystal, and he hit it so hard he never got up. And the same with the second cat, and the third, and the fourth. After that the mouse brought him two nickels a day, and then three, and by the end she had given him the whole fortune out of the wall a nickel at a time, and they were friends until she died.

The young brothers were laughing by then, and the old ones were shaking their heads.

Those four cats, said Bhai Yusuf, were those four jama’ats. That mouse was Kaneez. Cursing at the whole world from inside a glass, and nothing could touch her. And she was right not to let anybody in. What else could she do? He shifted his cane on his lap. Only now there’s no glass. Now she’s out in the open, and she’s gone still all the way down, like a pond in the morning. You can see right to the bottom. That’s what it looks like when a person has heard something and it took.

Who have believed and whose hearts have rest in the remembrance of Allah. Verily in the remembrance of Allah do hearts find rest!

— Qur’an 13:28

And the boy who cut the crystal? said the young Bangladeshi, who had been on that first gasht a year and a half before and had carried the first tray. Who was the boy, Nana?

Bhai Yusuf only looked at him, and got up with his cane, and went to make wudu for the night prayer.

•••

Every morning that summer, after Fajr, Kaneez walked from the house in Fresh Meadows to Meadow Lake in the park and stood on the path by the water before the joggers came. Nobody ever knew why. The lake lay flat and grey under the planes coming down into LaGuardia, without a ripple, holding the whole sky. When the sun came over the trees she walked home and tied on her apron and went into the kitchen, where her mother was already at the karahi, frying the first malpure of the day, one at a time, and laying them out in rows on the big steel tray for whoever came to the door.

Background

The verse

Wise people, after they have listened to the laws, become serene, like a deep, smooth, and still lake.Dhammapada 82, tr. F. Max Müller (1881)

In the commentary, a married daughter named Känä has been staying at her mother’s house. Four times her mother fries cakes for her to take back to her husband, and four times a monk on his alms round comes to the door and the mother gives the cakes away. The husband takes another wife. Känä blames the monks and abuses every one she sees, until they no longer dare walk down her street. The Buddha goes to the house and eats breakfast there. Känä hides and weeps. He asks the mother whether the monks took the cakes or were given them, and what blame then falls on them. Mother and daughter each say the monks did nothing wrong, and Känä is transformed. On his way home the Buddha passes the palace. When the king hears what has happened, he says that if the Buddha has made her rich in the wealth beyond the world, he will make her rich in this one. He adopts Känä as his eldest daughter and gives her in marriage to a great noble, and she feeds monks so generously that she cannot find enough of them. The Buddha then tells the Jätaka of a mouse squeezed by four cats, whom a stonecutter saves by hiding her in a hollow crystal. Read the full commentary story, or about the Jätaka tales. The sweet cakes in the story are close cousins of the malpua, a fried cake whose name and recipe go back to Vedic India and which Pakistani families still make for Ramadan.

Queens, 1989

New York’s Muslim community is old and mixed. In 1939 the Grenadian-born convert Shaikh Daoud Faisal founded the Islamic Mission of America in Brooklyn Heights, now known as the State Street Mosque. After immigration law changed in 1965, and especially in the 1980s, newcomers from Pakistan, Bangladesh, Guyana, Senegal, Egypt and elsewhere settled in the outer boroughs, in neighborhoods such as Jackson Heights. In 1983 Masjid al-Falah in Corona, Queens, opened as the city’s first purpose-built mosque (Islam in New York City).

The Tablighi Jamaat was founded in 1926 by Muhammad Ilyas Kandhlawi in the Mewat region of British India. After Partition its Pakistani center grew up at Raiwind, near Lahore, and the movement has since spread to more than 150 countries, the United States among them. It is a lay movement that keeps out of politics. Members go out in small traveling parties, or jama’ats, ideally paying their own way, carrying their bedding and staying in mosques. Each day they make a gasht through the local neighborhood, with a local guide, inviting Muslims to the evening prayer. The movement asks its members to give one night a week, one weekend a month and forty days a year to this work.
