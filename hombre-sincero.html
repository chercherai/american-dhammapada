Walking the American WayCommentary 26.13 · One Brahmin

Hombre Sincero

Yo soy un hombre sincero
De donde crece la palma.
— Versos sencillos I

Así lo oí yo, on Calle Ocho.

I. The Palms

Little Havana, Miami, Florida · April 1980

On the wall of Octavio Iturralde’s office on the ninth floor of a bank on Brickell Avenue there was one photograph, and everybody who came into the office was told about it whether they asked or not. It showed a road of red earth running straight away from the camera for a kilometer between two rows of royal palms, a hundred and twenty palms on each side, white-trunked and smooth and as tall as a six-story building, and at the far end of the road, small and white, a house with a portico. That was the Central Santa Rosalía, in the province of Matanzas. His great-great-grandfather had planted the palms. There had been Iturraldes in Matanzas since before the English took Havana. The house was a school now, he had heard. The palms were still standing. Nobody had told him so but he knew it, because a royal palm does not fall down for a revolution.

He was fifty-seven. He had left in 1960 with one suitcase, like everybody, and come back in April of 1961 with a rifle on the beach at Girón, like the best of them, and spent twenty months in Castro’s prisons, and come home to Miami on Christmas Eve of 1962, when the Brigade was ransomed for fifty-three million dollars in food and medicine. He did not talk about the prison. He was a vice president now. He had a house in Coral Gables with a mango tree, and a wife from a family as good as his, and a membership in the Big Five Club, which the exiles had founded so that the families of the Havana Yacht Club and the Vedado Tennis and the Biltmore and the Miramar and the Casino Español would have somewhere to dance at Christmas, as they had at home. He sent money every month to the widows of the Brigade. He was, as everybody said, a caballero.

On Saturday mornings he played dominoes in the little park on Calle Ocho, under the trees, at the concrete tables, with men who had been nobody in Cuba. He did not have to. His father had played on Saturday mornings in a park in Matanzas with the men who cut his cane, and called them by their names, and so Octavio did too. He played well. He slapped his tiles down hard on the concrete the way you are supposed to, and drank the little cups of black coffee the boy brought round on a tray, and at the Brigade dinner every April, when they all stood and sang, he sang the loudest of anybody, with his hand on his chest, the words every Cuban child learns before he can read.

Yo soy un hombre sincero, de donde crece la palma.

I am a sincere man, from where the palm grows. He meant it. His family had planted the palms.

II. Mariel

Mariel Harbor and the Florida Straits · May 1980

In the first week of April a bus crashed through the gate of the Peruvian embassy in Havana, and Castro took away the guards, and in two days ten thousand people were packed onto the embassy grounds, in the trees, on the roof, on the walls, and the government called them trash. Then on the twentieth of April Castro said that anyone in Miami who wanted their relatives could come and get them at the port of Mariel.

Miami went to sea. Men who had never been on a boat hired anything that floated, shrimpers and party boats and cabin cruisers and lobster boats from the Keys, and the captains charged a thousand dollars a head and more, and the boats went south across the Straits by the hundreds, and the first of them came back into Key West on the twenty-first with forty-eight people aboard. Octavio had a cousin in Havana, Lourdes, his mother’s sister’s daughter, and her husband and two boys. He hired a shrimp boat out of Stock Island with a captain named Earl who spoke no Spanish, and in the second week of May he went himself.

They sat in Mariel harbor for nine days. There were a thousand boats at anchor, and the Cuban officials came out in launches and took the lists of names and went away again, and sold them ham sandwiches at five dollars apiece, and nobody knew anything. On the ninth day a launch came alongside in the afternoon with a soldier standing in the bow and a load of people sitting on the thwarts, and the soldier told Earl to take them, all of them, now, and go. There were thirty-eight. Lourdes was not among them. There were men with shaved heads from the prison at the Combinado del Este. There was a boy of nineteen who was gay and had been beaten, and said so, as if he were giving his address. There was a man in a hospital gown who sat in the bow the whole way across and sang. There was a woman with a baby. There was an old Black man in a pressed white guayabera with a cardboard suitcase tied up with string, and when Octavio helped him over the rail the suitcase was so heavy he almost dropped it, and the old man said it was books.

Octavio shouted at the soldier that these were not his people and the soldier put his hand on his pistol, and Earl said something in English that did not need translating and started the engine.

It blew up rough in the Straits after dark. The boat was built for twelve men and a hold full of shrimp, and it rolled, and everybody was sick, and the man in the hospital gown sang, and the woman held her baby inside her blouse to keep the spray off it, and the boy who had been beaten wept. Then the old man with the suitcase got it open, and took out a book, and found the place by the light of the wheelhouse, and began to read out loud. He had a voice that carried without being loud. It was The Count of Monte Cristo, in Spanish, the part where Dantès is in the Château d’If and hears the scratching in the wall. He read it to them for four hours, in the wind, standing braced in the door of the wheelhouse with one hand on the frame, and nobody on that boat said anything until the lights of Key West came up out of the sea.

On the dock at the Truman Annex, in the floodlights, with the Immigration men and the Red Cross and the television cameras, the old man shook Earl’s hand for a long time.

Gracias, hombre sincero.

Earl, who did not know what it meant, said you’re welcome. The old man turned to Octavio and shook his hand too.

Gracias, señor.

Octavio gave him a twenty-dollar bill and his card from the bank and went home to Coral Gables and did not sleep. Lourdes came over in June, on a different boat, with nothing but her husband and her boys and the clothes they had on. He never did find out who had paid for it.

III. The Lector

Máximo Gómez Park · summer 1980

The old man’s name was Cándido Valdés. Octavio found that out in July, in the park, when he looked up from his tiles on a Saturday morning and saw him on the bench under the trees with the Diario Las Américas open across his knees, reading it out loud.

He was reading the whole thing. The front page, the editorials, the box scores, the deaths. He read it the way he had read Monte Cristo on the boat, standing up inside his voice, every word given its weight, and the men at the tables nearest him had stopped playing to listen, and then the next tables had, and when he came to the end of an article somebody would say otra, another, and he would turn the page.

It came out over the summer, the way things come out in a park. He was seventy-three. He had been a lector for forty-one years at a cigar factory in Havana, which was a job that only existed in Cuba and in Tampa: you sat on a platform at the front of the big rolling room, above three hundred men and women at their benches, and read aloud to them all day while they rolled, newspapers in the morning and novels in the afternoon, and they paid you themselves out of their wages, so you read what they voted for. Mostly they voted for Dumas. The rollers at one factory had loved The Count of Monte Cristo so much, the old men at the tables told each other, that the company had named a cigar for it.

He had no family. When he was asked his people he said he was a Valdés, and the Cubans at the tables understood, and the ones who were not Cuban had it explained to them. For two hundred years, any child left at the door of the Casa Cuna in Havana, the foundling home, was given the name of an old bishop of Havana who had looked after it. Valdés. It was the name for nobody’s son. There were thousands of them. Everyone in Cuba knew a Valdés who was a Valdés for that reason, and nobody asked.

He had come over on the boatlift because the factory had a new lector, a young one from the Party, who read Granma in the morning and the speeches in the afternoon, and the rollers had stopped voting. He had spent his first six weeks in Miami in the tent city under the I-95 overpass at Seventh Street, at the foot of Little Havana, with the cars going over his head all night. Now he had a room on Eleventh Avenue, over a botánica, for sixty dollars a month, which a church paid.

The men at the tables took to bringing him the paper. Then they took to bringing him coffee, and pastelitos, and in August one of them brought him a pair of reading glasses from the drugstore, and he read them the news of the boatlift, which was still going on, and the news from Liberty City, where the Black neighborhoods had burned for three days in May after a jury in Tampa acquitted the policemen who had beaten a Black insurance man to death after stopping him on his motorcycle. He read that in the same voice as everything else. Nobody at the tables said anything about it. In the paper and on the radio, every day, men who had come over in 1960 and 1961 talked about the Marielitos, how they were not like us, how Castro had emptied his prisons and his madhouses onto the boats, how the city was ruined, and he read those too.

And Octavio, who played at the third table, noticed what everybody noticed, which was that the old lector had a way of naming people.

He called the boy who brought the coffee round on the tray hombre sincero. The boy was a Marielito, sixteen, who had come over on a shrimp boat alone and lived with a cousin in Hialeah and sent his tips home to his mother. He called the Haitian who sold mangoes from a shopping cart at the corner hombre sincero, in Spanish, and then in French, and the Haitian laughed and gave him a mango. He called the woman who swept the park in the mornings mujer sincera. He called Tito the boxer, who had fought in Havana before the revolution and had drunk himself out of a room on Flagler Street and now slept behind the dry cleaner’s, hombre sincero, and Tito wept.

He did not call everybody that. Most people he called by their names. Some people he called señor. He called Octavio señor.

IV. The Question

The third table · a Saturday in September

It went on bothering Octavio all summer, and he could not have said why, and it bothered him more that he could not say why. He had pulled the man out of the sea. He had given him twenty dollars. His family had planted the palms in the song. If anybody under those trees was a sincere man from where the palm grows, it was an Iturralde of Matanzas. The old man knew the song as well as he did. He had heard him sing it.

On a Saturday in September, when the heat had begun to go out of the mornings, Octavio finished his game and stood up and went over to the bench where Cándido Valdés sat with the paper folded on his knee and the coffee boy’s tray beside him, and sat down at the other end of it.

Maestro. He used the word the rollers would have used. Can I ask you something?

Ask.

You call that boy hombre sincero. You call the Haitian with the cart hombre sincero. You call Tito hombre sincero, and Tito sleeps in the alley. And you call me señor. He heard his own voice and did not like how it sounded and went on anyway. My family has been in Cuba since before the English took Havana. My great-great-grandfather planted the palms in that song. I was born under them. I went back with a rifle. If that word belongs to anybody in this park it belongs to me. I’m asking you why you don’t use it.

The tables near the bench had gone quiet. Somebody turned a tile over on the concrete, and the click was loud.

Cándido Valdés looked at him for a while. Then he reached down into his shopping bag, under the newspaper and the mango the Haitian had given him, and brought out a small book with no cover left on it, held together with a rubber band, and took the rubber band off, and turned the pages until he found the place, though everybody at the tables could see he did not need the book.

I don’t know where I was born, he said. Somebody put me down at the door of the Casa Cuna in the night, and the nuns picked me up in the morning and wrote Valdés. That’s my palm. So I don’t call a man by where he was born. I wouldn’t know how. Martí didn’t either. Listen.

And he read it, standing up inside his voice the way he had in the door of the wheelhouse, a few lines from the piece Martí wrote in New York in 1893, when the Spanish were telling Cubans that a free Cuba would be a war of black against white.

Hombre es más que blanco, más que mulato, más que negro.

Los hombres de pompa e interés se irán de un lado, blancos o negros; y los hombres generosos y desinteresados se irán de otro.

— Martí, Mi raza

A man is more than white, more than mulatto, more than black. The men of show and self-interest will go off to one side, white or black, and the generous men, the ones who want nothing for themselves, will go off to the other. Every man at the tables knew what it said. Some of them had learned it in school, in the old Cuba, and some in the new.

V. The Poor of the Earth

The same morning

That’s the race, Cándido said. The only one. The generous and the disinterested. It isn’t born. Nobody is born into it. The boy with the coffee came over with nothing and sends his tips to his mother and keeps nothing. The Haitian gives me his mangoes. Tito has nothing left in this world to hold on to but his hands, and he gave me those, the day I came, to carry my suitcase.

And me?

You, the old man said, not unkindly, are holding on to a road with two hundred and forty palm trees on it, ninety miles away, that you will never walk on again. And a club for dancing. And a bank. And that word. With all that in your hands, what am I going to call you? You’re a señor. It’s not an insult. Señor is a fine word. It’s what you call a man by what he has.

Octavio said nothing.

You sing it every April, Cándido said. Hombre sincero, de donde crece la palma. Everybody stops there. Nobody reads the rest of the book. The man who wrote that song had nothing. Not a peso. He lived in a rented room in New York and wrote for the newspapers and gave everything he had to the war and went home to fight it and was shot off his horse five weeks after he landed. That’s who said hombre sincero. Listen to what he said he wanted.

He turned a few pages back in the coverless book and read again, quieter this time, as if it were only for the man on the other end of the bench.

Con los pobres de la tierra
Quiero yo mi suerte echar:
El arroyo de la sierra
Me complace más que el mar.

— Martí, Versos sencillos III

With the poor of the earth I want to cast my lot. The stream in the hills pleases me more than the sea. Octavio had known those lines since he was seven years old. Every child in Cuba knew them. He had never in his life heard them.

He sat on the bench. The tables around him had started playing again, quietly, out of courtesy, and the tiles clicked. The coffee boy came by with his tray and Octavio took a cup without looking at it, and paid, and the boy said gracias, señor, and went on.

Octavio thought about the photograph on his office wall. The red road and the palms. He saw it more clearly than he had seen it in twenty years, and he saw, for the first time, the men in the photograph, which he had looked at a thousand times and never seen: three of them, small, black, in straw hats, with machetes, at the foot of the palms on the left side of the road, where somebody had put them to give the picture its scale. Nobody knew their names. Nobody had written them on the back. They were somebody’s sons. They could have been Valdés, every one of them. They had held nothing, and the palms had been theirs as much as anybody’s, and they had come from everywhere and gone everywhere, and the road had gone on being a road.

Something that had been closed in Octavio Iturralde since the beach at Girón opened, a little, the way a fist does when you stop telling it to hold on. It did not feel like losing anything. It felt like setting down a suitcase he had been carrying so long he had stopped knowing it was heavy.

•••

He did not give away the bank, or the house in Coral Gables. He did not resign from the Big Five. People who knew him only from the bank would not have said he had changed at all. But the photograph came down off the wall of the office on Brickell that fall and went into a drawer, and that winter, when the last of the Marielitos were still sitting in the camps at Fort Chaffee because nobody would sponsor them, the ones with no family and no papers and the wrong color, the hard-to-sponsor cases, Octavio sponsored eleven men, and went to Arkansas himself to get them, and found them rooms and found them work, and never told the Brigade dinner about it.

One of them was the man who had sung in the bow of Earl’s boat in a hospital gown. Octavio found him a bed in a home in Allapattah, and visited him on Sundays, and the man sang to him.

VI. The Soup

Máximo Gómez Park · 1981

Cándido Valdés read the newspaper under the trees on Calle Ocho for six more years, until his eyes went, and then for two more after that from memory, Martí and Dumas and the Gospel of Saint Luke, which he said was the best-written thing in Spanish, though it was not Spanish. When he died, in 1988, the men at the tables took up a collection and buried him at Woodlawn, on Eighth Street, up the road from the park, and the stone they bought says CÁNDIDO VALDÉS, LECTOR, and nothing else, because nobody knew the year he was born.

But that was later. On a Saturday morning in the spring of 1981, a year after Mariel, Octavio came to the park with his newspaper under his arm and sat down at the third table, and the four of them turned the fifty-five tiles face down on the concrete and mixed them, which the Cubans call making the soup, all eight hands at once going round and round, the bone tiles clicking against each other with their black backs up, every one of them the same as every other from the back, so that you could not tell, until you drew it and turned it over in your hand, whether you were holding the double nine or the blank.

Octavio drew his ten without looking at them and stood them on their edges in a row. Across the park, on the bench, the old lector had the paper open on his knees and was reading the box scores out loud to anybody who wanted them, and the coffee boy was going round with the tray.

The boy stopped at the third table. Octavio took a cup and paid him and said, Gracias, hombre sincero, and the boy laughed, and said, De nada, hombre sincero, and went on.

On the bench, Cándido Valdés turned the page.

Background

The verse

I do not call a man a Brahmana because of his origin or of his mother. He is indeed arrogant, and he is wealthy: but the poor, who is free from all attachments, him I call indeed a Brahmana.Dhammapada 396, tr. F. Max Müller (1881)

In the commentary, a brahmin notices that the Buddha calls his own disciples “brahmins.” He reasons that since he was born of a brahmin mother, the Buddha ought to call him one too, and goes to ask. The Buddha answers that he does not call a man a brahmin because of the womb he was born from; a man who clings to possessions is only a bhovādī, one who goes about saying “sir,” and only the one who has nothing and grasps at nothing does he call a brahmin. At the end of the teaching, the brahmin enters the stream. Here the title becomes hombre sincero, from the first line of Martí’s Versos sencillos, and the “sir” becomes señor. Read the full commentary story. The poet is José Martí (1853–1895), whose lines from Versos sencillos (1891) became the words of “Guantanamera”; his essay “Mi raza” appeared in New York in 1893. The verses are quoted here in the original Spanish, which is in the public domain; the English in the story paraphrases them.

Miami, 1980

In April 1980, after some ten thousand Cubans crowded into the Peruvian embassy in Havana seeking asylum, Fidel Castro opened the port of Mariel to anyone who wanted to leave. Between April 15 and October 31, the Mariel boatlift brought about 125,000 Cubans to Florida, along with some 25,000 Haitians. Exiles hired whatever boats they could, and the Coast Guard reported that Cuban officials forced overloaded boats to take on passengers, sometimes at gunpoint, while boaters waiting in Mariel paid five dollars for a ham sandwich. Many of the newcomers were younger, more working-class and darker-skinned than the exiles of the early 1960s, and some had come from prisons and mental hospitals. They were held at the Orange Bowl and in a tent city under I-95 at the foot of Little Havana, and the hardest to sponsor were sent to Fort Chaffee in Arkansas. Many of the earlier exiles belonged to families of the old Havana clubs, and in 1967 they founded the Big Five Club in Miami. In Cuba, children left at the Casa Cuna, the Havana foundling home, were given the surname Valdés, after Bishop Gerónimo Valdés, one of its early patrons.

Cándido’s trade was that of the lector, hired by cigar workers in Cuba from 1865 on to read newspapers and novels aloud while they rolled; the Montecristo brand took its name from the novel the rollers loved. Octavio fought at the Bay of Pigs in April 1961; the captured men of Brigade 2506 were released in December 1962 in exchange for some $53 million in food and medicine. On Calle Ocho, Máximo Gómez Park, known as Domino Park, has drawn Cuban men to its tables since the early 1970s.
