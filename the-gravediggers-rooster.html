The Gravedigger’s Rooster

Blessed art thou, O Lord our God, King of the universe, who hast given to the cock intelligence to distinguish between day and night.
— The Authorised Daily Prayer Book, tr. S. Singer

Azoy hob ikh es gehert, by the coffee urn in Overland Park.

I. The First Shift

Overland Park, Kansas · Tuesday night, May 25, 1993

Shavuot came in on a Tuesday that year, and at Congregation Tifereth Jacob the evening service could not start until it was full dark, because the forty-nine days had to be finished first. So it was half past ten before Mel Tischler stood up in the social hall with a legal pad.

Mel was forty-six, a district sales manager for a business-forms company, and chairman of adult education. He coached his daughter’s softball team, and most of the congregation called him Coach, including the rabbi. The all-night study was his idea. Tifereth Jacob had not held one since it moved out from the city in 1964, and Mel had read about them in a magazine.

Twenty-two people had stayed. They sat at round tables with paper cloths on them, under the fluorescent lights. There was a forty-cup urn of regular and a smaller one of decaf, two cheesecakes, and Mrs. Rosenbloom’s blintz casserole under foil. Naomi Sacks was there because her son Josh was being confirmed in the morning and the confirmation class had been told to come. She was a night nurse at a hospital on the Missouri side, and this was her night off.

People, Mel said. Here’s the story. The morning God gave the Torah, our ancestors overslept. That’s in the midrash. He’s on the mountain, thunder, lightning, the whole production, and Moses has to go through the camp getting them up. So tonight we fix it. That’s what tikkun means. It’s a repair. We stay awake for them. Nobody sleeps on my watch.

He had asked Mr. Plotkin what a person studied on such a night. Mr. Plotkin was the shammes, the sexton. He was eighty-one, he had the keys, and he knew where everything in the building was. He had gone to a cabinet and come back with a carton of little books that had made every move with the congregation and had not been opened since the first one. They were in Hebrew, in small type: the opening verses and the closing verses of every portion of the Torah, then of each of the Prophets and the Writings, then of each tractate of the Mishnah.

You read the beginning and the end of everything, Mr. Plotkin had said. Nobody finishes. You taste.

Mel had run off the English on the office copier, eleven pages, stapled, and he had divided the night into three shifts. Torah from ten-thirty to twelve-thirty. Prophets and Writings until two-thirty. Mishnah until four-thirty, then the morning service at a quarter past five so they would be standing when the sun came up.

This comes to you straight from Sinai, people, he said, by way of Mr. Plotkin’s cabinet. So let’s give it everything.

Mr. Plotkin sat for a while at the table nearest the door with one of the little books and a glass of tea, reading in an undertone. A little after eleven Mel bent down to him. Go home, Mr. Plotkin. You need your rest. We’ve got it covered. The old man looked at him and closed the book on his finger. Then he got his hat. He lived in the apartments across the road so that he would never have to drive on a Sabbath or a holiday. He said he would be back before five to open up the chapel.

At twenty to twelve Mel put down his coffee.

I’m going to close my eyes ten minutes in the rabbi’s study, he said, so I’m fresh for the changeover.

II. The Changeovers

The social hall · half past twelve to four

At twelve-thirty a small double beep came through the door of the rabbi’s study, and a moment later Mel came out with his hair flat on one side.

By then the room had found its way into the night. Dr. Mandel, a retired dentist, had been reading the last verses of Deuteronomy aloud to his table, and nobody at it was thinking about the time. Others had drifted. Four were in the kitchen eating cheesecake standing up. Three of the confirmation class were lying on the carpet in the youth lounge.

People, Mel said. Did we come here to lie down? Is this what we’re doing? Up. Back to the tables. Second shift. Sinai, people.

He walked them in ahead of him and stood until every chair was filled. He said a few more words about commitment. Then he said he wanted to check a source, and the study door closed behind him.

It took the room half an hour to get back to where it had been. Naomi was in the Book of Ruth. She knew that hour from the floor, the one where the body asks to be let off, and she knew the only cure for it was to have something in front of you that needed doing. She had just found it again when the beep came through the door at two-thirty.

This time he found people on the padded bench in the lobby, under the memorial plaques with their little bulbs, and Mrs. Rosenbloom in the kitchen wrapping the casserole. He brought them all back. He said this was the home stretch. He said the people who built this congregation had not built it on eight hours’ sleep. He looked at his watch, thumbed it, and went to check another source.

After that nobody could keep to a page. They were too tired to read and too stirred up to rest, and every one of them was listening for the door. Six went home.

I’ll say this for him, Dr. Mandel said. The man is a dynamo. Where does he get it? He’s in there with the commentaries while the rest of us fall on our faces. Naomi, go and look. You’re the professional.

She went, and Josh went with her. She opened the study door the way she opened a door on the ward at that hour.

Mel Tischler lay on the rabbi’s couch under the rabbi’s raincoat with his mouth open. His shoes stood paired on the carpet. He had taken off his watch and set it on the arm of the couch, three inches from his ear.

She left the door standing open so the others could come and see, and they came, one at a time, quietly, as people file past at a funeral home.

So, Dr. Mandel said. Forty years I told people to floss.

Josh picked up the watch. It was the same Casio he had. He held down a button until the little bell on the face went out, and he set the watch back on the arm of the couch where it had been. His mother saw him do it and said nothing.

At four the lawn sprinklers came on outside by themselves. There were nine people left in the social hall, and not one of them could have told you what the Mishnah was about.

III. The Gravedigger’s Rooster

A quarter to five

Mr. Plotkin came across the dark lot in a suit and a hat with his tallis bag under his arm. He looked at the nine of them, and at the six chairs somebody had pushed together for Mrs. Rosenbloom, and at the cheesecake.

Nu, he said. You learned?

They told him all of it, the shifts and the speeches and the beep through the door. Dr. Mandel told most of it. Mr. Plotkin looked over once at the study, where a raincoat rose and fell. He sat down and put his hat on the table.

By us, in Lithuania, my father was the shammes, he said. Every morning before light he went down the street with a wooden hammer and knocked on the shutters. Three knocks, so a person knew it was time to get up and come to shul. That was for the householders. The boys in the study house got up before that, in the middle of the night, to learn. For them my father didn’t knock. They kept a rooster.

The rooster died. So one of the boys got another one from the gravedigger, who kept a few chickens by the cemetery wall. This one was hatched out there by himself. No hen sat on him. He never once heard an old rooster. And by a cemetery it’s quiet, one hour is the same as another hour. So he crowed when it came into his head. He crowed at midnight, and the boys got up and learned until they fell asleep on the page, and when it was really morning they were no good to anybody. Then he crowed at noon, when they were trying to go over it. A week they stood this. Friday he was soup.

My father said, don’t be angry with the rooster. Who showed him?

He took a prayer book out of the tallis bag. It was an old black one with English down the left-hand pages. He had owned it since 1923, the year he landed in Kansas City and taught himself to read English out of it. He turned it around on the table so they could see the place.

The first thing a Jew thanks for in the morning, when he comes to the blessings. Before his eyes, before his clothes, before his own two feet. This.

Blessed art thou, O Lord our God, King of the universe, who hast given to the cock intelligence to distinguish between day and night.

— The Authorised Daily Prayer Book, tr. S. Singer, Morning Service

To a rooster it’s given. A person has to get it for himself, and from himself. It’s very hard. To wake up another person is nothing, a hammer can do it. The one that’s hard to wake up is you. He closed the book. The Gemara says it short. K’shot atzm’cha, v’achar kach k’shot acherim.

Improve thyself and then improve others.

— Talmud, Baba Bathra 60b, tr. A. Cohen

Then he got up and went to the door of the rabbi’s study. It was open. He knocked on the frame anyway with one knuckle, three times, and in Yiddish, not loudly, he said what his father used to say to the shutters, which was that it was time to get up and serve the Creator.

He did not wait to see what came of it. He went into the chapel and put on his tallis. A person who has been up all night hears some of the morning blessings from one who has slept, so those were his to say, and he began, and the nine of them stood up behind him. When he reached the blessing for the rooster the windows on the east side had gone gray, and every one of them was awake. Naomi thought she had not been so awake at any hour of the night. Out in the lot the lamp on its pole, which also knew, went off.

Mel Tischler came in during the next page with his shoes in his hand. Nobody turned around. Mrs. Rosenbloom found the place for him and held the book out, and he took it.

•••

The next year there was no legal pad. Mr. Plotkin sat down at half past ten at the table nearest the door with a little book and a glass of tea, and he began at the beginning in an undertone. That was the whole program. Anyone who looked up at any hour of that night saw him there, eighty-two years old, with his finger on the line.

Twenty-six people saw the sun come up. Mel Tischler was one of them. He had sat at the old man’s elbow since half past ten and had not made one remark to the room. His watch was out in the glove compartment of his Buick. He had set it before he thought, and then left it there, and at half past twelve it went off in the dark with nobody to hear it.

Background

The verse

If a man make himself as he teaches others to be, then, being himself well subdued, he may subdue (others); one’s own self is indeed difficult to subdue.Dhammapada 159, tr. F. Max Müller (1881)

In the commentary the elder Padhānika Tissa, “Tissa the Striver,” takes five hundred monks into the forest with a meditation subject they have had from the Buddha. He tells them to be heedful, lies down, and goes to sleep. When the monks come in to rest at the end of the first watch of the night he wakes, asks whether they came there to sleep, sends them out again, and goes back to bed; he does the same at the end of the middle watch. Worn out and unsettled, none of them gets anywhere. At last they decide that their teacher must be a man of tremendous energy, go to watch him, and find out. When they return, the Buddha tells them it is not the first time. Once they were students who rose to their lessons at cockcrow. Their rooster died, and the one they got in his place had been raised in a cemetery and knew nothing of hours, so that he crowed at midnight and again at noon until they wrung his neck. No mother or father raised him, their teacher said, and he lived under no teacher. Read the full commentary story.

Overland Park, 1993

Shavuot marks the giving of the Torah at Sinai, and in 1993 its first day fell on Wednesday, May 26. The custom of studying through the night before, the Tikkun Leil Shavuot, comes from the Zohar and the kabbalists who followed it. A later explanation, the one Mel gives, rests on a midrash to the Song of Songs in which Israel slept late on the morning of the revelation and had to be roused. The traditional book for the night is an anthology of beginnings and endings: the first and last verses of each portion of the Torah, of the other biblical books, and of the tractates of the Mishnah. The evening service of the festival waits for full dark so that the seven weeks counted from Passover are complete. After the Second World War many of Kansas City’s Jewish families moved south and across the state line, and Overland Park, in Johnson County, Kansas, became the center of the community. Congregation Tifereth Jacob and its people are invented.

The blessing for the rooster stands first in the long row of morning blessings, and comes from the Talmud (Berakhot 60b), which appoints it for the moment a person hears the cock crow. The English is Simeon Singer’s, from the Authorised Daily Prayer Book of 1890 (text), which was reprinted in America for decades. The saying from Baba Bathra is given as Abraham Cohen put it in Ancient Jewish Proverbs (1911). In the towns of Eastern Europe the sexton who went round before dawn knocking on doors and shutters to call people to prayer was the shulklapper, the synagogue knocker. Three knocks was one of the usual counts.
