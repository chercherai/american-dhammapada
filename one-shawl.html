Walking the American WayCommentary 9.1 · Culla Ekasāṭaka

One Shawl

For behold, this life is the time for men to prepare to meet God; yea, behold the day of this life is the day for men to perform their labors.
— Alma 34:32

Fel hyn y clywais i, in Great Salt Lake City.

I. The Shawl

Great Salt Lake City · 1849–1856

Ifan and Gwen Pugh came to the valley in the autumn of 1849 with the Welsh, three hundred of them, who had sailed out of Liverpool singing and gone up the Mississippi in a steamboat and walked from the Missouri River to the mountains. In Merthyr Ifan had cut coal. In the valley there was no coal anybody had found yet, so he cut stone. He quarried it and hauled it and squared it for the public works, and for the foundation of the Temple when they started on it, and he was paid at the tithing office in flour and potatoes and squash and paper scrip, and he and Gwen did well enough for six years. They had an adobe house of two rooms with a dirt roof, and a clock, and a feather bed, and a Book of Mormon in Welsh that had come over in a later ship, and each of them had a good coat.

Then the grasshoppers came in the summer of 1855. They came out of the ground where they had been laid the fall before and ate the wheat as it sprouted. They ate the corn. They ate the squash vines down to the roots. Women threw shawls and bedsheets over their gardens to save something, and the grasshoppers ate the shawls. Gwen would not put hers out. She sat on the step all one week in August with a broom and beat them off the squash by hand, and saved four squash, and the neighbors laughed at her and then came to ask for seed.

There had been hardly any snow the winter before, and there was no rain that summer. The canyons burned. The oxen that hauled the stone for the Temple had nothing to eat, and the work on the Temple stopped, and Ifan’s work stopped with it. That winter a third of the cattle in the valley died in the cold. The Pughs ate the four squash and then sold the clock for flour. Then Ifan’s good coat. Then the feather bed and both blankets and Gwen’s second dress and her coat. In the spring of 1856 Gwen went up on the bench with a digging stick for sego roots, the way she had heard the first settlers did in the cricket years, and boiled pigweed, and they lived.

By that autumn they owned what they stood in, and one shawl.

It was grey Welsh flannel with a red stripe along the border, and Gwen’s mother had woven it in Llanidloes and given it to her at the dock in Liverpool, and it was the warmest thing either of them had ever owned. Whoever had to go out wore it. Whoever stayed home stayed by the fire. It was a woman’s shawl, but men wore shawls that year, the way they wore anything; Ifan wore it crossed on his chest and tied behind, and nobody on their street said anything about it, because half the men there had something of their wives’ on.

In September the home missionaries came. That was the year of the Reformation, when the whole church was called to repent and be baptized over again, and every ward had two men who went house to house and sat at every table and asked every family the questions. Do you pray in your family night and morning. Do you speak against your brethren. Do you pay your tithing promptly. The two who came to the Pughs were a hatter and a young clerk. They sat at Ifan’s table, which was a plank on two trestles, under the dirt roof, and Ifan answered every question the way it ought to be answered except the one about tithing. The clerk wrote it in the book. Ifan said that if he had anything he would give a tenth of it, and the hatter said he knew that, and the clerk wrote that down too.

II. Sunday

October 5, 1856

It was conference. The word had gone around the night before that Brother Franklin Richards had come in from the States with bad news, but nobody in the Welsh houses knew what it was.

Morning or afternoon? Ifan said, because they could not both go.

Morning, said Gwen, and put on the shawl, and went.

She came back after noon without her stockings. She sat down on the stool by the fire and held out her bare white ankles in her shoes and told him why.

There were people on the plains, she said. Hundreds of them. Two companies with handcarts, from the old countries, who had started too late in the year from the Missouri, and they were somewhere out on the Platte now with winter coming, and nobody in the valley had known they were coming at all. Brother Richards had passed them on the road. President Young had stood up and said they must be brought in, and help must be sent to them now, and he wanted teams and flour and teamsters, and that the faith of anybody who sat in their seat and did nothing would be in vain. And then the sisters had started to take things off. Right there in the Tabernacle. Petticoats. Stockings. Their shawls. A woman in front of Gwen had given her shoes and walked out barefoot in October. They piled it all in the aisles and men carried it out in armfuls to the wagons.

So I gave my stockings, Gwen said. They were mine.

She did not say anything about the shawl. She took it off and folded it and hung it on its peg by the door, and Ifan understood that she had worn it home because it was not only hers to give.

The bishop had sent word around that there would be a meeting in the ward schoolhouse that evening, and that whatever anybody had was to be brought to his yard, because the wagons were going east at first light on Tuesday and every ward was to fill its share. Ifan put on the shawl at dusk, crossed and tied behind, and went.

III. The Watches

The bishop’s yard · Sunday night

Bishop Ezra Tuttle was seventy-four years old and a shoemaker. He had come from Vermont to Kirtland and from Kirtland to Missouri and from Missouri to Nauvoo and from Nauvoo across Iowa in the mud, and he had buried his wife at Winter Quarters, and he still had the same awl. He lived alone in the back of his shop on a cot. He was not a speaker. People who had been in his ward for five years would tell you they had never heard him say more than a hundred words at a meeting, and most of those were the names of the sick.

At the schoolhouse that night he stood up and read out what the President had asked for, in the President’s own words, off a paper. Then he opened his Book of Mormon and read one verse. Then he sat down.

And now behold, my beloved brethren, I say unto you, do not suppose that this is all; for after ye have done all these things, if ye turn away the needy, and the naked, and visit not the sick and afflicted, and impart of your substance, if ye have, to those who stand in need—I say unto you, if ye do not any of these things, behold, your prayer is vain, and availeth you nothing, and ye are as hypocrites who do deny the faith.

— Alma 34:28

Ifan sat on the back bench in the shawl. The word that went into him was naked. He felt the shawl on his shoulders the way you feel a hand.

The bishop went home after the meeting and built a fire in his yard and sat down beside it on a box with his ledger on his knees, and people brought things. They brought them all night. Two wagons stood in the yard with their tongues down, and men loaded them by lantern light, and the bishop wrote down every gift in the ledger in a slow square hand, the name and the thing. Flour, a sack. Quilts, two. Boots, one pair, mended. Stockings. A buffalo robe with the hair half gone. A side of bacon from somebody who must have been hiding it since spring. Ifan came into the yard and sat down on the ground at the edge of the firelight, near the bishop’s feet, with the shawl around him, and did not get up.

He watched them come. A widow from the next street brought her dead husband’s boots and stood them side by side at the bishop’s feet, as if he might still step into them, and went home without a word. Two boys brought a crock of molasses between them, carrying it like a coffin. The hatter who had sat at Ifan’s table in September brought three hats, which made the men at the wagons laugh, and then they stopped laughing and loaded them, because a hat is a hat on the Sweetwater in November. Somebody brought a child’s red mittens. The bishop wrote them down. Mittens, one pair, red.

He was going to give it. He had known on the bench in the schoolhouse that he was going to give it. Then he thought of Gwen, at home by a fire with no wood to spare for it, in a dress and no stockings, and the winter coming, and the two of them with nothing between them and January, and no way for either one to go out of the door. He thought of the dock at Liverpool, and Gwen’s mother putting the shawl around Gwen’s shoulders with her own two hands and then looking past her at Ifan, not at Gwen, and saying to him in Welsh, Keep her warm. He thought of the squash on the step in August and Gwen with the broom. He thought: there are wards in this city with rich men in them. Let the rich men give. He thought: it is only one shawl, what is one shawl to hundreds of people. He thought of a thousand things, each one sensible, each one a little colder than the one before. And in between them, every so often, one other thought, very small and very clear, that said, Give it. And every time it came, the thousand sensible things came in after it and took it by the arms and put it out.

The bishop’s clock struck ten in the shop behind him. Ifan sat. The clock struck midnight. People stopped coming. The men loading the wagons went home to sleep for three hours. The bishop put more wood on the fire and sat on his box and wrote nothing, because there was nothing to write, and did not look at Ifan at all. The stars went over. The Big Dipper turned on its handle above the mountains. The clock struck two.

The clock struck three. Ifan thought, very clearly: two watches of the night are gone while I have been sitting here fighting. If this goes on, these sensible thoughts will grow, and grow, and they will hold me down for the rest of my life, and I will never get my head up out of them.

Before the clock struck four he got up on his feet. He untied the knot at his back. He took the shawl off, and the cold went into his chest like water, and he laid it down at the bishop’s feet, on the ground, folded. And then he did a thing he had not known he was going to do. He put his head back and shouted it out into the yard, in Welsh, three times, loud enough to wake the street, the word for when you have won a fight.

Gorchfygais! Gorchfygais! Gorchfygais!

IV. Blankets

Monday, before dawn

Hiram Bostwick had come up the street with his flour wagons a few minutes before. He had come into the valley with the first freighting trains, and he owned the biggest store on East Temple Street and a house with glass in every window, and he was bringing twenty sacks of flour to the bishop’s yard himself, because he was a man who liked to be seen to do a thing and also liked to do it. He was standing at the gate in a buffalo coat when the shouting started.

What was that? Ask him what he’s won.

His clerk went over and asked. Ifan told him, in English, plainly, standing in his shirt at the fire. One shawl, between the two of them. His wife had gone to the morning meeting in it and he had come to the evening. He had sat there since nine o’clock trying to give it. He had won.

Bostwick stood a while at the gate. That’s a hard thing, he said. That’s a harder thing than mine. And he sent the clerk across to his store, and the clerk came back with a pair of blankets, good white Mackinaw blankets with a black stripe, and gave them to Ifan.

Ifan looked at them and laid them on the shawl at the bishop’s feet.

Bostwick sent the clerk back for two pairs. Ifan laid them on the pile. Four pairs. He laid them down. Eight. The clerk was running now, and two of the bishop’s neighbors had come out in their nightshirts to see what the shouting was, and stayed to carry. Sixteen pairs. Ifan laid them all on the pile without so much as unfolding them, and the bishop wrote each one in the ledger, Blankets, two pair, I. Pugh, Blankets, four pair, I. Pugh, without a word.

When the clerk came back with thirty-two pairs on a handcart from the store, Bostwick stopped him at the gate.

Brother Pugh, he said, you keep one pair of these for yourself, and you give one pair to your wife. I won’t have it said in this city that I let a man freeze to death in front of me for the sake of being generous. So Ifan kept two pairs, and thirty pairs went into the wagons.

Then Bostwick sent the clerk to his house, and the clerk came back with two buffalo robes over his shoulder, the two best robes in the territory, people said afterward, winter robes with the hair thick and dark and soft as a cat, tanned by Shoshone women up on the Bear River and painted on the skin side with red and yellow lines. Bostwick put them in Ifan’s arms himself.

Ifan stood holding them. He thought: I am not fit to wrap myself in this. This was not given to me. He walked into the back of the bishop’s shop, where the old man had slept alone on a cot for nine years under a dirt roof that came down in mud on him every time it rained, and he took the bishop’s hammer and the bishop’s tacks and nailed the first robe flat to the rafters over the cot, hair side down, so that when it rained the bishop would sleep dry under a buffalo for the rest of his life.

The other he carried home to Gwen, along with the two pairs of blankets, at first light. She was asleep by the cold fire in her dress. He did not wake her. He nailed the second robe to the rafters over the plank table, where the home missionaries had sat in September and the roof had dribbled dirt on the young clerk’s ledger, so that whoever came to that table to ask the questions next time would sit in a dry place. Then he put both pairs of blankets over his wife and lay down on the floor beside her and slept until noon.

On Tuesday morning the wagons went east out of the city, sixteen of them from all the wards together, with four mules to a wagon and young men walking beside, and half the city out in the cold to watch them go up toward the mouth of Emigration Canyon. Somewhere in one of them, under thirty pairs of Mackinaw blankets and a widow’s boots and three hats and a child’s red mittens, there was a grey flannel shawl with a red stripe, folded in four. Gwen did not go to watch. She said she had seen wagons.

V. Fours

Tuesday and after

Bostwick came to the bishop’s shop on Tuesday evening to settle a bill for boots, and he saw the robe nailed over the cot in the back room, and knew it.

Bishop, who gave you that?

Brother Pugh, said the bishop.

Bostwick stood looking at it for some time. Then he said that a man likes to think he is the only one in the city who believes a thing all the way down, and it does him good to find out he isn’t. And the next day there came to the Pughs’ door, from Bostwick, four oxen, and four horses, and four sacks of flour, and four hundred dollars in gold in a canvas bag, and his claim to four acres of bottom land along the Jordan with water on it.

The ward talked of nothing else for a week. At the meeting on Thursday night, before the bishop came in, they were still at it on the benches. The Lord had paid Ifan Pugh before the wagons were out of sight. He had given one shawl at four in the morning and been paid in oxen by Wednesday. There it was, plain as day, cause and effect, and the Reformation was right, and the Book was right, and you only had to give.

The bishop came in and sat down at the table in front and asked them what they were all talking about. They told him.

He thought about it. Then he said, in the most words anybody in that ward ever heard him say at once:

Brother Pugh brought it at four in the morning, and he got fours. If he’d brought it in from his seat at the meeting Sunday night, I expect he’d have got eights. If he’d stood up in the Tabernacle Sunday noon and given it when his wife gave her stockings, sixteens. What comes back to you is a measure. It comes back about the size you let it out. When the good thing comes up in you, do it. Do it while it’s still up. Don’t sit in the yard all night with it. The other thing will grow in the dark and sit on you, and you’ll get to like it there.

Then he opened the Book and read.

For behold, this life is the time for men to prepare to meet God; yea, behold the day of this life is the day for men to perform their labors.

And now, as I said unto you before, as ye have had so many witnesses, therefore, I beseech of you that ye do not procrastinate the day of your repentance until the end; for after this day of life, which is given us to prepare for eternity, behold, if we do not improve our time while in this life, then cometh the night of darkness wherein there can be no labor performed.

— Alma 34:32–33

Ifan was on the back bench, in a new coat. He did not say anything. But when the call came at the end of the month for more teams and more teamsters, because the first wagons had found the handcart people at last and it was worse than anybody had said, he was in the bishop’s yard with his four oxen yoked before the bishop had finished reading the paper.

VI. The Stripe

November 30, 1856

The news came back over the mountains in pieces all November, and every piece was worse. The first of the handcart companies had been found on the Sweetwater out of flour, and on the night after they climbed the ridge in a snowstorm thirteen of them had died. The second was farther back, at the Platte, and they had sat in the snow waiting for help for nine days, and every morning there were more of them who did not get up. People in the valley said the names of the places as though they were the names of people. Rocky Ridge. Devil’s Gate. Red Bluffs.

The last of them came in on the last Sunday in November, in a hundred wagons, while the people were at meeting. The President dismissed the afternoon meeting and told the sisters to go home and get those people something to eat, and wash them and nurse them, and receive them as their own children. The street filled with people and wagons and the steam off the teams.

Gwen went down. She had a wool coat now and a new shawl from Bostwick’s store, blue, which she did not like as well. She went along the wagons looking for her husband’s oxen, and found them, thin as rails with their hip bones up like tent poles, and Ifan walking beside them with his beard white with frost and his face burned black by the wind, and she did not run to him, because of what was in the wagon.

In the back of the wagon, among the others lying in the straw under Bostwick’s blankets, there was a girl of about twelve sitting up. Her feet were wrapped in rags and her hands were wrapped in rags and her face had the look all their faces had, of somebody who had been looking a long way off for a long time. Around her shoulders, crossed on her chest and tied behind, she had a grey flannel shawl with a red stripe along the border.

Ifan said she had been put into his wagon on the far side of the pass, wrapped in it up to the eyes, and had not taken it off since. Her mother and father were in the ground somewhere back along the Platte. She was from Stockport. She had not said ten words the whole way.

Gwen climbed up into the wagon. She said something to the girl in Welsh, which the girl could not have understood, and the girl looked at her for the first time. Then Gwen took the shawl off the girl’s shoulders, very gently, and shook the snow out of it over the side of the wagon, and put it back around her, and crossed it on her chest, and tied it behind, tight, the way women tie them at home, so that it would stay.

They took her home to the adobe house, which had a fire in it now, and sat her at the plank table under the buffalo robe, and gave her bread.

Background

The verse

If a man would hasten towards the good, he should keep his thought away from evil; if a man does what is good slothfully, his mind delights in evil.Dhammapada 116, tr. F. Max Müller (1881)

In the commentary, a poor brahmin and his wife own a single upper garment between them, so only one can go out at a time. She goes to hear the Buddha teach by day, and he goes at night. Moved by the teaching, he wants to give the garment to the Buddha, but selfish thoughts hold him back through the first and second watches of the night. In the last watch he lays it at the Buddha’s feet and shouts three times, “I have conquered!” King Pasenadi, who is in the audience, rewards him with pairs of garments, doubling each time as the brahmin gives them all away, until the king makes him keep two pairs. He gives the brahmin two costly blankets, and the brahmin hangs one as a canopy over the Buddha’s bed and the other in his own house over the place where a monk takes his meals. Seeing this, the king gives him “the gift of fours.” When the monks marvel at how quickly the good deed was rewarded, the Buddha says that had he given in the first watch he would have received sixteens, and in the middle watch eights. Great Salt Lake City, 1856

After grasshoppers and drought destroyed the crops of 1855, Utah went into a hungry winter. That September, Brigham Young called for a Reformation, and each ward got home missionaries who visited every family and asked searching questions about their religious lives. On October 4, Franklin D. Richards reached the city with word that two late handcart companies, the Willie and Martin companies, were still on the trail. At general conference the next morning, Young called for wagons, teams, provisions and drivers. Lucy Meserve Smith remembered that “the sisters stripped off their Peticoats stockings and every thing they could spare, right there in the Tabernacle,” as recorded by the Church Historian’s Press. The first rescue party left on October 7 with sixteen wagonloads of supplies.

The Willie company was found on the Sweetwater River, out of flour. After the climb over Rocky Ridge, thirteen people died in a single night. The Martin company, caught by a blizzard near the last crossing of the North Platte, lost fifty-six people while it waited for help at Red Bluffs. The Willie company reached Salt Lake on November 9 and the Martin company on November 30, and Young told the Saints to take the survivors into their homes. The Welsh in the valley had come mostly through the work of Dan Jones, who led more than 300 Welsh converts out of Liverpool in 1849 and oversaw the first Welsh Book of Mormon.
