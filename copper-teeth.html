Copper Teeth

Amen I say to thee, this day thou shalt be with me in paradise.
— Luke 23:43

Voilà comme je l’ai entendu, on the Bayou Road.

I. The Swamp

The cypress back of New Orleans · spring 1738

The old women on the Bayou Road will tell you there were five hundred of them, less one. The register of the Superior Council says nine. Both are true in their way. Nine men in the cypress back of the city, where the ground goes soft and then goes to water and then goes to something that is neither, could be five hundred when they came out at night. They took hogs from the plantations at Chapitoulas and rice from the barges and powder from wherever powder was. They had a camp on a shell mound in the swamp that nobody could find twice, and a fire that nobody ever saw, and a leader called Jupiter who had run from the Company’s plantation across the river so many times that he had stopped counting the times and the King’s law had not.

The law was simple. A slave who ran for a month lost his ears and was branded on one shoulder with the lily of France. Twice, the other shoulder, and the cord behind the knee. Three times, death. There was not a man on the shell mound who had both his ears.

One night in March a stranger came through the water to the edge of the firelight and stood there until they saw him. He was tall and the color of a pecan shell, and his back and arms were so crossed with old scars that in the firelight he looked like something woven. When he opened his mouth to speak, his teeth were the color of a new copper sou. Nobody ever learned why. He said his name was Jean-Baptiste, which was the name the priest had given him, and he said, Let me live with you.

They brought him to Jupiter. Jupiter was sitting on a cypress knee mending a net, and he did not stop mending it. He looked at the man a long time. Then he spat into the fire.

No, he said. Not this one.

Afterward the young ones asked him why, and Jupiter said a thing they remembered when it came true. That man would cut off his mother’s breast and eat it if somebody paid him. He would open his father’s throat and drink. I have seen that look in white men and I have seen it in black ones. Send him back into the water.

But Pierrot, who was nineteen and Jupiter’s right hand and loved by everybody, had taken a liking to the stranger. Jean-Baptiste had carried his load through the trembling ground without being asked. He had cleaned Pierrot’s musket and found him the good eels. So Pierrot brought him back to the cypress knee two days later and said, Papa, this one is a good servant to us. Do him the favor. And because it was Pierrot asking, Jupiter shrugged and went on mending.

II. The Ladder

New Orleans · April 1738

The habitants of Chapitoulas got tired of losing their hogs. They went into the swamp with soldiers from the garrison and dogs and a Choctaw tracker the Council paid in cloth, and on the fourth day they came out with all of them, roped neck to neck, the whole five hundred, the whole nine, Jean-Baptiste walking last.

The Superior Council sat in judgment. It did not take them long. They were runaways, and most of them were runaways for the third time, and they had stolen, and two of them had fired on the King’s soldiers. The sentence was that they be hanged by the neck on the square before the church until they were dead, and their bodies exposed.

Then there was a difficulty, which was that there was nobody to hang them.

The colony had had a hangman. It did not have one now. The procureur general walked through the town with the warrant in his hand asking, and the soldiers looked at their boots, and the carpenters who had built the gallows said they were carpenters, and a tavernkeeper said that for a hundred livres he would do it and then, when his wife heard, said that he had been joking. Nobody in New Orleans would put his hands on the rope. It was the kind of thing that stays on a family.

So they went down into the prison and stood in front of Jupiter.

Hang these men, said the procureur, and you will have your life, and your freedom besides, and a piece of land, and a ration from the King’s stores as long as you live.

Jupiter looked at the men he had lived with on the shell mound, and he looked at Pierrot, who was nineteen, and he said nothing at all, and that was his answer.

They asked the next man and the next. They asked all of them, one by one, down the line of the cell, and every man of them looked at the others and shook his head. They asked Pierrot last but one, and Pierrot laughed at them.

Last of all they asked the scarred one with the copper teeth.

Oui, he said. Yes, certainly.

The old women on the Bayou Road say that Pierrot did not look at him after that, not once, not in the cell and not on the cart and not at the foot of the ladder, and that this was harder for Jean-Baptiste than anything Jupiter could have said. But the old women were not there. What is in the register is that on a morning in April 1738 nine men were hanged before the church of Saint Louis, and that a slave called Jean-Baptiste received his letter of freedom from the Council the same week, and two arpents of land on the Bayou Road outside the town, and his ration.

He did it well, for a man who had never done it. He was strong, and he was quick, and he did not hesitate on the ladder. When the last of them, who was Jupiter, had been turned off and had stopped moving, the crowd before the church was quiet for a long time, and then went home.

III. The Four Winds

1738–1765

That summer the garrison brought in a band of thieves from the south, from down the river below the English Turn, where there were deserters living with runaways in the marsh. The Council sentenced them, and again there was the difficulty, and they asked the leader and he refused, and they asked every man in the band and every man refused.

Then somebody on the Council said, The other day a man hanged nine. Where is he?

Someone had seen him on the Bayou Road, clearing his land. They sent a soldier, and he came, and they said, Hang these men and you will be paid, and he said, Oui, certainly, and he did it, and was paid.

After that the Council made it permanent. The executioner of high justice of the colony of Louisiana would have his land and his house on it. He would have thirty livres for a hanging and forty for the wheel and ten for a flogging, and a ration from the King’s stores. And because it had always been so in France, the clothes of the men he hanged would be his. Later they brought men in from the west, from across the river and the bayous beyond it, and later from the north, from Pointe Coupée and the Natchez country, and he hanged them all. The old women on the Bayou Road say he hanged five hundred from each of the four winds. The register says what it says. After the bands there were the single ones, one this month and two the next, a soldier who had killed his sergeant, a cook who had put something in a soup, a sailor, a thief of horses, a woman.

The woman was called Babet, and she was a laundress, and she was hanged in the summer of 1741 for setting a fire. At the top of the ladder she looked down at him and said, in front of everybody, I know your face now. I will come back for you. He turned her off without a word, the way he did them all.

He lived alone on the Bayou Road. He had married once, a woman from the Company’s plantation, and she had left him the first year, saying the bed smelled of other people’s coats. He planted orange trees on his land, because orange trees did well in that country, and they did. In March the whole road smelled of them. He had a copper still, the kind the apothecaries used, and in the spring he made orange-flower water in it, and put it on his face and hands, and pushed the blossoms into the buttonhole of whatever coat he was wearing. The coats were always good coats. They had always belonged to someone.

And every morning a boy came from the King’s plantation across the river with his ration: a sack of rice, a pot of milk from the King’s cows, and a crock of fresh butter. He cooked the rice in the milk until it was soft, the way they did in France, and put the butter on it, and that was what he ate. People on the road said it was a dish for a child or an invalid. He did not care. It was the softest thing in his life.

Nobody spoke to him who did not have to. At the market on the levee the women took his coins with two fingers, and once a man he did not know walked up to him on the Rue de Chartres and spat in his face, and Jean-Baptiste wiped it off with the lace cuff of a dead man’s shirt and walked on. Children threw mud at his door. At Mass he stood alone at the back, by the font, and nobody would take the holy water after him. The Council’s clerk, when he paid him, put the coins on the table rather than into his hand.

He was a free man, and he could sign his name, and he had land and orange trees and a coat for every day of the month. He did not think about the men. He was careful not to think about them, the way a man walking on the trembling ground in the swamp is careful where he puts his feet.

Twenty-eight years went by like that.

IV. The Coat

New Orleans · March 1766

That was the spring nobody knew who the king was.

Louis had given the colony away to his cousin in Spain, people said, in a treaty, years ago, the way you would give someone a horse you were tired of feeding. Nobody had believed it until the Spanish governor actually came up the river in the first week of March with a sour face and not enough soldiers to take possession of anything. The flag on the square was still French. The Council still sat. Everybody was waiting to see what would happen, and in the meantime there were three men to be hanged for robbing a ship’s purser on the levee and leaving him dead in the mud.

Jean-Baptiste was past sixty by then. He did not know exactly how far past. His hands had begun to shake in the mornings, and his knees did not like ladders.

The first man went well enough. The second man he turned off the ladder and the knot was wrong, and the man did not die. He hung there making a sound. Jean-Baptiste took hold of his legs to pull, the way he had done a hundred times, and his arms had no strength in them, and the man kicked him away. He took hold again. He had to climb back up the ladder in the end and do something to the rope with his hands, which were shaking, and when he came down again the crowd was making a sound too, a low sound, worse than the man’s. With the third man it went the same.

The Council met two days later. Jean-Baptiste stood at the back of the room in a blue coat that had belonged to a ship’s mate and listened to them talk about him as if he were a mule. He was quick once. Now he is cruel. It is a disgrace to the King, whichever king it is. We will find another one. They took his ration away. They let him keep his land, because it had been written down.

That was a Thursday. On Friday morning the boy did not come from across the river with the rice and the milk and the butter. Jean-Baptiste had kept a little of each back, out of an old man’s habit, so he cooked the last of the rice in the last of the milk himself, slowly, the way he liked it, and put it on the table under a cloth with the crock of butter beside it. He took the coat he liked best, an old brown one, worn soft at the elbows, which had belonged to a Swiss soldier hanged in 1752 for striking an officer. He cut a handful of orange blossoms. He took the flask of orange-flower water. And he walked down the Bayou Road to where the bayou ran slow and brown under the oaks, and took off his shirt, and bathed.

He had not done that in years, washed his whole body in moving water. The scars on his back stood up in the cold. He scrubbed his arms with sand. Then he put on the Swiss soldier’s coat over a clean shirt, and pushed the blossoms through the buttonhole, and put the orange water on his face and neck and the backs of his hands, and walked home smelling of a garden, and sat down at his table in front of the bowl.

The boy, Toussaint, set a basin of water beside him for his hands. Toussaint was ten. He was an orphan of the smallpox whom the Council had bound to the executioner two years before, to learn the trade, because nobody else in New Orleans would take him, and he did not know yet that there was no trade left to learn.

Jean-Baptiste lifted the cloth from the bowl. The steam came up.

Somebody knocked on the door.

V. The Door

The same morning

Fanchon had been on her knees in the Ursulines’ chapel since before light. She was a free woman of color, forty-four years old, a washerwoman by trade, and a member of the congregation the nuns had started for the women of the town, where planters’ wives and laundresses and slave women knelt on the same floor and said the same rosary. She taught the catechism to the new ones off the ships on Sunday afternoons, in French and in what she had of Bambara, and she was known for not saying much and for something else that people were careful how they spoke of.

When she got up from her knees that morning she stood a moment in the aisle and asked, the way she always did, Where should I go today? And the way it sometimes happened, she saw it: a table on the Bayou Road, a bowl of rice and milk with the steam coming up, a crock of butter, a man in a brown coat with orange flowers in the buttonhole. She knew whose house it was. Everybody knew whose house it was.

She thought, Will he let me in?

And she saw that too. So she put on her headcloth and took her basket and walked out through the town gate and down the Bayou Road between the orange trees, and knocked.

When he opened the door and saw her standing there, a woman from the town, from the church, on his step, with her basket on her arm, he did not understand at first what she wanted. Nobody had ever knocked on that door who was not a soldier or a clerk. Then he understood that she had only come, and something went through him that he did not have a name for, it had been so long. He thought, I have hanged men for twenty-eight years. This is the day they took the rice away. This is the day I cooked it myself. And she is standing at my door.

He took the bowl from the table and gave it to her. He put her in his one good chair, which had belonged to nobody, which he had bought, and spread the fresh butter on the rice for her with his own spoon, and stood beside her with a palmetto fan and fanned her, because it was warm in the house and he did not know what else to do with his hands.

Fanchon looked at the bowl, and at him. She could see what it cost him. The rice was the last of it; anybody could see that. And he wanted it. He had wanted it since the boy did not come.

Monsieur, she said, eat your own rice.

He stood there.

Sit down and eat it, she said. Toussaint, take the fan and fan him.

So the old hangman sat down on the bench at his own table in a dead man’s coat and ate the rice and milk with the butter on it, every spoonful, slowly, while the boy stood behind him moving the palmetto back and forth, back and forth, and the smell of the orange flowers went around the room. When he was done he wiped his mouth and got up and took the fan back from the boy and went on fanning her, and she let him.

She had brought bread in her basket. She ate a little. When she had finished she folded her hands and began the grace after meals, Agimus tibi gratias, and then she began to say a few things to him, plain things, the kind she said to the women off the ships on Sunday.

He could not hear her. He heard her voice, but the words would not stay in him. They ran off him like water off a duck. She stopped.

Monsieur. Where are you?

Madame, he said, I have done hard things for a long time. I have killed a great many men. When you talk, I see them. I see them on the ladder. I cannot hear you for them.

Fanchon thought for a while. She knew what she was going to say, and she knew it was not the whole truth, the way you know a bandage is not the whole cure. But you cannot dress a wound that a man will not hold still for.

Did you do it because you wanted to, she said, or did somebody make you?

The King made me, he said. The Council, with the King’s warrant. Every one of them, with a paper.

Well then, said Fanchon. If that is so, what have you done wrong?

He sat with that for a moment in the brown coat. It went into him a little way, as far as it could go, the way a peg goes into soft wood. If the woman from the church said so. And for the first time in twenty-eight years the men on the ladder stood back, a little, enough.

Bon, he said. Go on, madame. Please.

So she told him about the two thieves who were hanged on either side of Our Lord. One of them mocked, she said. The other one only said one thing. He said it hanging there, at the very end, when there was no time left to do anything good, no time to give alms or go to Mass or make it right with anybody. He had nothing in his hands at all. And here is what he said, and here is what he was told.

And he said to Jesus: Lord, remember me when thou shalt come into thy kingdom. And Jesus said to him: Amen I say to thee, this day thou shalt be with me in paradise.

— Luke 23:42–43

This day, said Fanchon. Not after. This one.

Jean-Baptiste was quiet a long time. The boy had stopped fanning without either of them noticing. Outside, a mockingbird was going through every song it knew in one of the orange trees, and the blossoms were dropping on the path in the warm air, and he heard it all. He heard each word she had said, as if each one had been set down in front of him on the table, one at a time, like the coins the Council’s clerk would not put into his hand. He did not see the ladder. He did not see anything but the room, and the woman in his good chair, and the boy, and the light coming in the door.

He found that he was very patient. He found that he could wait for whatever was coming, and that it did not matter much what it was.

VI. The Road

The Bayou Road · that afternoon

When she rose to go he walked her up the road toward the town, a little way, the way you walk a guest. He did not take her arm. He walked half a step behind her, in the Swiss soldier’s coat with the flowers in it, and people working in their gardens along the road straightened up and stared, the washerwoman from the Ursulines and the hangman, walking together in the middle of the afternoon.

At the bend before the town gate he stopped and bowed to her, a real bow, the way he had seen gentlemen do on the square, and she put out her hand and he took it. It was the first hand anyone had given him in twenty-eight years that was not tied. Then he turned around and walked back toward his house.

The cattle of the town grazed loose on the commons along the Bayou Road, and nobody minded them much. That afternoon there was a young red heifer standing in the road where it ran between two ditches, a pretty thing with a white star, and when Jean-Baptiste came down the road toward her she lowered her head and watched him come.

Toussaint saw it from the door. He said afterward that the old man was looking up into the orange trees and did not see her. He said the heifer came at a trot, then a run, and did not use her horns. She struck him with her shoulder, the whole weight of her, and he went over backward into the ditch, and his head struck the edge of a cypress stump that had been there since before the town, and he did not get up. The heifer stood over him for a moment. Then she went off across the commons at a walk, and nobody ever claimed her.

Toussaint ran and got down in the ditch with him. The old man was on his back in the brown coat with the orange blossoms still in the buttonhole and his copper teeth showing, and his eyes were open, and there was nothing in his face at all. No fear. Nothing waiting. Toussaint said it looked like the face of a man listening to someone tell him something good.

The old women on the Bayou Road say the heifer had Babet’s eyes. They say it the way they say everything, as if it were not their business whether you believe it.

VII. The Congregation

The Ursuline chapel · the following Sunday

The women of the congregation met after Vespers on Sunday as they always did, and nobody could talk about anything else.

Fanchon was in his house. Fanchon ate at his table.

Twenty-eight years he hanged people on that square. They took away his rice on Thursday. Friday he gave it to Fanchon, Friday he heard the catechism, Friday a cow killed him in the road. All in one day.

And where is he now? said a planter’s wife, a pious woman who had lost a brother at Natchez. Tell me that. Where does a man like that go?

Nobody said anything. They all looked at the oldest of them.

Her name was Marguerite, and she belonged to the nuns. She had come off a ship from Senegal before the town had a church, and she had taught Fanchon her prayers thirty years before, and the Ursulines themselves asked her things. She was so old that she sat on the floor against the wall during the meetings, with her legs out straight in front of her, and nobody thought it was strange.

He went where the thief went, she said.

The thief on the cross asked, said the planter’s wife. He had faith. This man heard a grace after meals and a few words from a washerwoman. Forgive me, Fanchon. A few words.

Twenty-eight years, somebody else said. And a few words.

Marguerite looked at them for a while from the floor, one at a time, the planters’ wives in their silk and the laundresses with their sleeves still damp and the slave women off the ships who did not have enough French yet to follow and were watching her face instead. She took her time.

Don’t weigh it, she said. You are not the one who weighs it. You hear sermons every Sunday, an hour long, and you go home the same. He heard one thing, and it was the right thing, and he went quiet. That is all a word is for. And she said it to them the way the priest read it out of Solomon.

The words of the wise are heard in silence, more than the cry of a prince among fools.

— Ecclesiastes 9:17

Then she closed her eyes, which meant the meeting was over.

•••

They buried him at the back of the cemetery, along the fence, where they put the ones nobody would claim. Fanchon paid the gravedigger. She and the boy were the only ones there, and the boy, who had nowhere now to go, went home with her and learned to wash linen instead.

The Council found his coats in a chest in the house, twenty-eight years of them, the coats of soldiers and sailors and thieves and cooks, and one dress, a laundress’s, folded at the bottom. Nobody would buy them. In the end the Ursulines took them for the poor, and that winter you could see them all over New Orleans, on the levee and in the market and on the Bayou Road, the dead men’s coats walking around on living men who did not know whose they were.

The orange trees went on blooming on his two arpents for years after the house fell in. Every March the whole road smelled of them, and people going in and out of the town gate would stop and break off a sprig for the buttonhole, without thinking whose trees they were, and walk on.

Background

The verse

Even though a speech be a thousand (of words), but made up of senseless words, one word of sense is better, which if a man hears, he becomes quiet.Dhammapada 100, tr. F. Max Müller (1881)

In the commentary, a scarred man with copper-colored teeth joins a band of thieves whose leader distrusts him on sight. When the band is captured, no one will act as executioner, and every thief refuses the offer of his life in exchange for killing his companions, until the newcomer agrees. He becomes the city’s executioner for fifty-five years. When he is dismissed in old age for bungling his work, he prepares the milk-porridge he will no longer be given, bathes, and dresses in the old clothes, flowers and perfume that came with the post. At that moment the elder Säriputta comes to his door. The man gives him the porridge; the elder tells him to eat it himself, then asks whether he killed of his own will. The king made me. Then what wrong did you do? His mind grows calm and he listens. Walking home, he is killed by a cow, and the Buddha tells the doubting monks that he has gone to a heavenly world, because one word that brings peace is worth more than a thousand. New Orleans, 1738–1766

French Louisiana ran on the Code Noir, which Louis XV extended to the colony in 1724. Under the code, an enslaved person who ran away for a month lost his ears and was branded with a fleur-de-lis; a second time, the hamstring was cut and the other shoulder branded; a third time meant death (the text). The colony struggled to find anyone who would carry out its sentences. In 1725 the Company of the Indies freed an enslaved man named Louis Congo and made him public executioner, with land outside New Orleans, a ration, and a fee for each flogging, hanging or breaking on the wheel. He was attacked at least twice, and the records of him stop after 1737. Jean-Baptiste, his band and the people of this story are invented.

The Ursuline nuns arrived in 1727. Their convent on Chartres Street, rebuilt between 1748 and 1753, still stands. The nuns taught the catechism to enslaved women with great success, and in 1730 they sponsored a laywomen’s confraternity whose members ranged from plantation mistresses to women of color. By the 1730s orange and citron trees were doing well in New Orleans. In 1762 France ceded Louisiana to Spain, and the first Spanish governor, Antonio de Ulloa, arrived in March 1766 without the troops to take control. After the colonists drove him out in the Rebellion of 1768, General Alejandro O’Reilly condemned its leaders to hang. According to an early history of the colony, no hangman could be found in all of Louisiana, so the five men were shot.
