Honey on the Knife

They are like a child who tries to eat a little honey smeared on the edge of a knife.
— Sutra of Forty-two Sections 22

Iwa gachi naneun deureotda, on Little River Turnpike.

I. Shirts

Annandale, Virginia · spring 1995

Han Myeong-su had a dry cleaner in a strip of shops on Little River Turnpike, between a Korean bakery and a store that sold rice cookers and phone cards. He had come over in 1979 and pressed other men's shirts for six years before he could buy a counter of his own, and he still pressed every collar himself, because a collar was where people looked. His wife had died the winter their daughter turned twelve. After that it was the two of them and the machines.

Seo-yeon worked the counter after school, and then after college, when she had a degree in accounting from George Mason and no particular wish to use it. She was quiet at the register. She had a way of looking at a stain as if it had told her something, and a coolness about her that the aunts at the bakery next door said was like water in a stone basin. Customers came back.

Men came back. A deacon's son from a Presbyterian church in Centreville brought one shirt at a time, so he would have a reason to come back on Thursday. A lawyer from Tysons brought shirts that were already clean. A Marine lieutenant from Quantico brought his dress blues in March and asked whether she ever went into the city. A man who owned three beauty-supply stores in Northeast Washington brought his mother, who looked at Seo-yeon's hands while Seo-yeon wrote the ticket. Their mothers called the house in the evening. Mr. Han did not know what to say to them, so he said she was young.

Kwon Jang-ho did not bring shirts. He was her mother's cousin's son, two years older, and he fixed the machines. He had grown up in the back of the shop with her, doing homework on the floor under the conveyor while the bagged suits went around over their heads. When they were small the aunts used to say those two would marry, and laugh. He was the only one who had never heard it as a joke. At twenty-five he could take apart a solvent machine in an afternoon and put it back so it ran quieter than new, and he came by the shop more than the machines needed.

One night in April, after the gate was down, Mr. Han sat on the bench by the press with his hands on his knees.

I can't give you to all of them, he said. Every one I say no to, his mother takes her coats somewhere else. He was quiet for a while. Your mother used to say you would have been happy at the temple. I never let her say it in front of you.

He meant the temple in Centreville, where he went on the first and fifteenth of the lunar month and stood at the back. He meant, could she leave home. Shave her head. Become a sunim.

When she was five her grandmother had taken her to the public bath in Seoul, sat her on a low stool, and poured the first bowl of hot water over her head. She had never forgotten it: the heat going down over her ears and shoulders all at once, and every tight thing in her coming loose. Her father's question felt like that.

Yes, she said. Appa. Yes.

II. The Candles

Centreville · winter 1996

The temple was a brick ranch house and a converted horse barn on four acres off Braddock Road, with a gravel lot and a stone lantern somebody had driven down from New Jersey in the back of a van. The Dharma hall was the barn. In the winter it held the cold like a well.

As a postulant she did what she was told. She washed rice and scrubbed pots and cut radishes, and she was given the hall to keep. In her week she rose at half past two, swept the floor from the altar to the door, filled the water bowl, and lit the two candles before the abbot came across the yard at three with the wooden moktak to wake the house.

On the fourth morning of her second week she lit the candles and did not get up.

Nothing had happened. The flame stood on its wick in the cold room the way a person stands who has nowhere else to be. It leaned when the furnace came on and straightened when it stopped. She looked at it, and went on looking, and at some point the one who was looking set itself down the way you set down a bag you have carried so long you forgot it was in your hand. She did not pick it up again. When the abbot came in with the moktak she was still kneeling there, and he looked at her face, and struck the moktak, and the morning went on.

At breakfast the cook put the scorched rice from the bottom of the pot into her bowl.

The cook was a small woman of nearly seventy in a quilted gray vest, with a scarf over her hair and forearms like a baker's. She had been widowed at twenty-two in the first summer of the war, and had cooked for thirty years at a nuns' monastery in the south of Korea before her son brought her to Virginia. Everyone called her Bosal-nim, which is what you call a laywoman at a temple, and which also means bodhisattva, though nobody thought about that. She spoke very little. She gave the scorched rice to no one. That morning she scraped it out of the pot in one brown sheet and put it in the postulant's bowl and went back to the stove.

Seo-yeon went to Korea that spring. She studied four years at a seminary for nuns in the mountains and took the full precepts, three hundred and forty-eight of them, and came back to Centreville in 2002 in gray robes with the Dharma name Cheongryeon. Blue Lotus. The aunts at the bakery said it suited her. She had the same coolness. People who had known her at the counter found it hard to look straight at her and hard to look away.

III. The Hermitage

Page County, Virginia · 2008

A dentist's widow in McLean left the temple a cabin and twelve acres in the Page Valley, south of Luray, between the long wall of Massanutten and the Blue Ridge. You reached it by a gravel lane that ran through Delmer Breeden's pasture. Halfway along, the lane bent around a round hollow in the grass with a sycamore growing out of the bottom of it. After rain the hollow held water. The cows went around it.

That's a sink, Delmer told her the first week. He was a tall man in his sixties who kept beef cattle and drove a school bus. Whole valley's hollow underneath. Luray Caverns is just the part they charge you to see.

In Korea old nuns lived alone in hermitages up the mountain, and Cheongryeon asked to live at the cabin alone. The abbot did not like it. The board did not like it. She asked a second time, and then they said yes, because she was the kind of person who did not ask a third time, and because it was a long drive and nobody else wanted to go.

She sat in the front room in the morning and the evening. She grew lettuce and perilla and long green peppers in a plot behind the cabin, and fed a cat that was not hers. There was no bath in the cabin, only a kitchen sink, so a previous owner had built a shed out back with a shower and a propane water heater. On the first and fifteenth she went down to Centreville in whatever car the temple sent.

Jang-ho brought her groceries. Somebody had to, and he drove all over the state anyway, fixing machines for the Korean cleaners from Frederick to Fredericksburg; by one newspaper's count four out of five cleaners around Washington were Korean, and he knew the back room of every one. Once a month his white van came up Delmer's lane with rice and tofu and kimchi from the H Mart and a box of whatever his mother had made. He was divorced by then. He fixed the pump. He fixed the pilot on the water heater, and kept the key to the shed. He sat on the cabin step and drank the tea she made and looked at her.

Aren't you lonely out here?

No.

I would be.

I know, she said.

In May she went down to Centreville for ten days before the Buddha's Birthday to fold paper petals with the bosals, thousands of them, pink and white and green, glued around wire frames into lotus lanterns to hang over the parking lot. While she was gone Jang-ho drove up the lane with the key on his ring. He was good with small wires. He put a camera in the light fixture of the shed.

In June he came back with groceries and said he wanted to look at the heater. He was out there a while.

In August he sat on the step and asked her to leave the robes. He had money put away. He would take her anywhere she wanted, California, Seoul, anywhere. He had loved her since they were eight years old under the conveyor, and she knew it, and she had gone and done this.

Jang-ho, she said. Go home.

He stood up. You'll be sorry, he said.

She was already looking at the lettuce.

IV. The Link

Centreville · October 2008

That fall the banks were failing on the television, and men who had been laid off stopped buying suits, and the cleaners on Little River Turnpike stood around their counters at three in the afternoon with nothing to press. On a Tuesday in October a link went around.

It came first in emails, with a subject line in Korean that said you should see what the temple's nun is. Then it was on a community message board where people sold used Hondas and asked for immigration lawyers. Somebody had it taken down. Somebody put it back up. It was of her, in the shed, and it is not going to be described here. Enough people did that.

The cook knew before anyone told her, because of the silence when she came into the temple kitchen on Wednesday and five bosals stopped talking at once.

The abbot drove to Page County himself. Cheongryeon was on her knees in the pepper plants. He stood at the end of the row and told her, badly, the way men tell such things, and she listened with dirt on her hands. When he was done she got up and asked him where it would have been. Then she walked out to the shed and stood in the doorway and looked up.

The deputy who came from Luray was a young man with a crew cut who took off his hat in the cabin and did not know where to look. He stood on a kitchen chair and took the camera out of the fixture and put it in a paper bag. Unlawful creation of an image of another, he said. In Virginia that was a Class 1 misdemeanor. Up to a year.

And putting it on the internet, the abbot said.

The deputy turned his hat over in his hands. Sir, I'm not sure there's a law for that part. Not yet.

Cheongryeon signed the statement with both her names. The deputy asked whether she was all right, and whether there was somewhere else she could stay.

I live here, she said.

On Sunday she came down to Centreville and walked into the Dharma hall and sat in her place on the left, where she had always sat, and did the bows. People looked at the floor. A few looked at her. After the service the board met downstairs in the fellowship room, at folding tables, with barley tea in paper cups. Mr. Yoon Sang-cheol presided. He owned fourteen cleaners between Chantilly and Springfield, and he had paid for the new roof on the barn.

A man who sold insurance said that for the good of the temple, and for her own good, she should perhaps return to lay life for a while, until people forgot. The Korean papers in Annandale had not printed anything yet. They would.

A woman from the flower committee asked the abbot straight out whether a sunim could still be a sunim after this. After so many people. Whether she was still clean.

Someone else asked what she had been doing out there by herself in the first place. What she had expected.

The abbot said he would write to the order in Seoul.

They asked Cheongryeon if she wanted to say anything. She had been sitting at the end of a table with her hands folded in her sleeves.

I'm living the way I lived before, she said. If the temple wants the robes back, it can have them. I'll fold them.

The cook was upstairs in the kitchen the whole time, washing the rice for Monday.

V. The Sink

Page County · November 2008

Nobody knows why Jang-ho drove out to the Page Valley one night in the middle of November. To take the light fixture down himself, maybe. To say he was sorry, or to say something worse. It was raining hard, the first real rain in weeks. Cheongryeon was asleep and heard nothing. Delmer Breeden, in the farmhouse at the head of the lane, heard nothing either.

In the morning Delmer's cows were bawling at the fence. He walked out in his boots. Where the sink had been there was a hole in the pasture thirty feet across, with the sycamore lying over it like a dropped match, and the white van standing on its nose in brown water at the bottom. Jang-ho was inside. The rescue squad came out from Luray, and it took them until afternoon to bring him up.

Delmer said he had driven that lane at dusk with a round bale on the front of the tractor, and it was solid. He said it to the deputy and to the man from the paper and to everybody at the feed store, and he was not a man who told stories. The ground had opened under that van, he said, like a mouth.

On Little River Turnpike they said the same thing, with less doubt. The earth had swallowed him. The old women at the bakery said there was an old word for it, for the way the earth takes certain men, and that it had taken men before, in the Buddha's time and since. They said it the way you would say a man was hit by a train. Nobody said it was a judgment. Nobody had to.

Cheongryeon went to the funeral home in Annandale and chanted for him for an hour beside the closed casket while his mother sat in the front row and would not turn her head. Then she went back to the valley in the temple's car.

The next Sunday Mr. Yoon came into the temple kitchen with the abbot behind him. The cook was rolling kimbap on a bamboo mat for the lunch after service, sixty rolls, her knife laid crosswise on the board with a little sesame oil on the blade so the rice wouldn't stick.

He thought he'd get away with it, Mr. Yoon said. He sounded almost sorry for him.

The cook finished the roll and set it with the others. She picked up the knife and looked at the oil on the edge of it. Then she said something from the old sutra the nuns used to read in the evenings at the monastery where she cooked, the one they say was the first ever carried into China. She said it in Korean. When it went into the temple bulletin the next month, somebody found the English for it in a book printed in Chicago in 1906.

People cleave to their worldly possessions and selfish passions so blindly as to sacrifice their own lives for them. They are like a child who tries to eat a little honey smeared on the edge of a knife. The amount is by no means sufficient to appease his appetite, but he runs the risk of wounding his tongue.

— Sutra of Forty-two Sections 22

It tastes sweet, the cook said. For a while. Until it's ripe. She wiped the blade with a cloth and began to cut. Then it isn't sweet.

VI. The Leaf

Centreville · December 2008

The letter from Seoul did not come. The question did not go away. It was in the way the flower committee arranged its chrysanthemums with their backs to the left side of the hall, and in the way men who had brought shirts to the counter on Little River Turnpike thirteen years before now stopped talking when her name came up. At the December board meeting the woman from the flower committee asked it again. This time she did not ask the abbot. She turned around in her folding chair toward the kitchen pass-through, where the cook was wiping the counter.

Bosal-nim. You cooked for nuns thirty years. You tell us. Is she still clean?

The cook came out of the kitchen with the rag in her hand. She was eighty and walked as if her feet hurt, which they did. She stood at the end of the tables for a while before she said anything.

Has anyone here been to the lotus ponds in Washington? By the river. Kenilworth.

A few of them had. You took the children in July, when the lotuses were up.

Go after it rains, the cook said. Look at a leaf. The rain sits on it like quicksilver. Big drops. The wind comes and tips the leaf, and the water runs off, all of it, and the leaf is dry. You can touch it. Dry. She folded the rag. The water was on it. Where's the water now? Is the leaf wet?

Nobody answered. The furnace came on.

What happened to her happened, the cook said. It didn't stick. It doesn't stick to somebody like that. It's still sticking to all of you.

Then she turned to Mr. Yoon, who was looking at his hands.

And buy a house. In town, near people, near a bus. No sunim lives out in a field by herself again. Not because of her. Because of men.

Mr. Yoon bought an end-unit townhouse off Route 29 in January, with a parking space and a little square of yard and a bus stop at the corner, a mile and a half from the temple. The board wrote it into the minutes: a nun practicing alone would live there and not in the county. The cabin in the Page Valley was kept for retreats, and no one went to it alone. Delmer Breeden filled the sink in the pasture with forty tons of shot rock and graded the lane around it the long way.

•••

Cheongryeon moved in the first week of January with two boxes and a cushion. The cook came with her in Mr. Yoon's car and walked through the four small rooms as if she were checking a kitchen, and opened the refrigerator, and shut it, and put a foil packet on the counter before she left.

The next morning Cheongryeon got up at half past two and swept the front room and lit a candle in the window. A man across the lot was scraping frost off his windshield for the early shift at Dulles, and he looked up at the light and then went back to scraping. When she had sat, she went into the kitchen and opened the foil. It was the scorched rice from the bottom of the pot, in one brown sheet. She broke a piece into a bowl and poured hot water over it from the kettle, and watched the rice loosen and turn the water the color of weak tea, the way it did in every kitchen in Korea, and drank it.

Background

The verse

As long as the evil deed done does not bear fruit, the fool thinks it is like honey; but when it ripens, then the fool suffers grief.Dhammapada 69, tr. F. Max Müller (1881)

In the commentary, Uppalavaṇṇā, "the one with the color of the blue lotus," is a treasurer's daughter of Sāvatthī sought by so many kings and rich men that her father, unable to answer them all, asks whether she would leave the world. She agrees as gladly as if her head had been anointed with oil. As a new nun, taking her turn to light the lamp and sweep the Uposatha hall, she makes the flame her object of meditation and reaches awakening. Later she lives alone in a forest hut. A young man, her cousin, who has desired her since before she went forth, hides in the hut and assaults her; as he leaves, the earth opens and swallows him. The Buddha speaks this verse about the fool for whom evil tastes like honey until it ripens. When others wonder whether one who is awakened still feels desire, he answers that desire does not cling to such a person, as water does not cling to a lotus leaf, and he asks King Pasenadi to build a dwelling for nuns inside the city, so that they no longer live alone in the forest. At the editor's direction, this retelling changes one thing: there is no sexual assault. In its place is a different violation of her body and her solitude. Her cousin hides a camera where she bathes and spreads the images. Everything else follows the source in order. Read the full commentary story, or about Uppalavaṇṇā.

Northern Virginia and the Page Valley, 2008

Korean immigrants began building businesses in Annandale, along Little River Turnpike, in the 1990s, and the area is now often called Koreatown; Fairfax County has nearly 40,000 Korean residents. Dry cleaning was a Korean trade across the region: in 2001 the Washington Post counted about 80 percent of the area's 1,200 cleaners as Korean-owned (Capital News Service). Korea is one of the few countries where the full ordination of women survived; nuns of the Jogye Order keep some 348 precepts (Korea Times). The Sutra of Forty-two Sections is traditionally held to be the first Buddhist sutra translated into Chinese; the English here is D. T. Suzuki's, from Soyen Shaku's Sermons of a Buddhist Abbot (Chicago: Open Court, 1906). The temple, the cabin and all the people are fictional.

Most of Virginia's karst follows Interstate 81 through the Valley and Ridge, where acidic water dissolves limestone into caves and the ground above can drop without warning, usually slowly and sometimes all at once; the state's highway department has recorded about 500 sinkholes that damaged roads in thirty years (Virginia Energy). Luray Caverns, found in 1878, lies under the Page Valley. Secretly filming a person undressed where she expects privacy had been a Class 1 misdemeanor in Virginia since 1994 (§ 18.2-386.1), but spreading such images was not a crime in the state until 2014 (§ 18.2-386.2). The lotus ponds the cook remembers are at Kenilworth Park & Aquatic Gardens in northeast Washington, where the lotuses bloom in the hot months of summer.
