The Near Bank

Shake off the chains with which ye are bound, and come forth out of obscurity, and arise from the dust.
— 2 Nephi 1:23

The way it was told to me, on the Malin road.

I. The Dinner Calendar

Malin, Klamath County, Oregon · June 2022

Elder Bowman was nineteen and came from a seed-potato farm outside Sugar City, Idaho. His brother had served in the same mission six years ahead of him, and had been the kind of missionary a ward remembers, so that when the younger one stood up to introduce himself in sacrament meeting an old sister in the second row said, quite loud, Oh, it’s the little Bowman. It stuck. He did not mind. He had been the little Bowman all his life. He had also shot a bow since he was six years old, and had twice been state champion in the 4-H barebow, and the fall he was fourteen he had put an arrow through a cow elk at forty yards across a draw, but he did not bring that up, because his brother had told him that nobody in the mission field wants to hear about your elk.

He had been out nine months. His companion that transfer was Elder Pappas, from The Dalles, a calm, heavy-shouldered boy who wanted to be a diesel mechanic and read the scriptures the way he would read a service manual, slowly, looking for the torque specs.

Their area was the whole south end of the county, down to the California line: Merrill, Malin, the farms along the Lost River, and the dry bed of what had been Tule Lake before the government drained it for homesteads. They lived in a single-wide on Brother Dalton’s place, a mile out of Malin. It was the second summer of the shutoff. The year before, for the first time since water first ran in it in 1907, the Bureau had kept the A Canal shut the whole season, and this year there was a trickle, and the fields that did not have a deep well under them had gone to weeds and blowing dirt. The farmers who had deep wells ran them day and night, and the water table went down like a drink through a straw. All over the basin the shallow house wells were sucking air. People hauled water in the beds of their pickups. They showered at a sister-in-law’s.

The well at the single-wide quit on the ninth of June. The kitchen tap coughed twice and ran brown and stopped. Brother Dalton came over and listened to the pump and took off his cap and looked at the inside of it. The driller in Klamath Falls had a list eleven months long, he said. The county had a tank with their name on it, a five-hundred-gallon poly tank that a truck would come and fill, and it would be delivered when it was delivered.

The ward fed the elders by a calendar that hung on the Relief Society board, a square for every night, and whoever signed the square brought dinner. That Thursday the square said Ruiz. Sister Ruiz worked swing shift at the door plant in Klamath and could not sit down with anybody, so she left two foil plates of enchiladas in a cooler on the step, with a note. They carried them to the meetinghouse to eat at the kitchen counter there, where it was cool. The custodian had taped a sheet of paper over the kitchen sink. Bldg well low. Water OFF except Sundays.

They stood there with the foil plates, and neither of them had had a drink since breakfast.

Havliks’, Brother Dalton said on the phone. A mile past me, the white house with the windbreak. They’ve got an ag well goes down six hundred feet. Old Frank Havlik never turned anybody away from that pump in his life. He passed in February. I guess the girl’s still there.

II. Water

The Havlik place · June and July

Malin was settled in the fall of 1909 by sixty-five Bohemian families who came out from the Midwest to farm the lake bottom. They named it for a town at home, and they found horseradish growing wild there the size of a man’s arm and took it for a sign. A Havlik had been one of the sixty-five. The house was his, a tall plain farmhouse with a porch on two sides and a row of Lombardy poplars the wind had been working on for a hundred years.

The woman who came around the corner of it, wiping her hands on her jeans, was perhaps twenty-seven. She had her hair tied up in a bandanna and dust to the elbows. She looked at the white shirts and the black name tags and the four blue jugs.

You’re the ones on Dalton’s place, she said. I heard his well went. Pump house is around the side. The hydrant sticks, you have to lean on it.

Her name was Tess. That was the first day, and all of it, except that when they had the jugs in the trunk she walked out to the car and said, When you need water again, come here. Don’t go anyplace else. I mean it. That well’s the one thing on this place that works.

They needed water every second day. Four jugs, twenty gallons, was drinking and dishes and a spit bath, with enough over to flush once at night.

The hydrant was at the pump house, fifty yards off across the yard, and it ran slow. Elder Pappas took the hydrant. He was better at leaning on it. That left Elder Bowman on the porch steps, in plain sight, holding the caps, and it left Tess Havlik, who seemed always to have just come in from something.

The second time she brought out two glasses of water with ice, and stood while he drank his. The third time it was lemonade. The week after, there was a plate of koláče on the porch rail, apricot and poppy seed, from a card in her great-grandmother’s handwriting that she had to hold up to the window to read. Then one evening when they drove in there was a card table on the porch with an oilcloth on it and two kitchen chairs, and she fed them. Chicken in paprika gravy, with dumplings sliced off a loaf with a thread, and a dish of fresh horseradish she had grated out by the shop with the wind behind her, which brought the tears straight down Elder Pappas’s face.

We’re not supposed to go inside, Elder Bowman had told her, the second day, red to the collar. Without another man there. It’s a rule.

So don’t come inside, she said. And had moved the table out.

She did not eat with them. She brought a third chair and set it at the corner of the table, near his, and sat with her boots up on the rail and talked, while Elder Pappas went off to see about the jugs.

She had been in Portland six years, doing something with spreadsheets for a company that sold running shoes. She came home in 2020 when her father had his stroke, to run the place for a few months, and it had been two years. One pivot of alfalfa still green off the deep well. Everything else fallow. Her mother was in Arizona with a man named Gary. Her brother was in Boise and called on Sundays.

You want to know the truth? she said. It was past eight and the light was coming in level under the porch roof, through the poplars. It is so lonesome in this house. You two are the only people who’ve driven up that road on purpose since the funeral. I don’t count UPS. Some days I talk to the pump.

He laughed. She looked at him when he laughed, a moment longer than a person needs to.

You grew up on a place like this.

Yes ma’am. Potatoes.

I can tell. You looked at that wheel line when you drove in like it owed you money.

It did. The far wheel line had a flat mover and two blown gaskets, and he could have had it rolling in an afternoon. He knew how to do every single thing that place needed. He lay on his back in the single-wide that night, on top of the sheet, with the box fan going, and fixed the wheel line in his head. Then he reset the pivot nozzles. Then he was standing in the pump house door in a pair of work gloves and she was coming across the yard with two glasses. He turned over. He fixed the wheel line again.

Elder Pappas, in the other bed, said into the dark, We should be bringing a member up there with us.

We’re on the porch. You’re fifty yards off.

I know where I am, said Elder Pappas.

III. Yellow

Malin · the end of July

By the last week of July Elder Bowman was not eating what the calendar brought. He pushed Sister Dalton’s funeral potatoes around the plate and said they were good. He was awake at two and again at four. In the mornings he sat through his hour of study with the book open at the same page of Mosiah and his eyes on the wall above it, and on the days that were not water days he was short with people, and on the days that were he shaved twice.

He had stopped fixing the wheel line. He had moved on to the conversation with his father, and the one with his brother. He had worked out that a man could be released honorably for a number of reasons, and that fifteen months was not so long to give back.

Elder Okafor, the district leader, came down from Klamath Falls on the twenty-eighth to work a day’s exchange. He was from Houston and missed very little. He rode with Bowman through three appointments and a flat tire, and at four in the afternoon, parked outside the Malin post office, he turned around in the seat.

Bowman. What is the matter with you? You’ve gone yellow. I mean it, look in the mirror, you’re the color of a legal pad.

Elder Bowman looked at the dash for a while.

I’m not happy out here anymore, he said. I think I want to go home.

Okafor did not argue. He called Elder Pappas, who said yeah three times in a tired voice, and then he called the zone leaders, and the zone leaders called Eugene. In the morning the four of them were on Highway 97 before it was light, with the lake on the left gone to mudflat and Bowman in the back seat, and over Willamette Pass and down through the big firs to the valley, three and a half hours, hardly a word.

IV. The Mission Home

Eugene · the next morning

President Taufa had taught wood and metal shop at a high school in West Valley City, Utah, for thirty-one years before he was called to Oregon. He was a broad grey man of sixty-two who was missing the first joint of his left ring finger, which he said was the only thing a table saw ever taught him that he had not already known. He had been born in Nukuʻalofa and brought to Salt Lake at nine. The elders said of him that he could sit through a whole interview and say eleven words, and you would walk out having confessed things you had not known you had done.

He had the other three wait in the front room. Sister Taufa gave them orange juice. The door of the office was left open, as it always was.

Elder Bowman. They tell me you want to go home. Is that true?

Yes, President.

The president nodded slowly.

Your brother served here. People in Klamath say Bowman and it means something. You came out in the same church, with the same book, under the same call. You could have had them saying, the little one, he was the best we ever had. And you are going to let them say he went home in his tenth month, trunky. That is a serious thing you are doing to yourself. I want you to hear me say so. He let it sit. Now. Why?

There’s a woman. Where we get our water. Bowman’s ears were burning. Nothing happened, President. We never went in the house. She just, she said some things. She talks to me.

I didn’t ask you if something happened.

No sir.

It isn’t strange, the president said. That she talks. Or that you listen. He had his scriptures on the desk, the old triple with the cover gone soft, and he put his hand on them and did not open them. I am not going to read you Alma thirty-nine. You know what’s in it. You haven’t gone anywhere and I don’t know that woman. I am going to tell you a story my father told me before every stake dance from the time I was fourteen. He was a deckhand on the copra boats when he was young. He had it from a ship’s cook who came from Calcutta. That is all I know about where it’s from.

He raised his voice a little, toward the front room. You elders may as well hear it too.

V. The Archer

As the cook told it

There was a young man once, he said, in a country with tigers in it, who was called the Little Archer. He had gone to the far side of that country to learn from the best teacher there was, and he learned so well that the teacher gave him his own daughter to marry. So he took his wife and his bow and started home.

At the mouth of a forest fifty bandits came out at them. The Little Archer had fifty arrows. He shot fifty times and fifty bandits lay down in the road. The fifty-first was their chief, and the quiver was empty. The archer threw down his bow and took hold of the chief with his hands and put him on his back in the dust and knelt on him, and called over his shoulder, Wife, bring me my sword.

She came with it. And she looked down at the bandit lying there, with his hair in his eyes, and he looked up at her. It took as long as it takes to say it. She put the hilt of the sword into the bandit’s hand.

The best shot in that whole country, said President Taufa. My father would stop there and look at us. Dead of one look. It wasn’t even his.

The bandit took the woman and went on. But as he walked he thought, She did that to a husband she had known for years, for me, whom she has known an hour. When she sees the next man, what will she do to me? They came to a river. Give me your gold, he said, your bracelets and the rest. I’ll carry it over first so it won’t be lost, and come back for you. She gave it to him. He waded across and went up the far bank and kept walking.

She called after him. You have everything I own. Come back. Come back for me now.

He called from the trees: You traded the one you knew for one you didn’t. You traded the sure thing for the thing that wasn’t. You would trade me next. I am going a long way from here.

So she sat down on the near bank in a thicket, with nothing. And while she sat there a jackal came along the shore with a piece of meat in his mouth. A fish jumped in the shallows. The jackal dropped the meat and went for the fish, and the fish went under, and a bird came down and took the meat and flew off with it. The jackal stood there in the water looking first one way and then the other.

The woman laughed out loud.

Who is that laughing in the bushes? said the jackal. I don’t hear any drum. I don’t see any dancing. This is a strange place to be so cheerful.

You fool, she said. You had meat and you wanted fish, and now look at you.

It is easy to see the other fellow’s mistake, said the jackal. You had a husband and you wanted a lover. Where are they? I think you will be sitting here a while.

She stopped laughing. You are right, jackal. I will go home, as far as it is, and find a husband, and do whatever he says.

A person who walks off with the clay plate, said the jackal, will walk off with the brass one. You’ll do it again. And he went up the bank.

Nobody in the front room said anything. Then Elder Pappas said, mostly to himself, My great-grandpa had one like that. He was on a section gang at Wishram. It was a fox, though, and two otters, and a salmon. The fox got the middle.

It gets around, said the president.

So she’s the wife, Elder Bowman said. He was angry and did not know at whom. That’s what you’re telling me. Tess is the wife with the sword.

The cook always ended it the same way, my father said. He would point. The archer, that was you. The woman, that was her. And the jackal that shamed her, that was me. My father ended it that way too. I told it that way for thirty years. President Taufa turned the stump of his finger over and looked at it. Then one night my wife asked me which one in that story has something good in his mouth and opens it for a thing that jumped. I have told it more carefully since. I don’t know that young woman, Elder. I know you. You have been walking up the shore for nine months carrying something. Tell me what you have been looking at.

The porch, Bowman said, after a while. About eight o’clock.

For how long?

All day. All night.

That’s how it is done. He opened the book now, and did not have to look for the place. It is never a chain at the start. Nobody would stand still for a chain. It is a glass of water. Then a plate. Then a chair.

yea, and he leadeth them by the neck with a flaxen cord, until he bindeth them with his strong cords forever.

— 2 Nephi 26:22

Flax is a soft thing. You could break one thread of it with your little finger. So you don’t. You think about her, and that is a thread. You think about her again, that is two. You shave twice. You go over it in the night and make it a little prettier each time, and every time you go over it you have laid on one more, and by August it would hold a bull. She did not tie that on you. She doesn’t know it’s there. You plaited it yourself, lying on your back.

Bowman said nothing.

I taught shop. A boy brings me a board, he loves that board, it’s got a figure in it like a flame. I say, turn it over. Look at the end grain. Look at the knot that’s going to fall out, and the pitch pocket, and where it’s cupped. I’m not telling him it is a bad board. I’m telling him he hasn’t looked at it yet. He has looked at one face of it in good light. He closed the book on his finger. You have been looking at one hour of that place, in the shade, with a plate in front of you. When the thought comes, don’t feed it and don’t wrestle it. Let it go quiet. Then go and look at the rest. Look at every part of it that is not eight o’clock on the porch, and keep looking until it is as plain to you as a board with the bark on. A man who will do that, and likes the quiet better than the picture, can take the whole rope off over his head.

He turned back toward the front of the book.

Awake, my sons; put on the armor of righteousness. Shake off the chains with which ye are bound, and come forth out of obscurity, and arise from the dust.

— 2 Nephi 1:23

I could transfer you tonight, the president said. Coos Bay. You would carry that porch to the ocean and polish it for fifteen months. No. Go back and get your water.

VI. The Hydrant

Malin · August

They were back by dark. The next water day was a Saturday.

I’ll take the hydrant, Elder Bowman said in the car, and Elder Pappas looked at him sideways and handed over the jugs.

It did stick. He leaned on it. From the pump house door a man could see the whole place, and for the first time since June he looked at it.

He saw the wheel line, which he knew. He saw that the green pivot was the only green thing to the horizon, and that it was green on a power bill. The meter on the pole was spinning like a coin. He saw a real estate sign lying face down in the weeds by the gate, with the post already on it, not yet stood up. Through the front room window there was a hospital bed, stripped, with the rails still raised, and a walker folded against it. There was a pair of a big man’s irrigating boots on the porch by the door, set side by side, with six months of dust in them. He had walked past those boots eleven times.

And he saw her come around the house with a feed bucket, when she did not know anybody was looking. She was not coming across the yard with two glasses. She was a tired woman of twenty-seven, with her mouth set and one hand in the small of her back, who had buried her father in February and had been keeping two hundred and forty acres alive by herself in a drought ever since, and had cut her own hair. Her hands were split at the knuckles from the alkali. She stopped by the boots and stood there a second and went on.

None of it was ugly. It was only all there. He looked for the woman he had been lying awake with, the one in the level light, and understood that he had made most of her himself, out of a wheel line and a porch and his own homesickness for a farm, and laid her over a real person the way you lay a decal on a window. He was ashamed. The feeling under the shame was better than he expected. He felt as if he had set down a pack.

The fourth jug ran over on his shoes.

She had the card table out. She fed them, and sat at the corner, and he was easy with her as he had not been once all summer, and asked about the sign.

Yeah, she said. I list it Monday. There’s a dairy out of Tulare wants the water right more than the dirt. She turned her glass around on the oilcloth. I go back to Portland in September. They held my job, can you believe that? I should have told you guys. I think I just liked having somebody on the porch. Dad sat right where you’re sitting.

Yes ma’am, said Elder Bowman.

He thought of it later, driving back with twenty gallons sloshing in the trunk. If he had gone home to Sugar City in August and driven back down in September in his father’s pickup, with fifteen months of his word thrown in the ditch behind him, he would have come up the Malin road to a locked house and a dairy’s sign. He saw himself standing in the yard, looking first one way and then the other. He laughed out loud. Elder Pappas asked what, and he said, Meat and fish, and Elder Pappas, who had been in the front room, did not need the rest.

•••

The county tank came the second week of August, on a flatbed, and was set on cinder blocks beside the single-wide, and a truck filled it Tuesdays. They did not need the Havlik well after that.

They went up once more anyway. It was a Saturday in September, with Brother Dalton and his two boys and the elders quorum president and a stock trailer, eight men in all, and Sister Ruiz on her day off with a cooler. They carried the hospital bed out through the front door and loaded forty years of Frank Havlik’s shop. Elder Bowman fixed the wheel line, since he was there. It took him an hour and ten minutes. Tess shook hands with every one of them at the gate. She gave Elder Pappas the horseradish root, wrapped in a wet towel, to plant at his mother’s, and she gave Elder Bowman the card with the koláče on it in her great-grandmother’s hand, and told him to give it to whoever he married, and he said he would.

He served out his two years. In Klamath they say Bowman now and mean either one. The little one went on to Coos Bay after all, and Roseburg, and finished in Florence with a new elder from Tonga whom he trained to lean on things.

The morning the transfer call came, he and Elder Pappas rinsed out the four blue jugs for the last time and turned them upside down on the fence posts along Brother Dalton’s lane to dry. They were still there when the car pulled out. From the road they looked like something that had been planted. The sun was coming up through them, and there was nothing in them at all.

Background

The verses

If a man is tossed about by doubts, full of strong passions, and yearning only for what is delightful, his thirst will grow more and more, and he will indeed make his fetters strong.Dhammapada 349, tr. F. Max Müller (1881)
If a man delights in quieting doubts, and, always reflecting, dwells on what is not delightful (the impurity of the body, &c.), he certainly will remove, nay, he will cut the fetter of Mara.Dhammapada 350, tr. F. Max Müller (1881)

In the commentary a young monk takes his ticket-meal to the assembly hall, finds no water there, and goes to a house for some. The young woman of the house tells him to come to her and nowhere else when he needs water. In time she gives him gruel as well, then a seat and a meal, and sits near him and says how lonely the house is. He grows discontented with the monk’s life and so sallow that visiting monks ask what is wrong; his teachers bring him to the Buddha, who rebukes him and then tells a story of the past. A brilliant young archer, married to his teacher’s daughter, kills fifty bandits with fifty arrows and pins their chief, but his wife, taken with the bandit at a glance, hands the bandit the sword. The bandit, reasoning that she would betray him as easily, strips her of her ornaments at a river and abandons her; the god Sakka, as a jackal who drops his meat to snatch at a fish and loses both, shames her. The Buddha identifies the monk as the archer, the woman as the wife and himself as the jackal, and speaks the two verses. Read the full commentary story; the tale within it is a version of the Jātaka of the Little Archer (no. 374). The old story lays the whole blame on the woman. The retelling keeps its events and its closing identification, and lets the teller have second thoughts.

The Klamath Basin, 2022

The Klamath Project, begun in 1906, drained lakes and marshes on the Oregon–California line and irrigates some 200,000 acres from Upper Klamath Lake. Malin was settled on former lake bottom in September 1909 by sixty-five Bohemian families, who named it for a town near Kutná Hora; it is still known for horseradish. In the drought of 2021 the Bureau of Reclamation, bound to protect endangered sucker fish in the lake and salmon downstream, kept the project’s main canal closed for the season, and the 2022 allocation was again a small fraction of normal. Farms that had deep wells pumped them, the water table fell, and shallow household wells failed across the county: 117 were formally reported dry by late July 2021, with officials suspecting more than 300, and reports kept coming through the winter. Families hauled water in five-gallon jugs. The state paid for storage tanks and trucked water for nearly 300 homes, and well drillers had waiting lists of up to a year.

Klamath Falls has had a Latter-day Saint congregation since 1930 and a stake since 1953; the area belongs to the Oregon Eugene Mission. Missionaries, most of them eighteen to twenty, serve in pairs for eighteen months or two years under a mission president, are fed by local members, and do not enter a home where a lone woman is present without another adult. “Trunky” is their own word for a missionary whose mind has gone home ahead of him. The chapter the president declines to read, Alma 39, is a father’s rebuke to a son who left his mission to follow a woman named Isabel. The people in this story, the ward and the Havlik farm are invented.
