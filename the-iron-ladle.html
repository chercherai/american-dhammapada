Walking the American WayCommentary 5.5 · Udāyī

The Iron Ladle

I cannot teach it to my son; nor can he learn it from me.
— Chuang Tzŭ, ch. XIII, tr. Herbert A. Giles (1889)

Wŏ shì zhème tīngshuō de, at the back door on 104th Street.

I. The Photograph

Broadway at 104th Street, Manhattan · autumn 1974

The Xiang Garden had nineteen tables, a red lacquer screen to hide the kitchen door, and a porcelain Laughing Buddha beside the register with his belly rubbed pale by two years of customers on their way out. Before that it had been a luncheonette, and before that a Cuban-Chinese place that sold fried plantains with the pork fried rice, and the old counter stools were still bolted to the floor along one wall where nobody had been able to get them up.

Zeng Shifu did the cooking. He was sixty-six. He had been a scullery boy in a restaurant in Changsha that burned with the rest of the city in 1938, and a cook for an infantry division all through the war, and he had crossed to Taiwan in 1949 with the division and its stoves, and for twenty years he had cooked at a restaurant off Zhongshan North Road in Taipei where the generals ate. In 1971 a cousin brought him over. He wore a sleeveless undershirt and a paper hat and cloth slippers, and he did not come out of the kitchen, ever, for anybody.

Yao Delu came out of the kitchen for everybody. He was from the same county as Zeng and had been at his elbow since 1940, when the division took him on to wash pots, and when Zeng crossed the strait Yao crossed with him, and when Zeng came to New York Yao came the next year on the same cousin’s papers. He owned a tenth of the restaurant. He stood at the door in a white cook’s jacket with a high collar, ironed, and seated people, and told them about Hunan, and recommended the smoked pork with leeks and the Dong’an chicken and, to anybody who seemed brave, the whole fish with chopped chilies. He told them Hunan food was not like the Cantonese food they knew from Chinatown. It was hot, and sour, and smoked, and honest. Chairman Mao was from Hunan, he told them, and he said it as though Mao had been a nephew who had disappointed everybody.

Since President Nixon had gone to Peking, Americans wanted to eat what was eaten there, and anything from China was in the newspapers. In the spring a critic came three times without saying who she was and wrote two columns about the Xiang Garden, and a photographer came to take a picture of the chef. Zeng would not come out of the kitchen. He would not come out for Chiang Kai-shek and he did not come out for a newspaper. So Yao went back and stood beside the big burner with the iron ladle in his hand, in his white jacket, smiling, and that was the photograph. It hung in the front window now in a dime-store frame, a little yellowed by the sun: Master of Hunan cooking at the Xiang Garden.

On the slow nights, after Zeng had gone home to his room on Amsterdam Avenue, Yao went back to the kitchen and sat in Zeng’s chair.

It was a bamboo armchair, the only one in the kitchen, and it stood by the back door where the air came in from the alley. Zeng sat in it between orders in the afternoon and smoked Pall Malls and said nothing. Nobody else sat in it. It was not a rule; nobody had ever said anything; the dishwashers and the young cooks just didn’t. But after ten o’clock Yao sat in it, with his tea in a jam jar, and his white jacket unbuttoned at the throat, and told the dishwashers about the war.

II. The Chair

The kitchen · a Monday night in November

The three young cooks came in the back way, as cooks do, a little after ten.

They worked at a new Szechuan place on Second Avenue in the Fifties, and Monday was their night off, and they had come all the way uptown on the subway to see the master of Hunan cooking. Kao was from Taichung and had cooked in Taipei. Wong was from Hong Kong and spoke Cantonese and English and not much Mandarin. Lin was nineteen and the youngest and had been in New York four months, and he had cut the photograph out of the newspaper and kept it in his wallet.

Through the screen door they saw the old man in the white jacket in the bamboo chair by the back door, and Lin knew him from the picture. They came in and bowed a little, and Kao said their names and where they worked, and said they had come to learn.

Yao was delighted. He sent the dishwasher for more tea. He asked them where their families were from, and told them about the burning of Changsha, which he had not seen, and about the generals in Taipei, and which of them had liked his food very salty.

Kao asked about the Dong’an chicken. How did Shifu balance the five flavors in it? It was the sourness that people talked about.

Balance, said Yao. Yes. That is the whole secret of Hunan cooking. You have to feel it.

Which vinegar did Shifu use? Rice vinegar or black?

Yao said black vinegar, from Zhenjiang, the best. Kao looked at Wong. Lin, who had eaten the Dong’an chicken at the front table an hour before and could still taste it, said nothing.

Wong asked in English how long the pork was smoked, and over what. Tea leaves? Rice husks? Camphor wood, the way they did it in Sichuan?

Long time, said Yao in English. Very long time. Patience. He laughed and slapped his knee.

Kao asked about huohou, the timing of the fire, for the chicken: how hot the wok, how long, when the chilies went in, when the ginger. He asked about the knife work, how fine the ginger was cut. He asked whether Shifu blanched the chicken first or poached it whole. Yao answered all of it. He answered it the way the photograph answered it, smiling and holding the ladle, and none of it was anything.

At last Lin asked him, very politely, to name the five flavors.

Yao said sour, and sweet, and salty, and hot. He stopped. He drank some tea. And fragrant, he said.

Wong laughed out loud. Kao got up. Yao laughed too, because they were laughing, and offered them more tea, and they said they had to catch the train.

Out in the alley among the garbage cans Kao lit a cigarette and swore. Thirty years, he said. Thirty years at the master’s elbow, in the master’s chair, in the master’s picture in the newspaper, and he can’t name the five flavors. Bitter. He forgot bitter.

Maybe he’s not the master, said Lin.

Then what is he?

Lin didn’t know. He had a feeling, the way you have a feeling about a sauce before you taste it, that they had come in the wrong door.

III. The Ladle

The same kitchen · Tuesday, before the lunch rush

Kao would not go back. Wong and Lin went back the next morning at eleven, before their own shift, and came in by the alley again, and this time the bamboo chair was empty, and an old man in an undershirt and a paper hat was standing at the big burner with his back to them, lighting it with a twist of newspaper.

It went up with a roar, a ring of blue jets as wide as a dinner plate, and he set the wok on it, and while it heated he turned around and looked at them.

Lin told him. He told it straight, the way it had happened, and he felt bad telling it, because the old man in the white jacket had given them tea. Wong put in a word here and there in English. They had come to learn from the master of Hunan cooking. The man in the picture could not tell them which vinegar. He could not tell them how the pork was smoked. He could not name the five flavors. He had forgotten bitter. How could a man live thirty years beside the master and not know that?

Zeng listened to the end. Then he laughed, a short laugh that turned into a cough, and turned back to the wok, and poured in oil, and swirled it, and did not say anything for a while. Out front somebody was vacuuming. A delivery boy came in with a crate of scallions and went out.

When he spoke it was in Mandarin, with a Hunan accent so thick that Wong understood almost none of it, and Lin, standing at his shoulder, put it into English as he went. It was an old story; Lin had read it in school in Taiwan and never thought of it since. A duke was reading in his hall, and a wheelwright working below put down his tools and asked what the duke was reading. The words of the sages, said the duke. And are the sages alive? No, they are dead. Then what you are reading is the dregs. The duke was angry and told him to explain himself or die. And the wheelwright said:

In making a wheel, if you work too slowly, you can’t make it firm; if you work too fast, the spokes won’t fit in. You must go neither too slowly nor too fast. There must be co-ordination of mind and hand. Words cannot explain what it is, but there is some mysterious art herein. I cannot teach it to my son; nor can he learn it from me.

— Chuang Tzŭ, ch. XIII, tr. Herbert A. Giles (1889)

Zeng threw a handful of ginger into the oil, and it hissed. Then he reached up without looking and took the iron ladle down from its hook over the stove. It was a cook’s ladle, a shallow bowl on a long handle, black, worn bright along one side of the rim where it had scraped ten thousand woks.

I brought this from Taipei, he said. Twenty-three years. It has been in every pot. It has been in every Dong’an chicken I ever made. He held it out toward the two young men, handle first, as if they might want to interview it. Ask it which vinegar.

Wong understood that much without Lin.

Zeng put the chicken in, and the chilies, and the Sichuan pepper, and worked the ladle under it and over it, and the fire came up around the wok, and the kitchen filled with a smell that went straight to the back of the throat and made both of them cough. He added the vinegar. Rice vinegar, Lin saw, pale. He tossed it twice and then dipped the ladle in and lifted out a little of the sauce, and held the ladle out again, this time bowl first.

Taste, he said.

Lin tasted. It was sour first and then hot and then something under both of them that he had no word for, and then the hot again, and his eyes ran. He stood there with his mouth open, breathing out through it. He had been going to ask a question, he could not remember what. Wong took the ladle out of his hand and tasted, and shut his eyes.

Zeng took the ladle back and wiped it on his apron and hung it on its hook.

Don’t blame Lao Yao, he said. He washed my pots for nine years in the war. Nobody ever washed pots better. Now go to work, you’ll be late.

•••

Lin worked at the Xiang Garden from the next spring until Zeng died in 1981, and afterward he had restaurants of his own, in Flushing and later in New Jersey, and he never wrote anything down for anybody. When young cooks came in the back door to ask him things, he gave them a spoon.

But that Tuesday, going out through the dining room on the way to the train, he and Wong passed Yao at the front, in his white jacket, buttoned to the throat and freshly ironed, dusting the Laughing Buddha by the register with a feather duster. He waved to them with it. A party of four from Columbia had come in early for lunch, and he was seating them by the window, under his own picture, and telling them that the whole secret of Hunan cooking was balance.

Background

The verse

If a fool be associated with a wise man even all his life, he will perceive the truth as little as a spoon perceives the taste of soup.Dhammapada 64, tr. F. Max Müller (1881)

In the commentary, the Elder Udāyī lives with the Buddha at Jetavana, and whenever the great elders leave the Dhamma Hall he goes in and sits in the preacher’s seat. Some visiting monks see him there, take him for a learned elder, and question him about the aggregates, the elements and the sense-spheres. He knows nothing about any of it. Scornful, they ask what such a monk is doing in the same monastery as the Buddha and report him. The Buddha answers with this verse: a fool can wait on the wise for a lifetime and learn the Dhamma no more than a spoon learns the taste of the curry. At the end of it the visiting monks themselves are freed. The next verse in the Dhammapada (65) completes the image: the intelligent man who sits with the wise for a moment knows the truth as the tongue knows the taste of soup. New York, 1974

Until the 1960s nearly all Chinese food in the United States was Cantonese, cooked by and for families from the Pearl River delta. After the Immigration and Nationality Act of 1965 opened the country to new arrivals, and especially after President Nixon’s visit to China in 1972, New York discovered Sichuan and Hunan cooking, with its chilies, sourness and smoked and cured meats. Many of the new cooks and owners came by way of Taiwan, where mainland soldiers and officials who had retreated with the Nationalists in 1949 had opened restaurants serving their home provinces’ food. The best known of them, the Hunanese chef Peng Chang-kuei, had cooked official banquets in Taipei and opened a restaurant on East 44th Street around 1973, and the dish he is credited with, General Tso’s chicken, became the most famous Hunan dish in America; when Peng later opened a restaurant in Hunan itself, the locals found it too sweet.

Taiwanese-run restaurants spread up and down Manhattan in the following years; one of the first of the Szechuan chains opened at Broadway and 97th Street in 1976, with Taiwanese students from Columbia driving its deliveries. Zeng Shifu’s first restaurant burned in the Changsha fire of November 1938, set by Nationalist officials so that the advancing Japanese would find nothing; more than 30,000 people died and about nine-tenths of the city’s buildings burned.
