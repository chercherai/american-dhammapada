White City

Making its dust my bed, its loneliest wastes
My dwelling, and its meanest things my mates
— Edwin Arnold, The Light of Asia, Book the Fourth

The way it come down the line, in the willows below the tank at Minot.

I. The Tank

Minot, North Dakota · October 1913

There was a water tank on the Great Northern a little east of the Minot yards, and one moniker had been cut into its timbers every fall for the best part of twenty years, each cut under the last: WHITE CITY, the month and the year, and on the day he left, an arrow pointing south. A man coming in off the threshing with his season's money in his shoe could read on that tank whether White City was still holding down the jungle in the willows. If the newest date had no arrow under it, he walked down to the river before he walked into town.

White City was forty-five that year and had been on the road since he was twenty-five. He shocked and pitched like anybody from July to freeze-up, on the rigs north of town. When the threshing shut down he kept the camp on the Mouse River for two weeks or three, until the river skimmed over. He hustled the wood. He kept forty-odd tin cans scoured with sand and hung bottom side up on the willow stubs so they would not fill with rain and rust: lard pails for cooking, the big square ones for boiling up clothes, tomato cans for coffee. Nobody had asked him to. But men who had come through that camp told it in other camps, as far off as the Wenatchee apples, that it was the cleanest jungle on the Great Northern, and that the stiff who kept it would tell you something if you wanted it told, after he had heard you out.

The name was from Chicago. He had been a plasterer's helper on the World's Fair, mixing staff, which is plaster and hemp, and laying it over lath to make the palaces the newspapers called the White City. In September of 1893, with the palaces long built and no work, he went one Monday into the big Art Palace on the lake front, where they were holding a congress of all the religions, because there were seats. A thin young man from Ceylon, dressed in white, read a long paper. He went by a title that somebody on the bench said meant a fellow with no house. White City remembered that. A week later he saw a paper-covered book about that man's teacher on a stall in State Street, and gave a dime for it.

The Fair shut at the end of October, and the banks had been shutting all summer. That winter he slept with other men out of work in the empty palaces. In January he stood in the crowd and watched the Peristyle burn. When the rest of it went, in July, he was shocking wheat in Kansas, and a man read it to him out of a newspaper.

He had the book yet. It was sewn into a square of kitchen oilcloth, and he did not need to open it.

That October the freeze shut the rigs down all in a week. Fifty men came into the willows in one afternoon, off a drag from the west and another down the Soo from Kenmare. They had passed the season on the rigs around Stanley and Berthold and up the Mouse River loop. They had come to see him.

II. The Season

The jungle on the Mouse River · the same afternoon

In a jungle nobody asks a man his name or where he was raised. White City asked each of them one thing, where he had been since July. Then he went on peeling potatoes into the big can and let the man say.

A Dane had pitched bundles for a rig north of Berthold, daylight to dark, at three dollars, and been docked for every hour it rained. He had slept in the straw stack, because the farmer's wife would not have harvest hands in the barn with the lanterns.

Two of them had been in Minot in August, when the Wobblies got up on boxes at the street corners and the town swore in fifty special police. They had been held in a bullpen outside the jail, and the fire company had turned the hose on them. When the bullpen was full, the rest were put in the stockyard pens beside the Great Northern track. They were offered their dinner if they would break rock for it. One of the two had broken the rock. He said so, and nobody in the willows said anything.

A brakeman out of Williston had wanted a dollar a division or hit the grit. A man with eighty cents had hit it, at twenty miles an hour, and he rolled his sleeve to show them the arm.

A stiff called Ohio Red had gone between the cars at Towner, in the dark, reaching for the ladder. The others on that train had put in a quarter apiece, and a preacher in Towner had read over him for nothing. Nobody knew what name to give the preacher. They gave him the moniker, and it is on a board there.

A boy from Indiana, seventeen, on his first season, had come off a rig near Kenmare with forty-one dollars. Two men with a pistol went through a boxcar full of harvest hands outside Burlington and took all of it, and his shoes, and put the whole car off on the grade. He had walked into Minot with his feet tied up in a grain sack.

A man from the pineries had made the best stake of his life, a hundred and twelve dollars, and had not slept three hours together since, for holding it.

Another had last year's story. Ninety dollars, one night, in a blind pig on Third Street, in a dry state. He had ninety on him again and could feel it wanting to go.

Some of them mended while they talked. A Syrian woman who walked the branch lines with a case of drawers on her back had sold them needles and thread at Stanley.

It took until dark. White City heard every one of them to the end. He asked the Dane how the straw had been to sleep in, and the Dane, surprised, said it was warm, and that you could see the whole sky.

When the mulligan was eaten and each man had washed his can in the river, he spoke. He was not a talker and it did not take long.

III. The King's Boy

By the fire · that night

You talk like men that had a thing took off you, he said. A house. A woman, some of you. I talked that way ten years.

There was a rich man's boy in India one time. A king's boy. He had three houses, one for the heat and one for the cold and one for the rains, and a wife, and a new baby. He walked out on the whole spread one night, on purpose, and went on the bum. He bummed his chuck at back doors with a bowl for the better part of fifty years, and kings came down to the road to ask him how it was done. A man wrote it up. This is the boy talking, the night he goes. He means the ground. The earth.

I choose
To tread its paths with patient, stainless feet,
Making its dust my bed, its loneliest wastes
My dwelling, and its meanest things my mates:
Clad in no prouder garb than outcasts wear,
Fed with no meats save what the charitable
Give of their will, sheltered by no more pomp
Than the dim cave lends or the jungle-bush,

— The Light of Asia, Book the Fourth

It goes on. That is the part I mean.

He went out of the dark into the white. That is all I ever got of it, and it was enough to go on. I know something about white. I mixed the White City. It was plaster and hemp on lath, with white lead blown on it out of a hose, and inside every palace was a shed. It burned in two nights. So I don't mean that kind.

The kind I mean, you have had already. You had it on top of a car coming out of Stanley at four in the morning, with frost on the running board and not a dime in your kick, and you felt all right and could not think why. You had it in that straw. You took it for a mistake. It was the thing itself, and it is what the boy went out for. Next time it comes, don't hunt around for what is wrong. Stay in it. Like it on purpose.

Third Street will have its doors open tomorrow. I am no sky pilot. I only say they have nothing in there as good as what you had on top of that car, and they want the whole stake for it.

You know the law in here. No man calls a can his own except while he is cooking in it. Take that law out of the willows with you. The stake is a can. So is your name, and your sore back, and whatever was done to you in August. Use it, scour it, turn it bottom up for the next man.

And boil up. I don't only mean your shirt.

He was quiet a while, and fed the fire.

You have met one or two that had it steady. They come into a camp and it gets quieter. They are not headed for anyplace better and they are not sore at anyplace behind. A bull can't do much with a man like that. He is as loose in the Minot stockyard as he is on the road. There is a kind of a shine on him, and you know it when you see it.

As one who stands on yonder snowy horn
Having nought o'er him but the boundless blue,
So, these sins being slain, the man is come
NIRVANA's verge unto.

— The Light of Asia, Book the Eighth

That last is the boy's word for it, White City said. I never found an American one.

Then he got up and banked the fire.

What came of it is told more than one way. It is agreed that in the morning thirty-some of the fifty walked up to the Minot post office before they went anyplace else, and bought money orders made out to themselves at General Delivery in Minneapolis or Spokane or wherever they meant to winter, paper that a stickup man cannot spend. It is agreed that the man with the hundred and twelve dollars slept that night from the time he lay down. The one who had lost ninety on Third Street lost it again. He came back down to the willows on the third day and scoured cans, and nobody said anything about that either.

The boy from Indiana went south on the Soo with White City and was seen with him the next fall, pitching bundles north of Berthold, in shoes.

The rest is what men say. They say that for years afterward you would meet a stiff now and then on the Great Northern, or out in the Wenatchee apples, who came into a camp quiet and hustled wood before he sat down, and who asked a man one thing, where he had been since July, and then let him say.

White City left the night the river skimmed over. On the tank the arrow was cut under that year's date, yellow in the gray wood. In the willows the cans hung bottom side up on the stubs, forty-odd of them, scoured and dry, and not one of them anybody's.

Background

The verses

A wise man should leave the dark state (of ordinary life), and follow the bright state (of the Bhikshu). After going from his home to a homeless state, he should in his retirement look for enjoyment where there seemed to be no enjoyment. Leaving all pleasures behind, and calling nothing his own, the wise man should purge himself from all the troubles of the mind.Dhammapada 87–88, tr. F. Max Müller (1881)
Those whose mind is well grounded in the (seven) elements of knowledge, who without clinging to anything, rejoice in freedom from attachment, whose appetites have been conquered, and who are full of light, are free (even) in this world.Dhammapada 89, tr. F. Max Müller (1881)

The commentary story is one of the slightest in the collection. Fifty monks who have spent the three months of the rains retreat in the kingdom of Kosala come to Jetavana at the end of the season to see the Buddha. He listens to the account of their season, and then gives them three verses on the life they have taken up: out of the dark state into the bright one, from home into homelessness, delight looked for in the solitude where it is hardest to find, nothing called one's own, the mind cleaned, and freedom here and not later. Many of them reach the first stages of awakening. (The Pāli title says five hundred visiting monks; the story itself says fifty.) Read the full commentary story.

The harvest stiffs, 1913

Before the combine, the wheat of the northern plains was cut, shocked and threshed by tens of thousands of migrant men who followed the harvest north by freight train from Oklahoma to the Canadian line and were paid off when the freeze stopped the threshing rigs. In the summer of 1915 the paper of the Industrial Workers of the World reported posted harvest wages in North Dakota averaging $2.50 a day, with $3 around Minot, warned that "stickups" were working that district, and told men to send their stakes ahead by postal money order. Between jobs they lived in "jungles," camps near a water tank and a stream. Nels Anderson, who had been a hobo before he was a sociologist, set down the jungle's law in The Hobo (1923): "no one can call a vessel his except at the time he uses it," and cans are to be cleaned, dried and left "turned bottom side up so that they will not fill with rainwater and rust." Jack London's The Road (1907) describes the names cut into railroad water tanks with a date and a direction: "Water-tanks are tramp directories." Minot, founded by the Great Northern in 1886 and reached by the Soo Line in 1893, was a wide-open town in a state that had been dry since statehood. In August 1913 it jailed IWW street speakers and their supporters in a bullpen and then in the Great Northern stockyard, as Prairie Public's account of the Wobblies in Minot tells it. The jungle on the river, White City and the fifty are invented.

The World's Columbian Exposition of 1893 was built largely of staff, plaster and fiber on temporary frames, sprayed white. It closed on October 30 in the middle of a national depression. Chicago's unemployed sheltered in the empty buildings that winter; a fire on January 8, 1894 destroyed the Peristyle, and another in July took most of what was left. During the Fair, the World's Parliament of Religions met downtown, and on September 18 the young Ceylonese Buddhist Anagarika Dharmapala, dressed in white, read his paper there. Anagārika is Pāli for "homeless one," the same word the verse uses. Edwin Arnold's The Light of Asia (1879), a life of the Buddha in English verse, was one of the first books to carry that story to ordinary Western readers. The lines White City says are from Books Four and Eight.
