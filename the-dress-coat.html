Walking the American WayCommentary 20.6 · Sūkarapeta

The Dress Coat

Speak not but what may benefit others or yourself; avoid trifling conversation.
— Benjamin Franklin, Autobiography

The way I had it told, on the wharves at Ragged Harbor.

I. The Stair

Gull Ledge Light, outer Penobscot Bay, Maine · September 1904

At sunrise the keeper on watch put out the lamp, and the keeper coming on went up and helped him hang the linen curtains around the lens so the sun would not get into the glass and set the lantern afire, and then the two of them came down together. That was the rule at Gull Ledge, and it had been the rule longer than anybody now on the ledge had been there.

Ora Dyer came down first that morning, and Charlie Hupper behind him. Ora was first assistant, forty-four, an island man from Ragged Harbor who had fished until his back went. Charlie was nineteen and second assistant, which meant he did what the other two told him and shoveled coal for the fog whistle. There was a window halfway down the iron stair that looked out over the west end of the ledge where the weed was, at low water. Ora stopped at it. He looked out a long minute, and Charlie, on the step above, saw the side of his face change. He smiled.

It was not a thing Ora did much. Charlie leaned to see past him and saw the ledge and the weed and the tide going out.

What is it?

Not now, said Ora. Ask me at supper, in front of Mr. Ames.

II. Supper

The same evening

Josiah Ames had been head keeper at Gull Ledge since the fall before. He was a Ragged Harbor man too, sixty years old, a widower. He talked about as much as a gull post. What he did, besides keep the light, was birds. He had a book in which he wrote down every bird he saw on the ledge, the day and the hour and the wind, and every bird that came against the lantern at night and was picked up dead on the gallery in the morning, and once a year he sent a copy of it to a man at Washington who wrote him back a letter of thanks. The Lighthouse Board's library box came out on the tender every few months with fifty books in it, and when it came Ames took out whatever had to do with birds first and the Bible last, and generally read both.

When the plates were cleared, Charlie asked.

There was a man on the west end of the ledge, Ora said, standing on the weed at low water. He had on a keeper's coat, the dress coat, with the buttons. He had a man's body and a man's hands. And he had the head of a hog. And out of the hog's mouth there came a tail, like a hog's tail only long, down to his knees, and maggots coming out the end of it and dropping off into the weed. That's what he was. I never saw such a thing as that. I smiled because I've heard there are such things, and I'd never seen one.

Charlie laughed, and stopped, because nobody else did.

Josiah Ames put down his fork.

You've got eyes, Ora, and you use them, he said. I've seen him. I saw him the morning after the big flight in May.

He told Charlie about the flight, since Charlie had not come yet then. A night of south wind and thick weather, the twenty-second of May, and the birds going north over the bay in the dark by thousands, and the light pulling them in. All night they struck the glass. In the morning Ames went out on the gallery with a basket. There were warblers, yellow and black and chestnut, thrushes, a woodcock, two hundred and some birds, and a few still alive in the corners that he warmed in his shirt and let go. He picked up every one and laid them in rows to count them for the book, and while he was doing it he looked down at the west end of the ledge.

I've seen him most low waters since, Ames said. I didn't speak of it. People on the islands will laugh at a keeper who says he sees things, and it doesn't do them any good to laugh at a thing like that. I let it alone for their sake. Now there's two of us, so I'll tell who it is. It's Lyman Sprowl. Ora knows the name.

Ora nodded. Charlie, who was from Rockland, did not know it.

III. Two Keepers

Gull Ledge · 1866–1886

Ansel Pote and Warren Crockett came onto Gull Ledge together in the spring of 1866, both of them out of the army. Ansel was keeper and Warren was his assistant, and they kept that light for twenty years. Ansel was one year older. In twenty years nobody on the islands heard either one say a hard word to the other. They lit the lamp together and put it out together. They wound the fog bell machinery in turns through every thick night, two hours on and two off, and wrote up the log in the same hand, near enough, so that the inspector once asked which of them had kept it. They had their Sundays. When the weather let them, one kept the light and the other rowed in to Ragged Harbor for meeting, and the next Sunday the other went. Both of them were bachelors. People said they were like a pair of oxen that had been yoked so long neither one knew which side he was on.

In 1884 the Board put the keepers into uniform: a double-breasted coat of navy blue with gilt buttons and a K on the collar for keeper, and a cap with a little lighthouse on the front. Ansel wore his on Sundays and when the inspector came. Warren carried it. From the first week that was how it was. When Ansel went in to meeting, Warren rowed him to the island and carried the dress coat down to the boat folded over his arm, and Ansel's dinner pail in the other hand, and held them on the wharf while Ansel climbed up. He would have carried Ansel up the ladder if Ansel had let him. Ansel did not ask it and did not stop it, and it was like that for two years.

IV. The Third Keeper

Gull Ledge · 1886

In the spring of 1886 the Board put a steam fog whistle on Gull Ledge in place of the bell, and a steam whistle wants a man to tend the boiler, so a second assistant was appointed. His name was Lyman Sprowl. He came out on the tender in May with a sea chest and a hymnbook.

He was thirty-five and he could preach. He had been a Methodist exhorter in Waldo County, not ordained, and the first Sunday on the ledge, with all three of them there because of the weather, Ansel asked him to read them a chapter and say something on it. He read them the prodigal son and talked about it for half an hour, and when he was done the two old men sat a while without getting up. They told each other afterward that they had got a minister out of the Board, and it was more than they had expected out of the Board.

That summer they took him in to the islands. Ragged Harbor had a meetinghouse and no minister, and Heron Island had no meetinghouse at all, and the Greens had nothing but a schoolhouse. Wherever the two keepers went they were known, and wherever they took Lyman the people asked him to speak, and after he spoke they asked him back. In July and August the three of them made the round of all the harbors where the keepers had friends, one keeper staying on the light, a Saturday night prayer meeting and a Sunday in each, and Lyman preaching. People cried. Children were named for him.

Lyman had his own thoughts. These two are old, he thought, and set in their ways, and they have had this light twenty years. He had a cousin who knew the new collector at Belfast, and the collector knew the congressman, and there was a new administration in Washington that was letting a good many old keepers go. If both of them went, he thought, there would be nobody to give the light to but the man who was already on it.

So one evening at the change of the watch he came to Ansel and said, Mr. Pote, there is something I ought to say to you.

Say it.

Lyman looked at the floor a while. It's a hard thing, he said. I'd better not. And he went down the stair, and went to Warren in the boiler house and did the same. The next evening he did it again to both of them. By the third evening the two old men were sick with it. Then Lyman came to Ansel in the watch room and said, Mr. Pote, I don't like to say it to your face. Has Mr. Crockett got anything to do with you?

What are you talking about? We're like two brothers. Twenty years. Whatever comes on this ledge for one of us is the other's. I never saw a wrong thing in him.

Is that so, said Lyman. Well, I'm sorry, then. Because from the first week I came here he has been telling me to look out for you. He says you'll seem decent and quiet, and I'm not to trust you. He's said it more than once.

Ansel heard that, and something in him went, the way a crock goes when you hit it with a stick. Lyman went down to the boiler house and said the same to Warren. Warren went the same way.

In twenty years neither of them had gone up the tower alone to light the lamp. The next evening Warren went up alone, ahead of time, and lit it, and came down, and Ansel passed him on the stair. On Sunday, Warren was to row Ansel in to meeting. He stood on the landing with his hands empty and thought, I'll not carry his coat. Then he thought, I have carried it two years, and it's my place to carry it, whatever he is. And he softened, and went up to the dwelling and said, Give me the coat, Ansel.

Ansel snapped his fingers in his face.

Get away from me. You're not fit to carry my coat.

That's all right, said Warren. I'd already thought I wouldn't.

Did you think I cared anything for this light?

Did you think I did? It's your light. Keep it.

They both wrote to the inspector that week. Ledge stations have two landings, one on the east end for a westerly blow and one on the west end for an easterly, and when the tender came in September Ansel went off by the east landing with his sea chest and Warren went off by the west, each in his own boat, and they did not look at each other. Lyman stood on the slip.

Don't do this, he said.

You stay, Lyman, they said.

So Lyman stayed.

V. The Islands

Ragged Harbor · the fall of 1886

The next Saturday Lyman came in to Ragged Harbor alone, and people asked him where the keepers were.

Don't ask me, Lyman said. They had a falling out and both of them went off the ledge. I did everything I could to keep them. I couldn't.

Some people said nothing. Others said, We have known those two twenty years and never saw so much as a cross look between them. If they were scared off that ledge, it was this one that scared them. They said it in their kitchens and not to his face, and the fall after, the Board made Lyman Sprowl keeper of Gull Ledge Light.

Ansel went east, Down East, to a light at the mouth of the Machias. Warren went west, all the way, and kept lights in Oregon. Neither one of them married. Neither one of them was any good afterward, the way they told it later. They could not keep a clean log. They lost their place in a chapter. Ansel would sit up in the watch room thinking, how could he say that of me, the day he saw that man come on the ledge. Three thousand miles away Warren sat up thinking, how could Ansel say that of me. Seventeen years went by like that.

VI. Tillamook

Tillamook Rock Light, Oregon · spring 1903

Ansel's sister's girl had married a man in the mills at Port Gamble on Puget Sound, where the East Machias people went, and when she was widowed she wrote for him. He got into the district out there. In March of 1903 he was ordered to Tillamook Rock.

Tillamook Rock is a lump of black basalt a mile off Tillamook Head, with the light and the keepers' dwelling built all in one on top of it, and the sea goes over it in winter. There is no harbor and no beach. A man goes on and off it in a breeches buoy, swung up from the tender on the derrick line with his legs hanging, and his chest comes after him. Ansel was sixty-two. He came up out of the sea in the canvas breeches with his eyes shut, and the head keeper unbuckled him and took him in and showed him where he would sleep. There was one room for the assistants. There was a man sitting on the other bunk with his boots off.

Ansel sat down on the empty bunk and could not stop crying.

Warren had known him the moment he came through the door. He sat with his hands on his knees and his eyes full and thought, shall I say anything or shall I not. Then he thought, that is no way to think. He got up in his stocking feet and went and stood in front of Ansel.

Ansel. All those years I carried your coat, did you ever once know me to do a wrong thing? In what I did, or what I said, or what I thought?

No. Never.

Then why did you tell Lyman Sprowl to look out for me?

I never said any such thing. He told me you said it about me.

I never said it.

They looked at each other. Then they both understood it at once, the way you understand all at once that the fog is coming in. He did it on purpose. He did it to get the light. And each of them told the other what he had thought for seventeen years, and asked his pardon, and got it. The head keeper said afterward that he left them alone with the door shut and did not go in for an hour, and that when he did they were sitting side by side on the one bunk and had not lit the lamp.

They kept Tillamook together that summer. In August they gave their notice.

Let's go back, said Ansel, and put him off our ledge.

VII. The Slip

Gull Ledge · October 1903

They came across the continent on the cars, and from Rockland out on the mail boat, and from Ragged Harbor a man rowed them to the ledge. Lyman saw the boat coming from the gallery. He was fifty-two. He had kept Gull Ledge seventeen years and he had kept it well, and he still preached around the islands, and the islands still went to hear him, some of them.

He came down to the west slip to meet the boat. He knew them both before they were close. He waded out to his knees and took hold of the bow, and when they stood up he reached to take Ansel's bag and Warren's, the way a younger keeper does.

Ansel snapped his fingers in his face. Then Warren did.

Don't you touch it, said Ansel. You are not fit to keep this light.

That was all either of them said. It was not much. Lyman Sprowl had preached in every meetinghouse on the bay for seventeen years and been talked about by the whole coast and never once minded it, and he could not stand that one sentence. He let go of the boat. He walked up the slip and across the ledge and down the east landing, and he took the station dory and rowed out of the lee of the ledge into a southeast chop with the afternoon coming on, toward nowhere at all that anybody could make out. The assistant on duty hailed him from the gallery. He did not turn his head.

They found the dory on the Greens two days later, bottom up. They found him at low water on the west end of the ledge, in the weed, in his dress coat.

Ansel and Warren stayed the winter on the ledge until a new keeper came, and then they went in to Ragged Harbor and took a house together over the wharf, and they died in it, Ansel in 1907 and Warren the winter after.

VIII. The Library Box

Gull Ledge · September 1904

The fog whistle had started while Ames was telling it. It went every forty seconds, and the dishes in the rack answered it.

One sentence, said Charlie. He did all that and they said one sentence to him.

He did all that with sentences, said Ames.

He got up and went to the library box under the window, which had come out on the tender in July, and took out a book he had been reading in the afternoons. It was Franklin's life, written by himself. He had marked a page with a primary feather from a yellow-shafted woodpecker. In it Franklin set down the virtues he meant to practice, one to a page, and kept a little book in which he made a black spot against each one every day he failed it. Ames read three of them out under the lamp.

2. Silence. Speak not but what may benefit others or yourself; avoid trifling conversation.

7. Sincerity. Use no hurtful deceit; think innocently and justly; and, if you speak, speak accordingly.

8. Justice. Wrong none by doing injuries, or omitting the benefits that are your duty.

— Benjamin Franklin, Autobiography

The mouth, and what's back of it, and the hands, Ames said. Keep them clean and you can stand to be looked at. He put the feather back in at the page. That's all there is to it. It's a short list.

He took the watch at midnight. Ora relieved him at four. At sunrise the two of them went up and put out the lamp and hung the linen around the lens, and came down together, and at the window on the stair Ora stopped again. The tide was out. Charlie did not look; he had decided not to. Ora went on down.

Next Sunday Charlie was to row Ora in to Ragged Harbor for meeting. Ora came down to the boat in his good coat with nothing in his hands. Charlie was standing on the slip holding Ora's dinner pail, and he did not know himself why he had picked it up.

Background

The verse

Watching his speech, well restrained in mind, let a man never commit any wrong with his body! Let a man but keep these three roads of action clear, and he will achieve the way which is taught by the wise.Dhammapada 281, tr. F. Max Müller (1881)

In the commentary, the elder Mahā Moggallāna, coming down from Vulture’s Peak with the elder Lakkhaṇa, smiles, and explains only before the Buddha: he has seen a peta with a man’s body and a pig’s head, with a tail growing out of its mouth and maggots oozing from the tail. The Buddha confirms it, having seen the same being long before and kept silent for the sake of those who would not believe. In the time of an earlier Buddha, he says, two elders, one a year senior to the other, lived together in a village monastery like brothers, the younger carrying the elder’s bowl and robe. A gifted visiting preacher, whom they took round all their alms villages, wanted the monastery for himself; over three evenings he hinted at something too grave to say, then told each elder that the other had warned him against him. The elders were shattered like struck pots, quarreled over the bowl and robe, and left by the east and west doors, telling the preacher to stay. The villagers who were wise said the preacher must have driven them off. For a hundred years neither elder could recite or meditate; then both came to the same monastery in the far west and were given the same room, wept, asked each other whether either had ever done wrong in thought, word or deed, saw the slander, and were reconciled. They went back and drove the preacher out with a snap of the fingers, and he, who had meditated twenty thousand years, could not bear one rebuke, fled and died. Read the full commentary story.

Maine and Oregon light stations, 1866–1904

Under the Lighthouse Board (1852–1910) keepers were federal employees of the Treasury, appointed with a good deal of political influence until they were brought under civil service in 1896. Lamps burned kerosene from 1877; from 1884 keepers wore a navy-blue uniform with a K on the collar for the keeper and numbers for his assistants. From 1876 the Board sent isolated stations traveling libraries of about fifty books in a wooden case, exchanged every few months by the tenders. Keepers at stations in the path of the spring and fall bird migrations picked up the birds that struck the lantern at night, and some kept records of them for naturalists.

Tillamook Rock Light, lit in 1881 on a basalt rock off Tillamook Head, Oregon, was known as Terrible Tilly; keepers and supplies went on and off by derrick and breeches buoy, and four keepers lived in the dwelling built onto the tower. Maine people had gone to the Northwest since the 1850s, when the lumbermen of East Machias founded the mill town of Port Gamble on Puget Sound and recruited workers from home. Franklin’s list of thirteen virtues is quoted from his Autobiography (ed. Frank Woodworth Pine, 1916). Gull Ledge, Ragged Harbor, and everyone in the story are invented.
