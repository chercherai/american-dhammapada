Walking the American WayCommentary 23.1 · Attānaṁ Ārabbha Kathika

Ask On, My Son

“Ask on, my son,” said Hillel.
— Shabbath 31a

Assim ouvi contar, on the Strand at New Amsterdam.

I. The Bark

New Amsterdam · September 1654

They came in on the Sainte Catherine at the beginning of September, twenty-three of them, four men and six women and thirteen children, out of Recife by way of the Spanish and the sea. The Portuguese had taken Recife back in January, and the Jews of Recife had been given three months to sell what they could and go. Most went home to Amsterdam. This boatload had been blown off its course, robbed by Spaniards as the story went, and picked up at Cape San Antonio by a French master named de la Motthe, who agreed to carry them to New Netherland for two thousand five hundred guilders, which they did not have.

He sued them in the burgomasters' court within the week. The court held their beds and chests and kettles against the debt and gave them forty-eight hours to pay. In the third week of September the Director-General, Stuyvesant, wrote home to the West India Company that he had told them to go, on account of their customary usury and deceitful trading with the Christians, and because they were poor and would be a charge on the deacons all winter.

They did not go. There was nowhere to go before spring, and the ice.

The oldest of them was Elias Valverde, who was seventy. He had been born in Porto with a Christian name, had come to Amsterdam as a boy when his father fled the Inquisition, and had learned there, in the Jewish school by the Houtgracht, the Hebrew that his grandfathers had been burned for. In 1638 he went out to Recife, under the Dutch, and for sixteen years he had taught the boys of Kahal Zur Israel to read. He had buried his wife there and both his sons. He came into New Amsterdam with his grandnephew Daniel, who was nineteen, a bundle of books in a tarred cloth, and a coat.

He was a small man, very upright, with a white beard cut square. He said little. In the evenings, in the back room on the Brugh Straat they rented from a ship's carpenter, he read aloud to the children of the twenty-three, and they sat around him on the floor in a ring.

II. The Tapster's Widow

The Strand · winter 1655

There was a woman on the Strand who knew him.

Her name was Margriet Claes, and she kept the tap-house by the Company's warehouse, where the sailors drank, and she had done well by it. She had been born in Recife too, the daughter of a Company sugar factor, and when she was sixteen and very handsome her father had gone to Elias Valverde, whose elder son was then twenty, and offered her. The boy could be baptized, the factor said. There would be a good dowry. A man of sense did not let a thing like that go by for a few old prayers.

Valverde had answered him in front of the whole counting-house. The words got round Recife by evening, in Dutch and Portuguese both, and the girl heard them from her maid. He had said that his son was not to be had for a font and a pretty face, and that a face was skin over a skull, the same as his own, and would be a skull again soon enough.

She married a ship's mate instead, and buried him, and came north. Now Elias Valverde had come in on a French bark with his bed sold out from under him, a beggar in her town.

She did not do anything herself. She put stuivers into the hands of the boys who swept out the tap-house, and of sailors who owed her for their drink, and of two of the Company's negroes who carried casks for her at the warehouse, and she told them where the old Jew walked in the mornings.

III. The Heerestraat

New Amsterdam · March 1655

He walked every morning from the Brugh Straat up the Heerestraat toward the land gate and back, because he had walked every morning in Recife and in Amsterdam before that, and an old man who stops walking stops.

At the end of February they began to follow him. A pack of boys first, and then grown men behind the boys, and then whoever was on the street and liked the sport. They walked behind him and beside him and shouted at him all the way to the gate and all the way back.

Thief. Fool. Usurer. Ox. Ass. Beast. You killed our Lord. You have no hope. Hell is waiting for you, old man, and all your people. One of them threw a fish head that struck him on the back of the neck. A sailor walked in front of him backward for a hundred paces with his breeches down. Women laughed in the doorways. The two Company men walked behind with the casks on their shoulders, and when the boys looked back at them for the next word they gave it, in a Dutch they had been taught for the purpose. On the third day Daniel went with him, and by the fifth Daniel was shaking.

They had come as far as the brewer's horse mill, where the street opened, when Daniel stopped him by the sleeve.

Uncle, let us go from here.

Where would we go?

Curaçao. There are Jews in Curaçao.

And if they shout at us in Curaçao?

Barbados, then.

And in Barbados?

Amsterdam. Anywhere. Uncle, they will kill you here.

Valverde stood in the street with the fish scale still on his collar and looked at the boy for a long time.

We came from Recife, he said. Before that I came from Porto. My father came from Lisbon, and his grandfather from Toledo. Every one of them said, let us go from here. Where the trouble is, Daniel, there it has to be finished. When it is finished you may go where you like. Who is it that shouts at us?

Everyone, said Daniel. The boys. The sailors. The women. Even the Company's slaves.

The old man was quiet. Then he told Daniel about the elephant.

IV. The Elephant

The Heerestraat · the same morning

When he was a man of fifty, he said, still in Amsterdam, they brought an elephant to the city from the Indies, a cow elephant called Hansken, and showed her at the fair. He had gone to see her with his sons, who were boys. She could wave a flag and fire a pistol and beat a drum, and take a coin out of a man's pocket with her nose and give it to her keeper.

They led her through the crowd at the fair, he said, from one booth to the next, through thousands of people. Boys pricked her with sticks. Men threw things to see her start. Somebody put a lit pipe to her ear. She did not start. She went where she was led, through all of it, with all of it falling on her from every side, and did what she had been taught to do. That was why they could lead her into a crowd. The Prince of Orange had sent for her, and a prince could have sat on her back.

So, said Valverde. It is my part now to be the elephant. Most people are not good. They will shout. I will go where I am going.

Behind them the brewer's horse went round and round inside the mill, blindfolded, turning the stone.

The horse is good because somebody trained it, he said. The elephant also. But a man has to be trained by somebody too, and there is only himself to do it, and that is better than the horse.

Then he told the boy the story of Hillel and the man who laid the wager, as he had told it to the boys of Recife every year at the beginning of the term: how a man bet four hundred zuzim that he could make Hillel angry, and came to his door on the eve of the Sabbath while he was washing, shouting, Where is Hillel? Where is Hillel? and asked him why the Babylonians had round heads, and an hour later why the people of Tadmor had weak eyes, and an hour after that why the Africans had broad feet; and how every time Hillel wrapped his mantle round him and came out and said, Ask on, my son, and answered him, until the man had lost all his money. He told it to the end, in his slow Dutch, standing in the street with the fish scale on his collar, and the boys who had been following them stood a little way off and listened, because nobody in New Amsterdam had told them a story that winter.

"Be warned for the future," said Hillel; "better it is that thou shouldst lose four hundred zouzim, and four hundred more after them, than it should be said of Hillel he lost his temper!"

— Shabbath 31a

The two Company men had set their casks down. They had been standing behind the boys, and now one of them, a tall man with grey in his hair, came a step closer.

The old man looked at him and said something to him in Portuguese, and the man answered in Portuguese.

V. The Stuivers

New Amsterdam · spring 1655

His name was Domingo. He had been born in Angola and baptized at Luanda by a Portuguese priest, and sold to the Dutch, and carried to Recife, and from Recife to this island, where the Company owned him and rented him out by the day. He had not heard his own language spoken to him as to a man in eleven years. He told Valverde that, standing by the horse mill, and Valverde told him who had been paying the boys, and Domingo said he knew.

The next morning the old man went out walking at the same hour, up the Heerestraat to the land gate and back. Two boys followed him, out of habit, and called him an ass, and then, because nobody took it up, stopped. A sailor said something. A woman in a doorway told the sailor to hold his tongue.

By the end of March the old man walked alone. By April the boys walked with him, because he would tell them a story if they did, about Hillel, or about the elephant, or about Recife; and one of them, a cooper's boy named Jochem, could tell the whole of the Hillel story by Pentecost without a mistake, and did, to anyone who would stand for it. The other Company man, Francisco, began to come to the back room on the Brugh Straat on Friday evenings and stand in the door, and after a while to sit, and Valverde gave him bread with the rest. People said afterward, in Dutch and in Portuguese, that every one of them who had been paid to shout at him that winter had come round, every one, and that nobody in the town could say quite how it had been done, since he had never once answered any of them back.

In June the Company's letter came from Amsterdam, written at the end of April. The directors could not, they said, consent to send the Jews away, in view of the large losses the nation had suffered in Brazil and the capital it had invested in the Company's shares. They might live and trade in New Netherland, provided their poor did not become a burden to the deacons. It was read in the council.

Domingo came to the tap-house by the warehouse the morning after the letter was read on the Strand, with the boys who swept and three of the sailors behind him, and they laid the stuivers Margriet Claes had paid them on her counter in a row, every one, and some of them worn smooth, and went out. She stood behind the counter and looked at the row of coins for a long time. Then she swept them into her apron, because they were money.

Valverde lived four more years. When he died they buried him in the little burying ground outside the wall that the Jews had at last been granted, the first ground the Jews of New Amsterdam ever owned. The boys who had once followed him up the Heerestraat shouting carried him there, Jochem the cooper's son at the front, and Domingo walked behind.

The verses

Silently shall I endure abuse as the elephant in battle endures the arrow sent from the bow: for the world is ill-natured.Dhammapada 320, tr. F. Max Müller (1881)
They lead a tamed elephant to battle, the king mounts a tamed elephant; the tamed is the best among men, he who silently endures abuse.Dhammapada 321, tr. F. Max Müller (1881)
Mules are good, if tamed, and noble Sindhu horses, and elephants with large tusks; but he who tames himself is better still.Dhammapada 322, tr. F. Max Müller (1881)

Māgandiyā, whose father once offered her to the Buddha and heard her body called a thing of filth, becomes a queen and never forgives him. She bribes townspeople and slaves to follow him through the streets with the ten terms of abuse: thief, fool, ox, ass, beast of hell, with no hope of heaven. Ānanda begs him to go to another city. And if they abuse us there? Then another. The Buddha answers that trouble should be settled where it arises, and compares himself to an elephant in battle that bears the arrows from all four quarters. By the end of his teaching the people who had been paid to revile him have entered the stream. Read the full commentary story; the grudge begins in commentary 2.1.

New Amsterdam, 1654–55

In early September 1654 twenty-three Jews from Dutch Brazil, four men, six women and thirteen children by one count, landed at New Amsterdam on the bark Sainte Catherine, after the Portuguese retook Recife. The master, Jacques de la Motthe, sued them for their passage, and the court held their goods against the debt. Director-General Peter Stuyvesant wrote to the West India Company asking to expel them, citing their “customary usury and deceitful trading with the Christians.” On April 26, 1655 the directors refused, pointing to the Jews’ losses in Brazil and their capital in the Company, and let them stay so long as their poor did not burden the deacons. They could not worship in public or buy a house; in February 1656 they were granted a burial ground. Their congregation, Shearith Israel, is the oldest in the United States.

Most of the Company’s enslaved workers in New Amsterdam came from West Central Africa and were Catholic and Portuguese-speaking, with names like Anthony, Manuel or Gracia “van Angola”; in 1644 eleven of the longest-held men were given half-freedom and land north of the town. Hansken, the elephant Rembrandt drew in Amsterdam in 1637, toured the fairs of Europe for eighteen years, waving a flag, firing a pistol and picking coins out of pockets, and died in Florence in November 1655. The story of Hillel and the man who bet four hundred zuzim he could make him angry is told in the Babylonian Talmud, Shabbat 31a; the version quoted is from Hebraic Literature, edited by Maurice H. Harris and first published in 1901. Valverde, Margriet Claes, Domingo and the rest are invented.
