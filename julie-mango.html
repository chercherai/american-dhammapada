Walking the American WayCommentary 3.1 · Meghiya

Julie Mango

For, O Krishna! the mind is fickle, boisterous, strong, and obstinate; and I think that to restrain it is as difficult as (to restrain) the wind.
— Bhagavadgîtâ 6:34

Is so I hear it, in the Redland.

I. Five Leaves

The Redland, south Miami-Dade County, Florida · June 2019

The Boodrams had a house and five acres off Krome Avenue, down where the county quits being city and goes to nurseries and avocado and mango, and for nine nights that June they were holding a Ramayan yagna in the yard. They had put up a rented tent with the sides rolled, and two hundred folding chairs, and a low stage with a white sheet over it and a cushion for the reader. Along the fence, nine new jhandi flags stood on bamboo poles. Every evening at six the cars came down from Broward, from Lauderhill and Miramar and Pembroke Pines, where the Trinidadians and Guyanese mostly lived, and people sat until ten hearing the Ramayan of Tulsidas sung and explained, and ate curry off paper plates afterward, and drove an hour home.

The reader was Pandita Chandra Maharaj. She was sixty-six. She had been born in Felicity, in central Trinidad, where her father made the bows and arrows for the village Ramleela every year, and she had come to Florida in 1984 and worked twenty-six years doing the books for a tile importer in Hialeah. When her husband died she went back to what she had learned sitting under her father's elbow. She studied for six years, and a mandir off State Road 7 installed her, and some of the old men stopped coming, and after a while came back. She read well. She did not embroider, and she spoke little outside the reading. People drove an hour for that.

She had one habit that people remarked on. When somebody came to her with a trouble, a son on pills or a husband gone quiet or a business going under, she did not answer straight off. She went on with whatever was in her hands until it was done. Then she said one thing, and it was generally short enough to remember in the car.

She stayed in the Boodrams' back bedroom for the nine days, because a reader does not go and come. In the daytime the house was empty. Mr. Boodram and his wife and both sons were at their nursery in Homestead from six in the morning, and nobody else came before five, when her sister-in-law Shanta drove down with the singers.

So in the daytime there was the Pandita, and there was Kavi.

Kavish Ramnarine was twenty-two. He had been born at Broward General and raised in Lauderhill, and he had left Broward College after three semesters and been let go by a warehouse in Opa-locka. He delivered food in his mother's Corolla. Fourteen months ago the Pandita had done the thirteen days' work for his grandfather, and on the thirteenth day he had asked if he could carry her harmonium to the car. He had been carrying it since. He drove her and set up the bedi. He knew which brass went where. He had stopped eating meat and had stopped a few other things too, or was trying, and he watched a great many videos about meditation. He had tried it in his bedroom at five in the morning with the door shut, and in the Corolla between orders, parked behind a Wendy's with the seat back. He could manage three or four minutes. Then his mother ran the blender, or the phone lit up, or a truck backed in, and he put it down to the place. He was sure that something large was about to happen to him if he could only get the conditions right.

On the sixth morning she sent him to the Publix on Krome for milk and ghee, and told him to bring back five mango leaves for the kalash, clean ones with no spot on them. The Boodrams grew palms and crotons and did not own a mango tree.

He bought the milk and the ghee. Coming back he left the car at the corner and walked in along the canal, since a canal bank in the Redland is as good a place as any to find a mango tree. The bank road was white limestone, and it was hot. The water was green and perfectly clear, and he could see fish hanging in it over the weed.

A quarter mile in, on the far side of a culvert, he came to the grove.

It was an old one. The trees had been planted forty feet apart sixty or seventy years ago, and nobody had topped them, so that they met overhead, and underneath it was a green room the size of a city block with a floor of dry leaves. The light came down in coins. It was ten degrees cooler inside the first row. Fruit hung everywhere on long stems. Most of it was the big red commercial kinds, but along the canal side stood a row of low, dense, round-headed trees with small flattened fruit going from green to rose, and he knew those. His grandfather had had one in a tub behind the house in Lauderhill and had babied it for twenty years against the fungus. They were Julies. An old man in a straw hat was sitting on an overturned crate by a pump house. He said in Spanish and then in English that the grove was sold and would be houses by next year, and that Kavi could take what he wanted, leaves, mangoes, anything, it was all the same to him. His father had planted the Julies for a Jamaican foreman in 1958, he said. There was nothing wrong with them except that nobody would pay for a mango that small.

Kavi picked five leaves. He stood a minute longer in the green shade. It was so quiet in there that he could hear a mango let go somewhere and come down through the leaves. He thought that this was a place a person could really sit. He had been looking for it for a year, in his bedroom with the door shut and in the Corolla in parking lots, and it had been here the whole time.

II. Three Times

The Boodrams' kitchen · eleven o'clock

The Pandita was at the stove with a bundle of bamboo garden stakes from the Home Depot. The mandir's children did Ramleela every October, and every summer she made the arrows, forty of them, because there was nobody else who knew how. She took the five leaves and looked at each one and nodded.

Panditaji, he said. There's a mango grove down the canal. An old one. Nobody's in there. It's the kind of place the rishis sat. I want to go and sit there today. Do my sadhana properly, the whole afternoon, till it's time. I think I need that.

She turned a stake over the gas flame.

Wait, she said. I alone here today. Wait till somebody else reach.

Shanta Aunty's coming at five.

So wait till five.

He washed the leaves and set them in the brass lota around a dry coconut, and he swept the stage. At half past eleven he came back to the kitchen door.

Panditaji, you have everything here. You finished what you had to do. I haven't finished anything. I don't see how I'm going to get anywhere carrying a harmonium. I'd like to go to that grove.

I alone here, Kavi. Wait.

He went out and wiped down two hundred chairs that did not need it. At noon he came back a third time and said it again, and this time he said it with his hands together.

She laid the stake down. She looked at him the way she looked at a leaf.

When a boy say he want to go and strive, what I could tell him? she said. I can't tell him no. Do what you think it is time to do.

He touched her feet, quickly, and was out of the yard before she had picked up the stake again. He left his phone in the car on purpose.

III. The Grove

Under the Julie trees · the afternoon

He chose the fourth Julie from the culvert because its roots made a seat. He folded his legs and set his back straight the way the videos said, with his hands in his lap. He shut his eyes and began to say the name of Ram with his breath.

He had perhaps four minutes of it.

Then, without any decision on his part, he was thinking about Reshma Singh. She sang in the youth group. At Phagwah in March she had been soaked to the skin in a white kurta, pink and green with abeer, laughing with her head back. He had looked away at the time. He did not look away now. He looked for a long while, and when he had finished with what he had actually seen, his mind supplied the rest. After Reshma there were others. He had gotten rid of certain things on his phone in January, and the phone was in the car, and it made no difference at all. He had them all by heart, and here in the place of the rishis they played for him one after another, and nothing interrupted them.

He opened his eyes. He was sweating. He shut them and went back to the name.

It was his cousin Darren next. Darren had the dispatcher's job at their uncle's trucking yard, the job that had been promised to Kavi in front of the whole family at a wedding. Darren called him Baba and Swami now and asked him when he was going up the mountain. Kavi began explaining to Darren what Darren was, and once he had begun he could not stop. He went from Darren to the uncle. From the uncle he went to his own father, who had gone to Orlando with a woman from his job when Kavi was eleven and sent a card at Divali with fifty dollars in it. He said to his father, in full, with pauses for effect, what he had been composing for eleven years. It did not make him feel better. He said it again with improvements.

The afternoon went on. A wind came up in the tops of the trees and the light turned yellow. Mangoes came down around him now and then with a thump, and he could smell the ones that had split. There were small black ants on his ankle. Twice he got as far as noticing his breath, and each time it was like putting a hand flat on water.

Last was the supervisor at the warehouse in Opa-locka. He had made Kavi stand in front of the line and be told, before thirty people, that he was too slow to be worth the badge. In Kavi's mind the man was in the parking lot beside his truck. Kavi had something in his hand, and he used it. He was thorough. He watched the man's face while he did it, and he found that he liked watching.

Something moved, and he opened his eyes. An iguana as long as his arm had come down the trunk of the next tree and stopped on a root six feet off, head up, its throat going. He had a piece of limestone in his hand. He did not remember picking it up. His arm was already back.

He put the stone down on the leaves, carefully, as if it might go off.

He sat with his hands shaking. He was a person who had given up meat. He had touched the Pandita's feet that noon, and for a year he had told himself that he was leaving the world behind, and he had come to the quietest place in South Florida to do it. All afternoon the world had sat in his lap. He had brought it in with him. He had not had ten clean breaths together.

How? he said out loud. I came here for God. How is this what's in me?

The first big drops came through the leaves then, and thunder off toward the Everglades, and within a minute it was raining the way it rains there on a June afternoon, straight down, as if a tank had split. He sat under it. He was not doing anything else that was any use.

IV. The Bank

The canal · after the rain

It stopped in forty minutes and the sun came out and the limestone steamed. He walked back along the canal, wet through, with six Julie mangoes in the front of his shirt, because he could not think what else to bring her.

Two boys had been fishing at the culvert before the storm and had gone. On the bank road, a yard from the water, lay a fish they had thrown there, a brown armored catfish, the kind from the aquarium trade that is in every canal now and that nobody wants on a hook. It was not dead. It lay on the hot white rock and bent itself double and sprang, and it came down in the same place. It did this again and again without any direction to it, and its gills worked. The water was three feet away and clear as glass. It had no way of knowing that.

Kavi stood over it a long time, longer than made sense. Then he put the side of his shoe under it and flipped it into the canal. It hung in the water where it fell for a second or two, then went down into the weed.

When he came into the Boodrams' yard he saw what the storm had done. The wind had got under the tent on the south side and torn four stakes out, and that whole wall of canvas was down across the chairs. The white sheet on the stage was grey with wet. The harmonium was not on the stage. Neither was the book stand, or the Ramayan in its red cloth, or the brass.

They were in the kitchen, dry, on the table. The Pandita was sitting beside them with her left leg out straight on a second chair and a bag of frozen peas on the hip. It was the hip the surgeon had done in November. Her sari was wet to the waist. The harmonium weighed thirty-one pounds; he had weighed it once.

V. The Arrow

The kitchen · four o'clock

He put the mangoes on the counter. He did not sit down.

Tell me, she said.

He told her. He was twenty-two and it was not easy to say to a woman of sixty-six, a pandita, what had gone through him under that tree, and he said it anyway. He told her about the girl and the others, and about Darren and his father, and about the man in the parking lot. He told her about the stone in his hand. I went there out of faith, he said. I swear I did. And that is all that came. Those three things, round and round. I don't understand it.

She heard all of it without moving her face.

You do a wrong thing today, she said. I not vex. But hear it. I tell you wait, I alone. Three times. And you went your way. When I ask you to stay, you must not leave me alone and go. I am not saying it for the hip. She lifted the peas and set them down again. You went because the mind say go. The mind see a pretty place and it start to jump, and you follow it out the gate like a man following a goat. Then you sit down in the pretty place and you surprise that it still jumping. Is the same mind, child. You carry it there.

She pulled the bundle of bamboo toward her across the table and drew one stake out.

Look down this.

He sighted along it. It wandered left, and then right, and it had a hook at the tip.

In Felicity my father used cane arrow, the flower stalk of the cane. He would cut two hundred after crop and lose half. Same thing. Nothing does grow straight. If you shoot that, it going in the neighbor yard. She lit the burner and held the stake above the flame at its worst bend, turning it, until the skin of the bamboo began to sweat. She took it off and bent it against the curve with both thumbs and held it there while it cooled, and put it to her eye. She put it back to the fire at another place. She did this five times. It took as long as it took. Then she handed it to him and he looked down it, and it ran dead true from his eye to the far wall.

He had watched her make thirty of these that week and had taken it for a thing old people did with their hands. He had not once looked down one.

It will take the bend again, if you leave it in the damp, she said. So you do it again. That is all the work is. Arjuna tell Krishna the same thing you tell me. He was a better archer than you.

For, O Krishna! the mind is fickle, boisterous, strong, and obstinate; and I think that to restrain it is as difficult as (to restrain) the wind.

The Deity said: Doubtless, O you of mighty arms! the mind is difficult to restrain, and fickle. Still, O son of Kuntî! it may be restrained by constant practice and by indifference (to worldly objects).

— Bhagavadgîtâ 6:34–35, tr. K. T. Telang (1882)

Constant practice, she said. He didn't say a nice grove.

Kavi stood holding the arrow.

There was a fish, he said. On the canal bank. Some boys threw it up on the rock. It kept jumping. It couldn't—

Yes, said the Pandita. That is it exactly. Tulsidas say so in the first book. You will hear it tonight.

VI. The Sixth Night

The Boodrams' yard · evening

Shanta came at five with the singers and found Kavi and the Boodram sons driving tent stakes. By six the wall was up and the sheet had been changed, and the cars were turning in off Krome Avenue. He carried the harmonium out and set it on the stage, and then the book stand and the book, and went back for her. She came across the yard on his arm, slowly, in a dry sari, and nobody watching would have known about the hip. Mrs. Boodram had come home to a wet yard and a wet reader and had started to cry, and the Pandita had told her to go and see about the pumpkin.

She read that night from the first book, out of her order, and she made no remark on it. She sang the doha once in the Avadhi, to the old tune, with Shanta on the dholak, and then gave it in English out of the worn green Growse she had from her father, who had it from a schoolmaster in Chaguanas.

Free from sensual passions and absorbed in devout affection to Ráma, the soul disports itself like a fish in the ambrosial lake of his beloved name.

— Rámáyana of Tulsi Dás, Childhood, dohá 26, tr. F. S. Growse

The Englishman put soul, she told the two hundred chairs. Tulsidas put man. The mind. He say the mind is a fish. Now a fish is a quick thing and a strong thing, and where it belong, it is beautiful. Take it out and throw it on the bank and you will see something else. It flap. It jump this side and that side, and you would swear is wickedness, but it only frighten and dry. It doesn't want a prettier bank. It want the water. She looked down the tent, over all of them, and her eye did not stop on the back row. And you can't throw yourself in. Somebody have to stand near you. So when your guru say wait, wait.

That was all she said about it. She went on with the reading.

Kavi sat at the back by the tent pole with the dhantal across his knees, the long steel rod and its striker, keeping the time for the singers. He kept it through the whole of that night's singing and did not once come in late. He noticed, somewhere in the second hour, that he had not been anywhere else. Reshma Singh was thirty feet away in the third row and he knew it, the way he knew where the tent pole was. Darren was not there. The man in the parking lot had gone back to being a man he had worked for once. The thoughts still came up, and each time he felt the pull of it and did not go. He did not feel holy. He felt like the stake when it came off the fire, sore along one side, and for the moment straight.

He went on driving her and carrying the harmonium, and he did not ask again to be let off. He went back to the grove only once. The next year he drove down Krome to see it, and it was a field of white limestone fill with survey stakes in rows and one sales trailer. Past the culvert the canal was the same, green and clear, with the fish hanging in it over the weed.

But that was later. On the sixth night, after the arti, when the plates had gone round and the cars were pulling out, he found her at the kitchen table with a knife and the six Julies. She had cut the cheeks off the first one and scored them. She pushed one across to him on the flat of the blade.

You pick these?

Yes, Panditaji.

Then something good come out of that place, she said, and bit into her half.

The verses

As a fletcher makes straight his arrow, a wise man makes straight his trembling and unsteady thought, which is difficult to guard, difficult to hold back.Dhammapada 33, tr. F. Max Müller (1881)
As a fish taken from his watery home and thrown on dry ground, our thought trembles all over in order to escape the dominion of Mara (the tempter).Dhammapada 34, tr. F. Max Müller (1881)

The commentary refers to the old discourse about Meghiya (Udāna 4.1), who was for a time the Buddha's attendant. Coming back from his alms round along a riverbank, Meghiya sees a lovely mango grove and asks leave to go and meditate there. The Buddha says: wait, I am alone, until another monk comes. Meghiya asks a second and a third time, and the Buddha answers that when a man speaks of striving there is nothing to say, and he should do what he thinks it is time for. Meghiya sits all day at the root of a tree and is overrun by three kinds of thought, of sensual pleasure, of ill will and of cruelty, and is astonished that he, who left home out of faith, should be so caught. In the evening he goes back and tells it. The Buddha reproves him for leaving when he was asked to stay, tells him the mind is flighty and must be kept in hand, and speaks the two verses. South Florida, 2019

Between 1838 and 1917 the British shipped several hundred thousand indentured laborers from north India to the sugar estates of Trinidad and British Guiana. Their descendants, the Indo-Caribbean people, kept a Hinduism centered on the Ramcharitmanas, the Ramayan that Tulsidas composed in Avadhi in the sixteenth century: yard flags on bamboo poles (jhandi), multi-night household readings called yagnas, and the open-air Ramleela plays, for which central Trinidad villages such as Felicity are known. From the 1960s on many families moved again, to New York, Toronto and South Florida, where Broward County towns like Lauderhill and Miramar have large Trinidadian and Guyanese communities and many mandirs. Pandits were by custom Brahmin men; women began to be installed as pandits in Trinidad in the 1990s, and the question is still argued. The Pandita reads from the English prose version by F. S. Growse, a British magistrate in India, first published in parts beginning in 1877; her gloss on the doha is fair to the original, where it is the mind (man) that the devotees make a fish in that lake.

The Redland is the farming district of southern Miami-Dade County, named for its pockets of red clay over limestone, and it has grown mangoes commercially for generations. The Julie, a small, flattened, richly flavored mango on a dwarf tree, is the favorite of Jamaica and Trinidad and is grown in Florida mostly by Caribbean families in their yards, since it suffers from fungus in the humid summers. Old groves in the Redland have been giving way to housing for decades. The drainage canals of South Florida are full of introduced fish, among them the South American armored catfish released from aquariums. The people, the yagna and the grove in this story are invented.
