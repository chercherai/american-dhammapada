Dinner on the Ground

Hatred stirreth up strifes: but love covereth all sins.
— Proverbs 10:12

Now the way I heard it, on the Little Buffalo.

I. Hagar

Newton County, Arkansas · 1886–1892

Jesse Coffey's daddy died when Jesse was fifteen, and after that Jesse did it all himself, the plowing and the fence and the stock and the wood, on forty acres of rocky bottom and steep ridge where Cutter Branch comes into the Little Buffalo. He kept his mother too. She had the rheumatism and could not do much but sit on the porch and shell beans and watch him work.

Son, let me go fetch you a wife.

I don't want a wife, Mama. I want to see after you as long as you live.

You're doing the field work and the house work both, and it ain't right, and I won't have it. Let me fetch you a girl.

He said no, and no, and then he quit saying anything. When she put on her bonnet to go up the creek to the Villines place, where there were four grown girls, he asked her where she was going, and when she told him, he said not there, and told her about a family down at the Gap he liked better. So she went where he wanted, and came back with Ada Treat, who was eighteen and red-headed and could outwork any two men on the creek. They were married at the Gap in the fall of 1886.

Ada had no children. One year. Three years. Five.

You picked her yourself, his mother said at last, and she's barren as a rock. A man with no young'uns, when he dies the place goes to the cousins and the name goes in the ground with him. Let me go fetch you another one.

Hush, Mama.

But she would not hush, and Ada heard it through the board wall, night after night, lying in the dark beside him. She lay there and thought it through. A son don't cross his mama, not on this creek. If the old woman goes out and fetches some girl, and the girl has a baby, I'll be the hired girl in my own kitchen till I die. Then she thought: suppose I fetch one myself.

She knew her Bible. She knew where Sarai said to Abram, and she went and found it to be sure.

And Sarai said unto Abram, Behold now, the LORD hath restrained me from bearing: I pray thee, go in unto my maid; it may be that I may obtain children by her. And Abram hearkened to the voice of Sarai.

— Genesis 16:2

She went up the hollow to the Sizemores, who were the poorest people on Cutter Branch and had a girl named Lettie, sixteen, who was quick and quiet and pretty. The Sizemores ran her off the porch the first time. Woman, what are you saying to us? She came back the next week and the week after. She said she was barren, and a place with no children dies out, and if Lettie gave Jesse a son, Lettie's boy would have the forty acres and Lettie would have the say of the house, and the Sizemores would never be hungry again. In the end, because they were hungry, they let her take the girl.

There was no preacher and no paper. Everybody on the creek knew what it was, and some of them said so, and the Coffeys did not go to meeting after that. But nobody's business is anybody's business in a hollow, and in a year people mostly quit talking.

II. Pennyroyal

Cutter Branch · 1892–1894

As soon as Lettie was in the house Ada thought: if she has a baby, it's her house. That's just how it'll be. I can't let her have one.

Honey, she told Lettie, the minute you think you're carrying, you tell me first. I'll see you through it.

Lettie said she would, and when it happened she did. Every evening Ada fixed her a cup of sassafras tea with her own hands, and that night she put pennyroyal in it, a good handful, steeped strong. Lettie lost the baby before the month was out. In the spring Lettie told her again, and Ada fixed the tea, and Lettie lost that one too.

That summer at a quilting at the Villines place, with Ada not there, the women sat around the frame and asked Lettie about it, and she told them, and they looked at one another across the quilt.

Child, you're a fool. Who do you reckon fixes your tea? That woman is afraid you'll get the upper hand. She's putting something in it. Next time you don't tell her nothing.

So the third time Lettie said nothing. But in the fifth month Ada saw her bending over the washpot in the yard, and saw the shape of her.

Why didn't you tell me you was carrying?

You brought me down here, Lettie said, and twice you've made me lose my babies. Why would I tell you?

Ada went in the house and stood by the stove. Now I'm done for, she thought. And after that she watched. She watched all summer and into the fall, and in November, when Lettie was near her time and down sick with a cold, Ada fixed her a cup of tea with whiskey and honey in it for the cough, and a double handful of the other thing.

It was too late for the tea to do what it did before. The baby was too far along. What it did instead was bring her to labor, wrong, with the baby lying crossways in her and no way out. The granny woman from the Gap came at midnight and worked till dawn and could not turn it, and went out on the porch and told Jesse there was nothing she could do.

Lettie knew. She raised up on the pillow and looked at Ada standing at the foot of the bed.

You killed me, she said. You fetched me down here, you, nobody else, and you killed my three young'uns, and now you've killed me. I'm going. And when I'm gone I'll come back. I don't care what I come back as. I'll come back and get yours.

She died before noon with the baby in her.

Jesse took hold of Ada in the kitchen. You've done for my whole family, he said, and he beat her with his fists and his knees and a stick of stovewood until the old woman got up off the porch and came in and stopped him. Ada lay in the back room all December. She died on Christmas Eve, of what he had done to her, though the doctor who came out from Jasper wrote down pneumonia. Jesse sold the stock in the spring and went off to the zinc mines in Missouri and was not seen on the creek again.

III. The Cat and the Hen

Cutter Branch · 1895–1910

The house on Cutter Branch stood empty after that, with the door open and the old woman's rocker still on the porch. Nobody would live in it. People said things about it, the way people do.

What the old women on the creek said was this. That the spring after the funerals, a yellow cat took up in the Coffey house, a thin one with a torn ear that nobody had ever seen before. And a red hen that had gone wild when the stock was sold was still laying in the loft. And the cat found the hen's nest and ate the eggs. And the hen laid again, and the cat ate them again. Three times. They said that the third time the hen stood in the loft door with her feathers all out and made a sound no hen makes, and then she went at the cat, and the cat killed her. And they said if you had been there and heard it, you would know who those two were.

They said more. That a few years after, a painter came down out of the bluffs above the Little Buffalo, a she-painter, the last one anybody in Newton County ever saw, and every spring for three springs she took the fawns from one doe that came down to drink at the mouth of Cutter Branch. They said the doe stood on the gravel bar and bleated for them, three springs running, so you could hear it a mile. They said that in the fall of 1910 a man from Jasper killed the painter on the bluff, and the same week the storekeeper's wife at the Gap was brought to bed of a girl, and they named her Opal. And that the next fall a boy shot that old doe in the bottom, and that was the fall the woman named Dorcas came walking down the creek road with nothing in her hands and nobody belonging to her.

That is what the old women said. The preachers said it was foolishness, and the old women said yes, it surely was, and went on saying it.

IV. The Cradle

Shiloh Gap · 1928–1931

Dorcas lived wherever somebody would let her, in a smokehouse, in a corn crib, in the loft of the church house one winter till the deacons found out. She did washing and hoed corn for whoever would pay her in meal. She was dark and thin and nobody could guess how old she was. Dorcas is a Bible name. It means a deer.

She and Opal had been thick as thieves since they were girls. Opal was the storekeeper's daughter, with shoes in winter, and Dorcas was nobody's, and they had sat together on the store porch with their feet hanging off and talked the way girls do. When Opal married Clell Henson in 1928 and went to live at the mouth of the hollow, where the mail road comes in off the ridge, Dorcas was the first one to walk out and see the new house.

The baby came the next fall, a boy. Dorcas walked out to see it.

Where's Opal at?

In the back room, Clell's mother said. She just had the baby.

Boy or girl? Let me look at it.

She went in and bent over the cradle, close, the way women do, and nobody thought a thing about it. She stayed a while and then she went home. In the morning the baby was dead in the cradle with not a mark on him. People on the creek said what they said, that she had bent down over him and drawn out his breath, the way a cat will do a baby if you leave it, and that was why she had been so close. Opal did not believe it. Nobody could make her believe it.

The second one came in the summer of 1930, the summer the rain quit. It did not rain in June and it did not rain in July. The Little Buffalo went down to a string of green holes. The corn burned in the field before it tasseled and the gardens dried up and blew away. Dorcas walked out to see the baby, and bent over the cradle, and in the morning that one was dead too.

That was the winter everybody went hungry. People ate poke and wild onions and squirrel and whatever else there was. Down at England, in Lonoke County, hundreds of farmers came into town and stood in the street and told the merchants they would have food for their children or know why. The Red Cross came into Newton County with flour and lard and slips of paper you had to fill out. Dorcas did not fill out the slips of paper. She looked at Opal's mother's shelves in the store at the Gap the way a dog looks at a door.

In the spring Opal was carrying again. She went to Clell.

Something out here has killed two of my babies, she said. I don't care what it is. I don't care what anybody says it is. This one I'm going to have at Mama's.

It happened that in May Dorcas had gone off with a truckload of pickers out of Jasper, following the crops, berries and then beans and then peaches, the way hill people did that year to get a little money, and she was gone four months. Some of that crew did not come back. It was a hot summer. Opal went to her mother's at the store in June, and had the baby there in July, a boy, and lived.

V. The Baptizing Hole

Shiloh Gap · August, 1931

Dorcas came back in the middle of August with what she had on and walked straight out to the Henson place at the mouth of the hollow.

Where's Opal at?

Clell's mother stood in the door. Where you won't get at her. Something's been coming in this house and killing her babies, and she's gone to her mama's to have this one.

She can go where she likes, Dorcas said. She won't get away from me. And she turned around and started for the Gap, walking fast in the heat, and Clell's mother said afterward that the dog would not go out from under the porch for an hour.

•••

There was a revival on at the Gap that week, a brush arbor on the flat by the creek, cedar posts with oak boughs laid across the top for shade and split-log benches under, and lanterns hung on nails at night. The preacher was a Pentecostal girl from over in Missouri named Velma Gentry. She was nineteen years old and had been preaching since she was fourteen. She wore a plain white dress and no jewelry and no paint, and she did not holler, and she did not dance on the benches, and people came from three counties anyhow, in wagons and on foot and in a Model T or two, to sit under the arbor in the heat and listen to her. Her brother Ancel drove her from meeting to meeting in an old truck and led the singing with a guitar, and slept in the truck bed, and saw to everything else.

On the Sunday afternoon Opal and Clell carried the baby up to the arbor and Sister Velma took him in her arms and dedicated him to the Lord in front of everybody, and they named him out loud, Jesse, after nobody in particular, because Opal liked it. Then they started home down the creek path with the baby, because Opal said she would not hide at her mother's forever.

Below the arbor the creek bent around a rock ledge and made a deep green hole where they did the baptizing. It was very hot. Opal handed the baby to Clell and waded into the baptizing hole in her slip and ducked under and came up and sat on the ledge in the shade. Then Clell went in, and Opal sat on the rock with her wet hair down her back and nursed the baby.

She looked up, and Dorcas was coming down the creek path.

Clell! Clell! Come quick! Come quick! It's her!

She did not wait for him. She got up with the baby and ran, barefoot, in her wet slip, back up the path and up the bank and in under the brush arbor, where Sister Velma was standing at the front under the lanterns talking to sixty or seventy people who had stayed for the evening meeting. Opal ran straight down the aisle between the split-log benches and fell on her knees on the sawdust and laid the baby down at the girl preacher's feet.

Here. I give him to you. He's yours. Just let him live.

Sumner Daves was standing at the back of the arbor where he always stood at revivals, a blacksmith as wide as a door, to keep the drunks from coming in off the road. He saw Dorcas come up the bank, and something in her face made him step out into the opening and put his arm across it, and he would not let her by.

Sister Velma looked down at the baby on the sawdust, and up at the opening.

Ancel, she said. Go bring that woman in here.

Ancel went down the aisle and spoke to Sumner Daves, and Sumner took his arm down, and Ancel brought Dorcas in.

Here she comes, Opal said, on her knees, without looking around. Here she comes.

Let her come, said Sister Velma. Hush, now.

Dorcas came down the aisle and stood in front of her. The arbor was so quiet you could hear the creek.

Why have you done this? Sister Velma said to her. Her voice was not loud. Why do you keep doing it? You two have been at it since before either one of you was born. If you hadn't walked in here tonight you'd have gone on till Judgment, the cat and the hen, the painter and the doe, one of you and then the other, taking the young'uns and taking the young'uns. Every one of you saying, she done it to me, I'll do it to her. It don't stop that way. It never has stopped that way, not once since the world was made. Listen at me.

Say not, I will do so to him as he hath done to me: I will render to the man according to his work.

Hatred stirreth up strifes: but love covereth all sins.

— Proverbs 24:29; 10:12

Dorcas stood in front of the girl preacher with her hands hanging at her sides. Nobody in the arbor could see her face but Sister Velma. Whatever Sister Velma saw in it, she nodded, once, as if somebody had told her the answer to a question.

VI. Bread

The same night

Opal, Sister Velma said. Pick up your baby and give him to her.

Opal looked up from the sawdust. I'm scared to.

I know it. Don't be. You've got nothing to be scared of from her now.

Opal picked up the baby off the sawdust and stood up in her wet slip, shaking, and held him out, and Dorcas took him.

She held him a long time. She put her face down against his head and smelled of it, the way women do. She kissed him on the forehead and on both his hands. Then she gave him back to his mother, carefully, the way you hand somebody something that might spill, and stood there in front of everybody and began to cry.

What are you crying for? Sister Velma said.

I never had enough to eat, Dorcas said. Not one day of my life. I've always got by somehow, but I never had enough. Not once. And now I don't know how I'm going to live.

Sister Velma looked at her a while. Then she turned to Opal.

Take her home with you. Let her live in your house. Feed her the best you've got.

If thine enemy be hungry, give him bread to eat; and if he be thirsty, give him water to drink: For thou shalt heap coals of fire upon his head, and the LORD shall reward thee.

— Proverbs 25:21–22

Then Ancel struck up the guitar, because he did not know what else to do, and after a minute people began to sing.

VII. Wet Year, Dry Year

Cutter Branch · 1931–1934

Opal took her home and made her a pallet in the loft over the front room and fed her the best they had, which that year was cornbread and sorghum and a little side meat, and buttermilk when the cow was fresh.

But in September Clell started knocking corn off the cob for meal on the porch below the loft, with a stick of hickory, and every time the stick came down Dorcas sat straight up on her pallet.

I can't stay up here. It sounds like it's coming down on my head. Put me somewhere else.

So Opal put her in the crib by the meal grinder, and then in the springhouse, and then in the kitchen lean-to, and then in the cellar, and then in the shed by the ash hopper, and then in the little house by the mail road gate. And Dorcas would not stay in any of them. The grinder sounds like it's grinding me. The boys come and empty the slop buckets right by the door. The dogs lay up against the wall all night. The boys use that side of it for a toilet. Your mama-in-law throws the sweepings right there. And the big boys sit on the gate and tell fortunes with a broom straw and a Bible and holler.

At last Clell said there was nobody living in the old Coffey house up at the head of Cutter Branch. It had stood empty for thirty-seven years. He and two Villines boys went up and put a new roof on it and a door that shut, and carried up a bedstead, and Dorcas went up there by herself and lived in it. Every morning Opal or the boy carried up a covered dish with the best of whatever they had.

•••

In the spring of 1932 Dorcas said to Opal, It's going to be wet this year. Don't plant nothing in the bottom. Plant your corn up on the ridge.

Clell laughed. Nobody on the Little Buffalo plants on the ridge if he has bottom ground. But Opal made him, and that June it rained for nine days without stopping, and Cutter Branch came up over the bottoms from hill to hill and took every row of corn on the creek and laid it down in the mud. Up on the ridge, Clell's corn stood up green and dry.

The year after that she said nothing, because it was an ordinary year, and in the spring of 1934 she said, Dry this year. Plant the bottoms. Right down by the water. And that summer the ridges burned up and the bottoms by the creek, where the ground held the damp, made a crop.

People came to Opal. How did you know? Wet or dry, your corn comes in every year, and ours burns or drowns. Who's telling you?

Dorcas, Opal said. Up at the old Coffey place. She tells me wet or dry, and I plant high or low according. Don't you see me carry the dish up there every morning? That's where it goes. You carry her something too, and she'll tell you.

So they did. They carried up cornbread and beans and fried pies and jars of kraut and whatever there was, and Dorcas sat on the old Coffey porch in the old woman's rocker and looked at the sky over the bluffs and told them, high ground or low, and she was never wrong. She put on flesh. She lived to be very old. She was the one who told the people at the Gap that there ought to be eight families to take the dinner up to her, turn and turn about, and that on Decoration Day, when the whole Gap spreads the dinner on the ground under the cedars at the cemetery, the first plate ought to be fixed before the blessing and carried up the hollow.

They still do it. Dorcas has been gone a long time now, and the old Coffey house fell in years ago and there is nothing at the head of Cutter Branch but a chimney and a walnut tree. But every Decoration Day at Shiloh Gap, when the boards are laid across the sawhorses and the women uncover the dishes, somebody fixes a plate first, chicken and dumplings and deviled eggs and a piece of every pie, and covers it with a clean dish towel, and one of the children carries it up the hollow in both hands and sets it on the chimney rock, and comes back, and then they say the blessing.

Background

The verse

For hatred does not cease by hatred at any time: hatred ceases by love, this is an old rule.Dhammapada 5, tr. F. Max Müller (1881)

In the commentary, a barren wife, afraid that a fertile second wife will make her a servant in her own house, chooses the second wife herself, then secretly gives her drugs that make her miscarry, twice. The third time the child lodges crosswise and the young wife dies cursing her, vowing to come back and devour her children, and the husband beats the barren wife to death. The two are reborn in the same house as a cat and a hen, and then as a leopardess and a doe, and in each life one eats the other's young and vows revenge. At last one is born a woman of Sāvatthī and the other a yakkhinī, an ogress, who comes as a friend and devours two of her babies. With the third, the mother flees into the monastery and lays the child at the Buddha's feet. The Buddha has the ogress brought in, tells them that hatred is never ended by hatred, and has the mother hand her the child. The ogress kisses it and weeps because she has never had enough to eat; the mother takes her home, tries lodging her in one place after another, and settles her outside the village, where she tells the farmers whether the year will be wet or dry and is fed by all of them. The Arkansas Ozarks, 1930–1934

In the drought of 1930, June and July were the driest Arkansas had recorded, Little Rock went seventy-one days without rain, and August temperatures reached 113 degrees. Between a third and a half of the state's crops were lost, and by November outbreaks of pellagra and typhoid were being blamed on it. When the Red Cross opened its food program in January 1931, more than 165,000 people signed up in three days. On January 3, a few hundred farmers gathered in the town of England, in Lonoke County, and demanded food for their hungry neighbors; local merchants handed it out, and the England food riot made national news.

The Assemblies of God was founded at Hot Springs, Arkansas, in 1914, and from its first year it gave credentials to women as evangelists, though not as pastors until 1935; by the fall of 1914, 142 of its 512 credential holders were women missionaries or evangelists. Country revivals were often held under a brush arbor, poles driven into the ground with cut branches laid across the top, put up when a traveling preacher sent word ahead. Decoration Day, when families clean the graves of a country cemetery and spread a dinner on the ground, is kept on a Sunday each spring or summer throughout the Upland South, including Arkansas and southern Missouri.
