Low Ground

Let your conversation be without covetousness; and be content with such things as ye have.
— Hebrews 13:5

Way I heard it told, on the swamp at Prospect.

I. Clearing

Prospect, Robeson County, North Carolina · March 2016

The field below Dwight Locklear's house had been tobacco for sixty years and then it had been nothing for eleven. His granddaddy had cleared it with a mule and a grubbing hoe in the year the Depression ended, and his daddy had cropped it every summer of Dwight's boyhood, and Dwight had cropped it himself, primed it and looped it and hung it in the two log barns at the top of the turn row, until the government bought out the quota in 2004 and paid him to stop. The checks came every year for ten years. He had spent them, more or less, on what a man spends things on: a truck, a roof, the light bill, his daughter's tuition at Pembroke, his wife's teeth. The last one came in 2014. The barns were still standing, leaning on each other, with broomsedge growing in the doorways. The field had gone to sweetgum and dog fennel and broomsedge and pine seedlings as high as his chest.

In March of 2016 he went down to clear it.

He had a little Kubota with a box blade and a bush hog, and a chainsaw, and his grandson Tyrese on Saturdays when Tyrese could be got out of the bed, and for two weeks he cut and dragged and burned, and the smoke went up white from the bottom of the field and lay along the edge of the swamp at evening, and the people driving the Prospect road slowed down to look at it. Forty acres. He meant to put it all in cotton. Cotton was sixty-some cents and the man at the gin said it would go higher, and Dwight had worked the numbers on the back of a feed-store receipt at the kitchen table until his wife Glenda took the pencil out of his hand and told him to come to bed.

He was sixty-one. He had a good brick house with a carport, and the forty acres, and twelve more across the road that he rented out, and a note at the Farm Service Agency, and a bad shoulder, and no use at all for anybody's church. Glenda went to the Holiness Methodist church down the road three times a week and he drove her there and sat in the truck in the lot with the radio on the farm report. He would tell you, if you asked him, that he believed in the price of cotton and the weather and his own two hands, and that the first two did not care about him and the third one was all he had. He had told the preacher that once. The preacher had laughed and said, Well, Dwight, that's a start.

The mail came by at ten-forty every morning except Sunday.

The carrier on the Prospect route was a young woman named Leanna Oxendine. She was twenty-nine. Her people were from out toward Pembroke, and her grandmother had taught second grade at Prospect School for thirty-one years and had taught Dwight himself to read, in a room that was not there anymore. Leanna drove the route in a white Jeep she had bought used off a carrier in Bladen County, with the steering wheel on the right-hand side so she could reach the boxes, and a sign on the roof that said U.S. MAIL, and she put a hundred and four miles on it every day. She had the face her grandmother had had, round and brown and unbothered, a face that did not frown at anybody. People on the route said she knew every dog on it by name. People said she would carry a pound cake to your sister three miles up the road if you asked her, though it was against the rules, and would not take a dollar for it, which was also against the rules, in the other direction.

Dwight's box stood at the head of the turn row, on a cedar post, where the field met the road. On the second morning of the clearing he was down there with the chainsaw, cutting up a sweetgum he had dragged out, and the white Jeep stopped at the box, and Leanna Oxendine leaned out the right-hand window with the mail in her hand.

What you doing, Mr. Dwight?

He let the saw idle. He did not much feel like talking to the government, and it was plain enough what he was doing.

Clearing, he said.

She nodded. She put the mail in the box and put the flag down and drove on.

II. Every Day

The Prospect route · April to July

The next morning he was on the Kubota with the box blade, pushing the burn piles flat, and the Jeep stopped at the box.

What you doing, Mr. Dwight?

Clearing.

She nodded and drove on. At the end of the month he had the disk on, breaking up the old ground, and the cattle egrets had come in from somewhere behind the tractor to pick at the grubs the way they used to come behind his daddy's, and she stopped.

What you doing?

Disking.

She drove on. In April he spread lime and pulled the hipper and made his beds. Bedding up, he told her. In May, with the soil warm enough to hold your hand in, he borrowed his cousin Lonnie's planter and put in Deltapine cotton, forty acres of it, eight days of planting with the radio on, and the Jeep stopped every one of those days at ten-forty, and every day she leaned out the window and asked him what he was doing, and every day he told her, and she nodded and drove on.

It got so that he looked for her. He did not like that he did, and he did. He would be at the far end of the field and see the white Jeep coming slow down the Prospect road, stopping and starting at every box, and he would turn the tractor and work his way up toward the turn row so as to be near enough to hear. He did not know what she wanted. She never wanted anything. She asked, and he answered, and she put the mail in the box and went on, and that was all, and somehow by the end of May it had become a part of the day the way the heat was a part of it.

In June the cotton was up and the pigweed came up with it.

It was the Palmer pigweed, the kind the spray did not kill anymore. The man from the extension office in Lumberton had warned everybody about it at the winter meeting. It could grow two inches in a day. One plant left standing could put out a hundred thousand seeds and ruin a field for ten years. There was nothing to do but go through it with a hoe, row by row, and pull what the hoe missed by hand, and he could not pay a crew, so he and Tyrese and Glenda's nephew went through the forty acres with hoes in the June heat, the way his granddaddy had chopped cotton before there was a spray of any kind, and came in at night with their hands too sore to close.

What you doing, Mr. Dwight?

Chopping. He leaned on the hoe. He was soaked through. What's it look like I'm doing?

She did not answer that. She looked past him down the rows toward the bottom of the field, where the ground fell off a little toward the swamp, where the gum trees stood in the black water with their knees out, and she looked at it for a while, longer than she had looked at anything else, and then she put the mail in the box and put the flag down and went on.

He thought about that look all afternoon, and could not have said what it was.

•••

It was Homecoming week in July that he said it. Pembroke was full of people who had come home from Baltimore and Detroit and Greensboro, and the parade had gone down Main Street on the Saturday with the floats and the Miss Lumbee and the church choirs on flatbed trailers and the old men in the convertibles waving, and Glenda had gone and taken the grandbabies, and Dwight had not gone, because he had cotton to look at. It was squaring up good. The blooms had come out white in the morning and gone pink by evening and fallen, the way cotton blooms do, and behind every one there was a little green boll the size of the end of your thumb.

The Jeep stopped at the box. He walked up the turn row to it before she could ask.

Leanna. You been coming by here since the day I cleared this field.

That's my route, Mr. Dwight.

I know it's your route. He put his hand on the hot hood of the Jeep and took it off again. What I'm saying. If this crop makes, you're in it. Half of what clears after the gin and the note. I won't sell a bale of it without you getting yours. You're my partner. I mean it.

She laughed. It was her grandmother's laugh too, low and not unkind.

Mr. Dwight, I work for the federal government. I can't take anything worth more than twenty dollars. They'll fire me.

Then I'll owe it to you.

All right, she said. You owe it to me.

She put the mail in the box. She looked down the field toward the bottom again, the same long look, and drove on.

III. Watching

August and September

All August he watched it.

He watched it the way his granddaddy had watched tobacco curing in the barns, all night, with a chair and a lantern, getting up every hour to look at the heat. He walked the rows in the morning before the dew was off and in the evening after the heat broke and counted bolls on a stalk, and pulled one off and cut it open with his pocketknife to look at the locks inside, wet and white and tight, and figured. He figured nine hundred pounds of lint to the acre, then a thousand. He figured it at sixty-eight cents. He did the arithmetic so many times he did not need the receipt anymore. In the evenings the deer came out of the swamp to browse the leaves at the bottom of the field, does and yearlings, and he sat in a lawn chair at the edge of the turn row with his daddy's .30-30 across his knees and did not shoot them, only sat, so they could see him, and they lifted their heads and looked at him and went back in the trees.

What you doing, Mr. Dwight?

Watching it.

Leanna nodded.

On the porch on Sunday evenings his brother-in-law Harold came by and they sat and talked the way men talk on porches in Robeson County, about who was sick and who had died and whose boy was in jail, and about the tribe and the federal recognition bill that had been going to pass in Congress every year of their lives and never had, and about the Klan rally at Hayes Pond in January of 1958, which Harold had been at, eleven years old, standing at the back with his daddy's shotgun that was not loaded, when five hundred Indians came out of the dark around that cornfield and put out the one light the Klan had and ran them off into the swamp, and two Indian boys carried the Klan's banner home with them like a deer. Harold told that one every few Sundays. Dwight had been three. He liked to hear it anyway.

That summer Dwight talked about cotton.

You count your chickens, said Harold.

I ain't counting chickens. I'm counting bolls. A boll's a boll.

Well, said Harold, who went to the same church as Glenda. The Lord giveth.

The Lord ain't been down there with a hoe, said Dwight.

•••

The bolls began to open in the second week of September. They cracked along their seams and the locks swelled out, white, one here and one there across the field, and then all of them, until from the porch in the early morning the whole forty acres looked like a sheet left out on the grass. He sprayed the defoliant on the twentieth and the leaves curled and browned and fell and left the white standing on the bare stalks. People slowed down on the Prospect road to look at it. A man he did not know stopped his truck and got out and took a picture of it with his phone.

On the twenty-eighth a storm nobody had forecast stalled over the county and put down ten inches of rain in a day, and the swamp came up out of the trees and into the bottom of the field, a foot deep, black water standing between the rows of white. The Lumber River went over its banks in Lumberton. It went down again, slowly, over the next week, and left a line of dirt on the lowest stalks, and the cotton above the line was fine. Dwight walked the bottom in his boots every evening and watched the water go down. It was going down. The river was still high, and the swamp was still high, and the ground was full, but it was going down.

Leanna stopped at the box on the Monday after.

What you doing, Mr. Dwight?

Getting ready to pick it. He had the custom picker booked, a big John Deere that made the round yellow modules, for Monday the tenth. He had paid the man half in advance. Monday week. You come see it. You come see your half.

She looked down the field toward the swamp for a long time.

I'll be by, she said.

IV. Water

The weekend of October 8

The hurricane had come up from the islands all that week, and the television followed it the way it always did, the lady on Channel 6 standing in front of the map with the cone on it, and the cone moved east, and east, and by Friday they said it would run up the coast of South Carolina and turn out to sea, and Robeson County would get a soaking and some wind. Dwight sat in front of the television with his boots on. Glenda filled the bathtub and the big pots with water the way you do and put the flashlights on the kitchen table and went to bed.

The rain started Saturday morning before light and did not stop. It did not rain the way it rains in a summer storm, in bursts, with the sun out between. It came down all at once and steady, so heavy it hissed on the carport roof, so heavy you could not see the barns from the kitchen window, all day, and the wind came up behind it in the afternoon and blew it sideways, and went on blowing. The power went at four. At dark Dwight put on his rain suit and went out with the big flashlight and stood at the head of the turn row, and the light went out over the field and showed him nothing but rain going sideways through it, silver, and somewhere down below him, under the noise of the wind, a sound he had never heard that field make before, which was the sound of moving water.

He stood there until Glenda came out on the carport in her housecoat and called him in.

The rain stopped some time before morning. When he woke on Sunday the sky was high and blue and washed and the wind was still blowing hard out of the northwest, cool, the first cool day of the fall, and it was so quiet without the power that he could hear the swamp. He went out in his sock feet without his coffee.

The field was gone.

The swamp had come up out of the trees in the night and taken the bottom two-thirds of it, and the water was not black now. It was brown, the brown of the river, moving, with foam on it and whole limbs turning slowly in it, and it stood among the stalks to the height of a man's waist. Where the field still showed above the water at the top, near the road, the wind had gone through it. It had beaten the stalks down flat in the rows, all lying the same way, east, like hair, and stripped them. The cotton was out of the bolls. It was in the mud between the rows in grey wads, beaten in. It was strung along the fence wire by the road in long dirty streamers. It was caught in the ditch, and in the broomsedge, and in the branches of the sweetgums he had not cleared, hanging there like rags. On the bare stalks the empty bolls stood open with nothing in them, brown and curled, like hands.

It looked like a field that somebody had already picked. It looked like the field had been swept.

He stood in the wet grass at the head of the turn row in his sock feet until Glenda came out and took him by the arm.

•••

The river came up all that day and the next, and by Monday it stood higher at Lumberton than anybody had ever measured it. It went over Interstate 95 and closed it. It came into the water treatment plant and the whole town went dry, twenty thousand people, and trucks came in with bottled water and the churches opened their fellowship halls. It went into South Lumberton and West Lumberton to the eaves of the houses, and people were taken out of their windows in boats, and the people who had boats went in from Pembroke and Prospect and Saddletree in their own johnboats with their own outboards, Indian and Black and white, and carried out whoever was on the roofs. Glenda's sister and her husband came out of their house in South Lumberton in a deer hunter's johnboat with the dog and a garbage bag of clothes and moved into the spare room. The schools closed and did not open again for three weeks. The power came back on Wednesday.

Dwight went to bed on Sunday afternoon and did not get up.

He did not say he was sick. He lay on his side facing the wall with the blinds down, in his clothes, and when Glenda came in he did not turn over. He would not eat. She made him chicken and pastry on the gas stove the way his mother used to make it, with the pastry rolled thin and cut in strips, and set it on the nightstand, and it went cold. On Tuesday Harold came and sat in the chair by the bed for an hour and talked about the river and about the people up and down the state who had drowned in their cars, and Dwight did not answer him, and Harold went out to the kitchen and told Glenda he had seen this before, in men, and it was not good.

What Dwight thought about, lying there, was the girl in the Jeep.

He thought: she has been coming by since the first day. Since I cleared it. Every day. Clearing, disking, bedding, planting, chopping, watching. And I told her, half. I told her I wouldn't sell a bale without her getting hers. Partner. And now there is not one bale. There is not one pound. I promised it and I wanted it and it is in the mud. And he could not tell, lying there, whether what was eating him was the money, or the promise, or the wanting, or something under all three of them that had no name at all, but he could not get up from it, and he could not eat around it.

V. The Kitchen

Thursday, October 13

The mail started running again on Thursday on the roads that were open. The Jeep came down the Prospect road at ten-forty, slow, going around the places where the shoulder had washed out, and it did not stop at the box on the cedar post. It turned in at the turn row and came up the drive and stopped under the carport, and Leanna Oxendine got out of the right-hand side with a bundle of mail held together with a rubber band and knocked on the kitchen door.

Glenda opened it. Her sister was behind her at the stove.

Dwight heard the voice from the back room. He had not been out of the bed in four days, but he heard it, and he knew it, and he said so loud they heard it in the kitchen, the first thing he had said since Sunday.

That's my partner. Bring her in. Give her a seat. Give her some coffee.

They gave her a seat at the kitchen table, in his chair, at the end. They gave her coffee with sugar in it. She sat with the bundle of mail in front of her and her cap on the table and drank some of it.

Where's he at?

He's in the bed, said Glenda. He's been in the bed since Sunday.

Go get him, said Leanna Oxendine.

Glenda went back. In a little while Dwight came out of the hall in his sock feet and the clothes he had been lying in for four days, with his hair flat on one side, and sat down at the side of the table, on the bench, where the grandchildren sat.

What's the matter, Mr. Dwight?

He looked at his hands on the oilcloth.

You know what's the matter. You come by here since the day I cleared it. Every day. And I told you, if it makes, you're my partner. I told you half. I wanted to give it to you. He stopped. I wanted it. I wanted it so bad. And it didn't make. And it's like something's got hold of me. I can't eat. Glenda'll tell you. I can't eat a thing.

Leanna turned her coffee cup around on the oilcloth.

You know where that comes from? What's got hold of you?

No, he said. I don't know. But you know. I believe you do.

She was quiet a while. Glenda's sister had turned off the stove. Out the window, past the carport, you could see the top of the field, the flat stalks, the grey cotton in the mud, the brown water still standing at the bottom with the sky in it.

It comes from wanting, she said. That's all. The grief comes from it, and the being scared comes from it. You wanted that cotton so bad you lost it before it ever came up out of the ground. You were sitting out there at night with a rifle guarding it from the deer. You were scared every day of your life since March, Mr. Dwight. I saw it on you every morning at that box. You were grieving it before it ever went in the water.

He looked at her.

Didn't nobody ever tell you that? Grandmama used to make me say it. Every time I cried over something I couldn't have. And she said it to him across the table, plain, the way you would read somebody an address off an envelope.

Let your conversation be without covetousness; and be content with such things as ye have: for he hath said, I will never leave thee, nor forsake thee. So that we may boldly say, The Lord is my helper, and I will not fear what man shall do unto me.

— Hebrews 13:5–6

That's the church talking, said Dwight. But he did not say it the way he had said it to the preacher.

Maybe. Leanna drank the rest of her coffee. I'm just telling you what I know. You can't lose what you're not holding on to. You can't be scared for it either.

Then she did a thing that Glenda told about for years afterward, at church and at the beauty shop and at Homecoming. She reached across the table and took the bundle of mail out from under the rubber band, and went through it, and took out the envelope from the crop insurance company and the envelope from the Farm Service Agency and the light bill, and laid them face down on the oilcloth in front of him, one, two, three, the way you would deal cards, and put her hand flat on top of them, and left it there a minute. Then she took her cap and put it on and went out the kitchen door and got in the right-hand side of the Jeep and backed it down the drive.

Dwight sat at the table a long time with his hands on either side of the three envelopes.

Glenda said that what happened next she could not describe, and she had seen people get saved at the altar since she was a girl, and it was not like that. Nobody shouted. He did not cry. He only sat there, and something in his shoulders let go, she said, the way a fist lets go when you sleep, and his face got younger. She said it was like watching somebody set down a sack of feed he had been carrying so long he forgot it was on him.

Then he looked up at her.

Is there any of that chicken and pastry?

VI. The Flag

November

The insurance paid some, the way insurance does. The Farm Service Agency stretched out the note. The government in Raleigh talked about disaster money for farmers and some of it came the next year, and some of it never did. The water went down out of the field by the end of October and left a line of river mud on everything, a foot up the stalks, on the fence posts, on the cedar post that held the mailbox, and a smell that hung in the bottom for weeks.

Dwight went down and walked it in his boots. He did not walk it the way he had in August. He walked it slowly, the way you would walk somebody else's field, and looked at what the river had done, which was a great deal, and what it had not done, which was also a great deal. It had not taken the ground. The ground was still there, black, heavy with silt the river had brought down from Hoke County and Scotland County and laid on it for free. It had not taken the barns. It had not taken the house, or Glenda, or the grandbabies, or Harold, or the deer, which came out of the swamp at evening as if nothing had happened and browsed the green that was coming up through the mud between the rows.

The cotton was still in the fence. All along the wire by the road the streamers of it hung where the wind had put them, grey from the river, drying white again in the sun, stirring when a truck went by. Nobody had pulled it down. He had meant to. He had stopped meaning to.

He went up the turn row to the box on the cedar post on a Tuesday morning at the end of the month, with frost still in the shade of the ditch. There was nothing to mail. He opened the box and looked inside it and shut it again, and then he reached around and put up the little red flag on the side, the way you do when you want the carrier to stop, and stood there next to it with his hands in his jacket.

At ten-forty the white Jeep came down the Prospect road, slow, stopping and starting, and saw the flag, and stopped.

Leanna Oxendine leaned out of the right-hand window and looked in the empty box, and looked at him.

What you doing, Mr. Dwight?

Standing here, he said.

She nodded. She put the mail in the box, and reached out and put the flag back down, and drove on up the road toward Pembroke, and he stood a while longer by the cedar post with the cotton moving on the wire beside him in the cold wind, white, and the sun coming up over the swamp.

Background

The verse

From greed comes grief, from greed comes fear; he who is free from greed knows neither grief nor fear.Dhammapada 216, tr. F. Max Müller (1881)

In the commentary, a Brahmin farmer who holds wrong views is clearing his field by the river when the Buddha comes by and asks what he is doing. The farmer answers and the Buddha goes on. He comes back day after day, through the plowing, sowing, weeding and guarding, and asks the same question each time. At last the farmer promises him a share: if the crop prospers, he will not eat without giving to him, and the Buddha will be his partner. The night before the reaping, a great storm beats the crop flat, and the field looks as if it had been cut clean. The farmer takes to his bed and will not eat. The Buddha comes to his door, has him called out, and asks what is wrong; the farmer says the desire of his heart has not been fulfilled. The Buddha asks whether he knows where his grief comes from, and the farmer answers that he does not, but the Buddha knows. Grief and fear, the Buddha says, both come from craving. Robeson County, 2016

The Lumbee take their name from the Lumber River, and most of the tribe's members live in Robeson County, around Pembroke, Prospect and the other communities "on the swamp." North Carolina recognized the tribe in 1885. The Lumbee Act of 1956 named them Indians but withheld federal recognition, and the tribe pursued it in Congress for decades, until the Lumbee Fairness Act was signed in December 2025. Lumbee Homecoming has brought people home to Pembroke every summer since 1968. At the Battle of Hayes Pond in January 1958, several hundred Lumbee broke up a Ku Klux Klan rally near Maxton; two of them carried off the Klan's banner. Like much of eastern North Carolina, Robeson County farmed tobacco under a federal quota until the 2004 buyout, which paid quota holders over ten years. By 2016 cotton growers across the South were fighting Palmer amaranth, a pigweed that had become resistant to glyphosate, and many went back to chopping it by hand.

On September 28, 2016, a stalled storm dropped about ten inches of rain on Robeson and neighboring counties and pushed the Lumber River out of its banks. Ten days later, on October 8, Hurricane Matthew came ashore in South Carolina and dumped another ten to fourteen inches on the same ground. The Lumber River at Lumberton rose to a record of about 24 feet, more than three feet above the old mark. Floodwater shut down sections of Interstate 95 (the U.S. Geological Survey photographed the crossing under water) and reached Lumberton's water treatment plant, leaving more than 22,000 people without running water. Robeson County schools stayed closed until October 31. Twenty-six people died in North Carolina, and soybeans, sweet potatoes, peanuts and cotton were all caught in the field at the start of harvest.
