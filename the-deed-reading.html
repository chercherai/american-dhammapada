The Deed Reading

Be sure your sin will find you out.
— Numbers 32:23

I heard it like this, in Boley.

I. The Porch

Boley, Okfuskee County, Oklahoma · summer 1913

Isaac Grayson read papers for nothing. That was the whole of it, at first.

He had hung out his shingle on Main Street in the fall of 1911, a narrow frame office between the barbershop and the Farmers' Gin, with a back room where he slept on a cot. He was twenty-seven. His grandmother had been owned by a Creek family on the Arkansas River before the war, and after the war she was a Creek citizen with a Creek name, and when the Dawes men came with their books, Isaac and every one of his brothers and sisters had been enrolled as Creek Freedmen and allotted a hundred and sixty acres apiece. He had sold forty of his own to go to school. He had read law in a white lawyer's office in Muskogee, sweeping it out in the mornings, and passed the bar on the second try, and come home.

He did not talk much. People said he talked in the courtroom like a Baptist preacher and everywhere else like a man who was paying for every word by the pound.

In the evenings, when the heat let go a little, he carried his lamp and a kitchen chair out onto the porch of the office and sat there, and anybody who had a paper could bring it. A lease. A deed. A guardian's report from the county court. A letter from a man in Tulsa who wanted to buy your mineral rights for a dollar an acre. He would hold the paper to the lamp and read it to you, every word, out loud, and tell you what it said, and then tell you what it meant, which was usually something else. He did not charge a nickel. When people asked him why, he said, It's your land.

That was all. But by the summer of 1913 the line on his porch went down the steps and along the board sidewalk past the barbershop most nights, farmers in from the bottoms still in their overalls, widows, young men who had just turned twenty-one and come into what was left of their allotments, old Creek-speaking men and women who had been born in the Nation before there was any Oklahoma, standing in the dusk waiting their turn, talking low, fanning themselves with their papers.

And people stopped signing things.

That was the trouble. Boley was the finest town of colored people in the world, the paper said so every week. It had two banks and three gins and an ice plant and its own electric light, and two colleges, and a brand-new Masonic Temple, the tallest building between Okmulgee and Oklahoma City, people said. Booker T. Washington had come and made a speech. But Boley sat in the middle of a great deal of land that belonged to Creek Freedmen, and in 1908 Congress had taken the restrictions off Freedman land, so that it could be sold or leased or mortgaged like anybody's, and then they found oil at Cushing, and since then a man who knew how to get an old woman's mark on the right line of the right paper could do very well indeed.

II. The Land Men

Pettiford's Real Estate & Loan, Main Street · August

Cyrus Pettiford had done very well indeed. He was a notary public and an agent for two lease companies in Okmulgee and a broker of farm loans, and he had sat in the front pew of the Baptist church for six years, and the county judge at Okemah had made him guardian of the allotments of eleven minor children, whose rents he collected and whose reports he filed, and nobody had ever in six years read one of those reports out loud. His partner was Lyle Ransom, who wrote letters for people who could not write, and read them their letters back for a dime, and had a way of reading a letter from a lease company so that it said what Pettiford needed it to say.

They were not having a good summer. Since spring Pettiford had not closed one lease worth closing. Widows who had signed whatever he put in front of them for six years were coming into his office, sitting down, folding their hands, and saying, I believe I'll take that home and study on it. Which meant they would take it to the porch. Two of his wards' mothers had gone to the county court about the rents. Lyle Ransom had not read a letter for anybody since June. Men who used to tip their hats to Pettiford in the street now said Evening and kept walking, and some nights you could stand at Pettiford's window and look down Main Street and see the lamp on Grayson's porch and the line going down the steps and nobody at all on yours.

He's got this whole town reading, Ransom said.

He's got this whole town thinking it's smarter than the men who built it, said Pettiford. Who can we use?

They thought of Ora Lee Waters at about the same moment.

She was nineteen and the best-looking girl in Okfuskee County, everybody said so, tall and dark and quick, with a laugh you could hear across the street. Her mother was a Creek Freedwoman from out by Castle and her father was dead. Ora Lee had been one of Pettiford's wards until her eighteenth birthday, when the law said a girl came of age, and she had come into her hundred and sixty acres and found there was a mortgage on it with her own mark at the bottom, from when she was sixteen, that she did not remember making. She owed Pettiford three hundred and ten dollars. She sang in the choir. She also went to the Saturday night dances out on the river road, which the choir did not know about, and Pettiford did.

She came into the office the next afternoon to pay her interest. Pettiford wrote the receipt and did not say a word to her. Ransom sat in the corner with his newspaper and did not look up.

Mr. Pettiford, did I do something to you?

Nothing.

Mr. Pettiford. Somebody done you wrong?

He put the pen down. Ora Lee, look out that window. You see that lamp? That man is taking the bread out of my mouth and yours. He's turning this town against the men who built it. Every lease I lose is money that should have come to this town.

What do you want me to do about it?

You're a good-looking girl, said Pettiford. Folks talk about a good-looking girl. Give them something to talk about. And that paper on your land, he said, and took it out of the drawer and laid it on the desk between them, that paper could get lost.

Ora Lee looked at the paper a long time. Then she said, All right.

III. The Flowers

Main Street · August and September

Every evening after that, when the lamp went out on the porch and the last of the line was walking home, Ora Lee Waters came down Main Street the other way, toward the gin, in her Sunday dress. She carried a fruit jar of zinnias from her mother's yard, and a bottle of bay rum from the drugstore, and a paper sack of oranges from the depot, and she walked slow, past the barbershop where the men sat out on the bench, past the pool hall, past the women on the porches.

Where you going this late, Ora Lee?

Going to sit up with the lawyer, she said. I sit up with him most every night.

She went around behind the gin in the dark and through the cotton wagons and out the other side to Lyle Ransom's sister's house, and slept there. In the morning she came back up Main Street the same way, slow, with her hair half down, and when the women at the pump asked her where she had been all night she yawned and said, Where you think? I been with Lawyer Grayson. He don't let a girl get much sleep. And she laughed her laugh that you could hear across the street.

Every morning Isaac Grayson found a jar of zinnias on his front step, or a bottle of bay rum, or oranges, and did not know where they came from, and did not ask, and carried them around back and threw them on the heap behind the gin where the gin threw its trash, burrs and lint and broken bale ties and dirt, so that by September there was a slope of withered zinnias down one side of the heap, red and orange and dried to paper.

Only once did she come to the porch in daylight. It was a hot evening at the end of September, and she stood in the line like anybody, behind a farmer from the bottoms and in front of two old women from Clearview, with her hands empty, and when her turn came she sat down in the kitchen chair across from him under the lamp. The people in the line went very quiet. She had never been that close to him. He was younger than she had thought, and tired, with ink on his second finger.

You don't have a paper, he said.

No. She looked at the lamp and not at him. Say a girl had a paper. Say there was a mark on it she don't remember making. What would you read her?

Whatever it said.

Could a paper like that be wrong?

Papers are wrong all the time, said Isaac Grayson. Bring it and see.

She sat a moment longer with her hands in her lap. Then she laughed, the laugh you could hear across the street, and got up and went off down the sidewalk, and that night the jar of zinnias was on his step as usual.

The talk went the way talk goes. Some people did not believe it. Most people did not want to, and believed it anyway. A few of the widows stopped coming to the porch. A deacon came by and stood in the door of the office with his hat on and said there were some things a church could not have, and went away without waiting for an answer. Pettiford closed two leases in September.

Then, on the second Saturday of October, he paid two men from the Cushing field forty dollars apiece.

IV. The Heap

Behind the Farmers' Gin · October

On Monday morning Cyrus Pettiford walked into the town marshal's office with his hat in his hand and his face drawn down long, and Lyle Ransom behind him.

Marshal, Ora Lee Waters ain't come home. Her mother's out of her mind. Since Saturday night.

Who'd she go with?

Pettiford looked at the floor like a man who does not want to say a thing. Marshal, you know where she's been going nights. The whole town knows. What happened to her there, I couldn't say.

The marshal was a big quiet man named Hollis Lee who had been a sergeant in the Tenth Cavalry and did not like Pettiford and did not like Grayson much either, because he did not like lawyers. He looked at Pettiford a while.

Then go look, he said.

They went to look. They took a dozen men with them, men from Pettiford's church and Ransom's lodge, and they went straight down Main Street to the gin, and around behind it, and Pettiford stood back with his hands folded while the others poked at the heap with cotton hooks, and it was Ransom who called out.

She was lying in the zinnias. Somebody had hit her once on the side of the head and thrown her up on the heap like a sack, and she had rolled a little way down the slope of dead flowers, and her Sunday dress was spread out on them, and there were burrs in her hair.

They took the door off the gin office and carried her on it up the middle of Main Street, eight men, at noon, with the whole town coming out of the stores. Pettiford walked in front. When they came opposite the lawyer's office he stopped them and turned around to the crowd, and said it loud.

Look here. Look what they did. That man and his people. He couldn't have it known what he'd been doing nights, so they put her out of the way and threw her on his trash. Look at her. Look at her, Boley.

Isaac Grayson stood in his doorway and looked. He did not say anything at all.

The marshal had the door set on two sawhorses on the steps of the Masonic Temple, in the shade, with a sheet over it and two men to watch, and sent a boy on a mule for the girl's mother, and then he went into his office and shut the door and sat a long time with his head in his hands. Everybody in Boley knew what he was thinking about. Two years before, a colored woman and her boy had been taken out of the county jail at Okemah at midnight and hanged from the bridge over the North Canadian, and in the morning hundreds of white people had come out to look, and a man had made photographs. If the county got hold of this, a dead girl and a colored lawyer and a story like this one, nobody knew what the county would do. Not to the lawyer. To the town.

V. What Shall Be Said

Boley · that week

By dark the whole town had it. By the next day the whole county. Pettiford's people went from porch to porch, and to the barbershop, and the gins, and the two colleges, and out into the bottoms on horseback, saying it. See what they did. See what the lawyer's people did. The ones who believed it said it to the ones who didn't, and after a while the ones who didn't stopped saying anything.

It fell on the people who had stood in the line. Not on Isaac. On them. A widow who had taken her lease to the porch had a cup of coffee thrown on her in the bank. A young man who had been read his own guardian's report out loud, and had gone to the county court about it, was knocked down outside the pool hall by two of Pettiford's lodge brothers and kicked. Old Aunt Sukey Grant, who had been born in the Creek Nation in 1838 and could not read one word, was spat at on the board sidewalk by a girl of fourteen. Children chanted it in the schoolyard. The people from the line stopped going to the post office. They stopped going to church.

On the Wednesday night seven or eight of them came to the back door of the office, because they did not dare come to the front any more, and stood in the back room among the law books, and asked him what they should do. Should they go to the county? Should they get guns? Should they leave? Some of them were crying.

Isaac Grayson had not said twenty words since Monday. He sat on the edge of his cot with his elbows on his knees.

Don't fight anybody, he said. Don't argue. Don't explain. You weren't there and you don't know, and neither do they. When they say it to you, you say this back to them. Only this. Then walk on.

And he said it to them, twice, so they would have it, the way he would read them a paper.

A false witness shall not be unpunished, and he that speaketh lies shall perish.

— Proverbs 19:9

But if ye will not do so, behold, ye have sinned against the LORD: and be sure your sin will find you out.

— Numbers 32:23

They did it. That was the thing people remembered afterward, even people who had chanted in the schoolyard. When the widow in the bank was called a whore's friend to her face, she said, He that speaketh lies shall perish, and finished her business and walked out. When the boys outside the pool hall started on old Aunt Sukey Grant again, she stopped on the sidewalk with her cane and turned around, and said in her old Creek-country voice, slow, so they would hear every word, Be sure your sin will find you out, and the boys went quiet and went inside. It was not much. It was a few words, and everybody in Boley already knew them. But you could not fight it and you could not argue with it, and after a few days, people who said the thing on the sidewalk began to hear those words before they had even finished saying it, and some of them stopped saying it.

VI. Choc Beer

The bottoms below Boley · Saturday night

The marshal had not been idle. On the Tuesday he had sent for his deputy, a young man named Elias Moore who had been raised in the bottoms and knew every still and choc joint between Boley and the Canadian, and told him, Go find out if anybody else did this.

Oklahoma had been dry since statehood. That did not stop anybody. Down in the river bottoms below town there was a woman named Mattie who brewed choc beer in a washtub out of barley and hops and anything else that would work, the beer people called Choctaw beer from the old Territory days, and sold it out of her kitchen on Saturday nights by the dipper, and the oil field men came over from the Cushing field on payday because in Boley nobody looked twice at a colored man with money.

Two of them were there that Saturday. They had been there the Saturday before, too, and had been spending free. Elias Moore sat in the corner of Mattie's kitchen with a dipper he did not drink from and listened to them get drunk.

It took till midnight. Then the bigger one wanted the other one to pay for the round, and the other one said he had paid for the last three, and the bigger one said something about who had done the work, and the other one stood up and knocked his chair over.

Who did the work? Who did the work? You hit her one time, one time, and throwed her up on the lawyer's flowers, and you been drinking her forty dollars for a week, and you ask me who did the work?

The kitchen went quiet. Mattie put down her dipper.

Hush, said the bigger one. You hush. Let it be. Let it be.

Elias Moore stood up in the corner with his pistol out and said, No. I don't believe we'll let it be.

VII. The Temple Steps

Boley · Monday

The two men told it all in the marshal's office before daylight on Sunday. The forty dollars apiece. Who paid it. The pick handle. The heap. They had not even known her name.

On Monday morning Hollis Lee walked down Main Street with Elias Moore beside him and a shotgun under his arm and went into Pettiford's Real Estate & Loan and came out with Cyrus Pettiford, and then went three doors down and came out with Lyle Ransom, and walked them both up the middle of the street to the steps of the Masonic Temple, where the girl had lain under the sheet all week until her mother took her home to Castle to bury her. By then the whole town was following.

He did not take them to Okemah. Not yet. He stood them on the Temple steps in front of everybody in Boley, and the mayor came out of the bank and stood beside him, and the marshal said, not loud, the way a sergeant says a thing he is only going to say once:

You carried her up this street and told them who did it. Now you tell them.

Pettiford looked at the crowd. He was a man who had talked his way out of a great many things. He opened his mouth, and the first thing that came out of the crowd at him, before he could say a word, was an old woman's voice from down in front, slow, in a Creek-country accent.

Be sure your sin will find you out.

Then somebody else said it. Then a great many people, not loud, not all together, the widows and the farmers and the young men from the line, and some of the very ones who had said the other thing all week, until it was going through the crowd on Main Street like a wind going through cotton.

Pettiford said it. He said they had paid the men. He said they had done it to bring disgrace on Lawyer Grayson because he was ruining their business. He said there was no fault in Grayson, that Grayson had never laid eyes on the girl at night in his life, and that none of the people from the line had any part in it. The marshal made him say it twice, so the back of the crowd could hear. Then Ransom said it. Then the marshal put them in a wagon and drove them to the county jail at Okemah himself, with the shotgun across his knees, and at the trial in the spring they got life at McAlester, all four.

The lamp was back on the porch the next evening. The line went down the steps and along the board sidewalk past the barbershop and the pool hall and around the corner, longer than it had ever been, and the people in it stood in the dusk with their papers and did not talk much.

The last one in line, near ten o'clock, was a woman from out by Castle in a black dress, with a paper folded small in her hand. She did not sit in the chair. She held the paper out to him and he took it and unfolded it under the lamp. It was a patent from the Creek Nation, signed by the Principal Chief, for a hundred and sixty acres, and pinned to the back of it was the mortgage, which the marshal had found in Pettiford's desk.

That's hers, the woman said. Read it to me.

Isaac Grayson unpinned the mortgage and laid it face down on the porch boards, and held the patent up to the light, and read it to her slowly, every word, the way he read everybody's, down to the description of the land: the quarter section, the township, the range, the creek that ran along the south line of it. And the name that was written in at the top, which was Ora Lee Waters, and still was.

Background

The verse

He who says what is not, goes to hell; he also who, having done a thing, says I have not done it. After death both are equal, they are men with evil deeds in the next world.Dhammapada 306, tr. F. Max Müller (1881)

In the commentary, rival ascetics who have lost their followers and their gifts to the Buddha persuade a beautiful wandering nun, Sundarī, to ruin his name. Every evening she walks toward his monastery with garlands and perfumes, telling people she spends her nights with him, and every morning she walks back the same way. After some days the rivals pay men to kill her and throw her body on the heap of withered garlands near the Buddha's chamber, then report her missing to the king, lead the search, carry her body through the city, and cry out that the monks have murdered her to hide their teacher's sin. The whole city turns on the monks. The Buddha tells them only to answer with a verse about liars and deniers. The king's men find the killers quarrelling drunk over their pay, and the king makes the rivals go through the city proclaiming their own guilt and the Buddha's innocence. The same tale is told in the Udāna and the Jātaka. Read the full commentary story.

Boley, 1913

Boley opened for settlement in 1903 on the allotment of Abigail Barnett, daughter of a Creek Freedman, and was incorporated in 1905. By 1911, the Oklahoma Historical Society records, it had more than four thousand people, two banks, three cotton gins, an electric plant, an ice plant and two colleges, and Booker T. Washington had visited and praised it; its Masonic Temple, finished around 1912, was said to be the tallest building between Okmulgee and Oklahoma City. The Freedmen of the Creek Nation, descendants of people the Creeks had enslaved, had been made Creek citizens by treaty and were allotted land on the Dawes Rolls. In 1908 Congress lifted all restrictions on the sale of Freedman land, adults' and minors' alike, and put minors' land under the county probate courts, where guardians could be appointed to manage it. The discovery of the Cushing oil field in 1912 made such land, and the papers that controlled it, far more valuable.

Oklahoma entered the Union dry in 1907, and home-brewed choc beer was sold on the quiet for decades. Boley was also a town that lived with fear. In May 1911 a crowd of white men took Laura Nelson and her son from the county jail at Okemah and hanged them from a bridge over the North Canadian River; hundreds came out to look, and the town photographer made pictures. Historians have read the photographs as a warning meant for Boley, and rumors that Boley men would retaliate set Okemah's men guarding Main Street. More on the lynching of Laura and L. D. Nelson.
