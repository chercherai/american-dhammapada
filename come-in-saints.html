Walking the American WayCommentary 26.1 · Pasādabahula

Come In, Saints

Wherefore, brethren, giue rather diligence to make your calling and election sure.
— 2 Peter 1:10 (Geneva Bible)

As it was spoken before the church, at Newark on Pesayak River.

I. The Relation

Newark, East New Jersey · the Lord’s day, February 1667/8

The relation of Hopestill Ambler, planter, made before the church at the noon exercise, he desiring to be received into full communion. Taken down as near his own words as the clerk could keep up, the room being cold and the ink thick.

Brethren, I will be short, for I have tried this before and been long and it came to nothing.

I was born in Norfolk and came over a child in the year 1638 with the company that sat down at Quinnipiac, and I have lived all my life since among the people of God, at New Haven and then at Branford, and now here. I have sat under the best preaching in the world. I have seen men and women stand where I stand now and tell how the Lord broke them and bound them up, and the tears ran down my face, and I went home and could find no such thing in myself. Not the breaking and not the binding. Thirty years. I have been a good neighbor, I hope, and paid my rates. I am not a saint. I never could say I was.

But I will tell you what I can say, which is that I have loved the saints. I have loved them more than my meat. When I was a boy at New Haven and a member went by in the street, I would stand and look after him as you would look after a ship going out with all its canvas. I could not help it. I did not know what they had, but I knew it was the one thing worth the having.

You know why we came here. When the King’s charter gave our colony to Connecticut, and the Connecticut way was to make every man a freeman whether he was in covenant or no, and the synod had set up the half-way way of baptizing the children of those who had never owned the covenant themselves, Mr. Pierson said we must go out again into the wilderness and keep the old order or lose it, and we came. We left our houses. I left a house at Branford I built with these hands, the chimney still warm, and the church carried its book in a chest. We came here to make a town where none but members of the churches should be freemen or bear office. A town of saints.

And I came, who was not one.

II. The Noon Table

The house lot nearest the meeting-house · summer and autumn 1667

The Lord gave me the lot nearest the meeting-house. I did not ask for it. It fell to me by the draw, and I took it for a sign of what I was to do, which was to feed people.

Between the morning exercise and the afternoon, the families that live near go home to their dinners, but there are those whose lots lie out toward the great meadows and over the brook, and the young men who have no house yet, and they used to sit in the meeting-house and eat cold bread. So from the first Lord’s day in July I set my table for them. My wife and I fed sixteen. Pottage, and rye-and-Indian bread, and what fish the river gave, and in the fall venison the Indians sold us, and beer.

Brethren, you know how I greeted them. Some of you were among the sixteen. I stood at my door and I said: Come in, saints. Sit down, saints. Saints, will you have more? I said it to every one. I said it to Sergeant Ruggles, who gave his relation at Branford in 1644 and has walked with God ever since, and I said it to the Lacey boy, who is eighteen and who I know for a fact went over to the Dutch at Bergen in August and came back merry. I said it to all of them alike. I could not see what the Lord sees and I did not try. When a man sat down at my table I saw a saint, because I wanted so much for there to be saints, and I could not bear to think the next one in my door was not.

I thought it was a kindness. It was not received as one.

The young men went red. They looked into their pottage. I learned afterward that they said to one another, going back across the lot, Old Ambler thinks we are of the elect. Old Ambler will find out. One of them said it was like being called Captain when you have never been to sea. And the old members, the ones who had given their relations and been received, they did not go red, but they went quiet, and I learned afterward what they said too, which was: He calls us saints and he calls the Lacey boy a saint in the same breath. He has no discerning. One said I made the word cheap.

In October they stopped coming. Not all at once. One Lord’s day there were twelve, then nine, then on the first Lord’s day in November there was no one, and my wife had a pottage for sixteen on the fire, and we sat at the table the two of us and looked at the benches, and I could see through the window into the meeting-house, where they were sitting in their coats eating cold bread off their knees, with the frost on the inside of the glass.

I am not ashamed to tell you I wept. I had thought, if I could not be one, I could at least feed them. And now I could not even do that.

III. Goodwife Bray

Her house by the Watering Place · November 1667

So I went to Goodwife Bray. You all know why. She is the oldest woman in this town, past seventy-nine. She was the midwife at Branford twenty years and at New Haven before that. There is hardly a soul here under thirty that she did not catch in her two hands, and she says she can tell a man’s whole disposition by the way he came. She does not speak in meeting, as women may not, and she does not need to.

I told her the whole of it. She heard me through, shelling beans into her apron. Then she asked me why I said it. I said I did not know. I said, because I love them. Because it is the only thing in the world I want, and I want it so much I see it everywhere.

She said, Go home and set the table.

The next Lord’s day, between the exercises, she put on her hood and walked across the lot to the meeting-house, slow, on her stick, and went in where the sixteen were eating their cold bread, and stood in front of them. Some of you were there. I was not. This is what was told me, and if I have it wrong the brethren who were there will set me right.

She said, Which of you will tell me why you have left Hopestill Ambler’s table for this?

And the young men told her: he calls us saints, and we are not, and it shames us. And the old members told her: he calls us saints, and he calls them saints, and he cannot tell the difference, and it is no honor to be praised by a man who praises everybody.

She said, Did any of you think he was mocking you? No, they said. Did any of you think he was lying? No. Then what is it? And nobody could answer.

Then she told them. She said, The church takes in a member upon the judgment of charity. You know that. You old men know it best of anyone, you who were taken in upon it. Not one of you was seen into by the elders down to the bottom. They heard your relation and judged in charity that the work was true, because only God sees the heart. That is all Hopestill Ambler does. He judges in charity, and the only difference between him and the elders is that he does it all the way. There is no fault in that. I have known him since he was eleven years old, and I tell you that man’s love for the saints has no bottom to it. It is the one thing in him that has no bottom.

Then she turned on the young men. And you. You go red because he calls you what you are not. Well, then. I did not catch you in my hands to see you sit on a bench and eat cold bread because a good man thinks too well of you. Make him right. Do not go red. Go and be it.

And she said the scripture to them, there in the cold meeting-house, the one we all had at Branford from Mr. Pierson in the old Bible.

Wherefore, brethren, giue rather diligence to make your calling and election sure: for if ye doe these things, ye shall neuer fall.

— 2 Peter 1:10 (Geneva Bible)

Then she went home on her stick, and that same noon twelve of them came across the lot to my door, and the pottage was still hot, and I said what I always say. I could not help it. Come in, saints. And the young ones did not go red. They looked at me as though I had set them a task. The Lacey boy said, Yes, Goodman Ambler. We are coming.

IV. The Chimney

The same house lot · January 1667/8

Brethren, I would have stopped there and been content. But the Lord was not done, and this is the part I have never been able to say until now, and I am standing here to say it.

On the eighth night of the eleventh month, the chimney took fire. It was clay and wattle, as most of ours are yet, built in haste last summer, and it went up in the night and took the thatch, and by the time we had the children out the whole house was burning. You came running, all of you, with buckets from the Watering Place. The sixteen came first. They were young and they ran. They threw water till their coats froze on them, and it did no good, and we stood in the snow and watched it go. Everything I had built with these hands since we came over the water from Branford. The second house in six months.

I stood there, and Goodwife Bray came and stood by me, wrapped in a blanket somebody had put round her, and the light of my house was on her face. I said to her, That is twice. The Lord takes everything I make.

She said, Hopestill. Did you think you made it?

And she said the other scripture to me. Not loud. I do not think anybody heard it but me.

For we knowe that if our earthly house of this tabernacle be destroyed, we haue a building giuen of God, that is, an house not made with handes, but eternall in the heauens.

— 2 Corinthians 5:1 (Geneva Bible)

Brethren, I cannot tell you what happened then except that I saw it. I stood in the snow and watched the house go down, the ridgepole and the rafters and the door I had hung, everything I had made, everything Branford had made, everything New Haven made, the whole of our commonwealth with its book in a chest, all of it made with hands and all of it burning, the way it always would, the way it had to. And I saw that the thing I had been looking for in the saints for thirty years was not made. It was not made by them and not by me. It was not in my house and could not burn with it. It stood where it had always stood. It was the only thing on that lot that night that was not on fire.

And I was not afraid. That is the sum of what I have to tell. I had always been afraid. I had been afraid I was not one of them. And standing there with nothing, I did not care any more whether I was one of them. I only saw it, and was glad of it, and wanted nothing else.

The sixteen built me a house in nine days. You know that too. They cut the timber on the hill and carried it on their shoulders across the ice. They would not let me lift a stick. When it was done I stood at the new door and I said what I have always said, and I will say it as long as I live: Come in, saints.

That is my relation. I cannot say I am a saint. I can say I have seen the house not made with hands, and that I am not afraid. The brethren will judge.

V. The Vote

The clerk’s note · the same day

The brethren being asked whether they were satisfied, Sergeant Ruggles rose and said he had heard many relations and few so short, and that the judgment of charity had never been easier to give, and he for one was satisfied. The rest held up their hands. Hopestill Ambler was received into full communion. When the vote was declared he said nothing, but sat down, and was seen to be laughing without noise.

Noted also: that this winter, since November, nine of the young men who ate at Ambler’s table have come before the elders desiring to give their relations, of whom three have been heard and received, the Lacey boy among them. The others are in the way.

Noted also, at Goodwife Bray’s request, though it is not the church’s business: that when the exercise was over and the people went out, Ambler stood at his new door in the snow, the pottage being hot, and called to the whole congregation coming down the steps, members and half-way and the children and the two Dutchmen who had come over from Bergen to hear the preaching, Come in, saints, and a great many more than sixteen went in, and the door was left open, and nobody went red.

Background

The verse

Stop the stream valiantly, drive away the desires, O Brahmana! When you have understood the destruction of all that was made, you will understand that which was not made.Dhammapada 383, tr. F. Max Müller (1881)

In the commentary, a brahmin named Pasādabahula, “Great Confidence,” hears the Buddha and from then on feeds sixteen monks at his house, greeting every one of them as an arahat, a fully awakened one. The monks who are not yet awakened think he believes there are arahats among them; the ones who are think he cannot tell; all of them grow uncomfortable and stop coming. The brahmin, grieved, goes to the Buddha, who tells the monks that the greeting is only an expression of confidence, in which no fault can be found, that the brahmin’s love for arahats is boundless, and that they should therefore cut off the stream of craving and settle for nothing less. Newark, 1666–1668

The New Haven Colony allowed only church members to hold civic rights. When the royal charter of 1662 folded it into Connecticut, which had no such rule, and as the churches debated the Half-Way Covenant of 1662, which let the children of baptized but unconverted parents be baptized in turn, the minister of Branford, Abraham Pierson, who disapproved of the covenant, led his people away. With settlers from Milford under Robert Treat, they founded Newark on the Passaic, carrying Branford’s church records with them. The Branford company arrived in 1667, the land was bought from the Hackensack that July for a list of goods that included guns, kettles, coats and ten pairs of breeches, and in the new town no one was to enjoy full citizenship, vote or hold office unless a member of a Congregational church (Newark Public Library).

Puritan churches admitted full members by a “relation,” a spoken account of the candidate’s experience of saving grace, which the church weighed by what it called the judgment of charity, since only God could see the heart. The Geneva Bible, the Bible of the Puritan migration, is quoted from the 1599 edition (text). Hopestill Ambler, Goodwife Bray, Sergeant Ruggles, the Lacey boy and the fire are invented.
