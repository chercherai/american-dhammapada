Dit and Dah

Passion is overcome only by him who has won through stillness of spirit the perfect vision.
— Śāntideva, The Path of Light, tr. L. D. Barnett

Di ké dak gi thö pa, at the Garden.

I. Two Things

Camp Hale, Colorado · winter 1962–63

The Americans called the place the Ranch, and the Tibetans called it Dumra, the Garden. It was a fenced corner of an old Army camp at nine thousand two hundred feet, a few Quonset huts and four classrooms under the snow, with signs on the road that said explosive ordnance to keep the elk hunters out. The instructors went by first names only. Lyle taught code.

He was thirty-eight, from Hastings, Nebraska, a Navy radioman in one war and an instructor at a Navy school after it, and his wife believed he was testing cold-weather equipment for the Army. He had Classroom Three, thirty men who had come in October. They had been flown in at night with the windows covered, and nobody had told them what country they were in. Most were Khampas, from the east of Tibet, horsemen and traders and a good many former monks, and none of them had ever seen a telegraph key. They had American names, because the Americans could not manage the others. In five months Lyle had to bring every one of them to twelve words a minute, sending and receiving, in five-letter groups off a one-time pad, on a set that ran from a hand-cranked generator. After that they would go home and be the only voice coming out of their country.

The first morning he held up two fingers.

Two things, he said. That's all there is. A short one and a long one. Dit, and dah. Everything you will ever send, the whole alphabet and every number, is put together out of those two. A man who has the two of them in his hand can say anything.

The interpreter put it into Tibetan. He was a young man named Jampa who had been to a mission school in Kalimpong, and the Americans called him Jim. He stood beside the instructor's desk all day, in every class, and translated standing. Lyle never saw him use a chair.

The men liked the two things. They said the word to one another, nyi, nyi, and held up two fingers at Lyle when they passed him on the path to the mess hall. In the evenings, after the practice hour, he would go by their hut and see a dozen of them gathered at one bunk, and hear it again. Nyi. The two. He was pleased. He thought they were drilling.

The bunk belonged to the man the Americans had named Walt. He was forty-one, the oldest in the class by ten years, and the others did not use that name. They called him Gen-la, which Jim said meant teacher. He had been the chant master of the great monastery at Lithang until 1956, the year of the siege, when the Chinese planes came over it. He had gone to a lama then and given back his vows in the proper form, and after that he picked up a rifle. He had the slowest fist in Classroom Three and the best ear. He could copy a message through any noise Lyle put on the line, because, Jim said, he had spent twenty-five years listening for one wrong voice in a hall full of them.

II. The Question

March 1963

By February Lyle knew the class was good. Jim knew something else about it, and it was Jim who told him, in his careful school English, on the path one night.

These ones have stopped asking when they will go, Jim said. The other rooms ask every day. In October these ones quarrelled at volleyball, and Hank and Dale did not speak for a week. Now look at them. I think they are very near something. It only needs a push.

Lyle said twelve words a minute was the only thing they needed to be near. Jim agreed that this was so.

The last Saturday of the course, the sending test was over and all thirty had passed it. Lyle stayed behind after supper to box up the practice keys. The men came into Classroom Three anyway and sat down at their desks, all thirty, with Walt in the second row where he always sat. The stove ticked. Nobody had called a class.

Jim came in last. He walked to the front, to his place by the desk, and he did not sit. He put his hands together and spoke to the second row in Tibetan. He spoke slowly and formally, and Lyle caught the one word he knew.

What did you ask him?

I said, Gen-la, people are always speaking of the two. What are the two?

You know what the two are. Dit and dah.

Jim did not answer that. He was waiting, and so were the thirty.

Walt said two words. Then he said a little more, perhaps a dozen words, in his ordinary voice. Then he straightened his back and chanted four lines, low, from the chest, and it was the first time Lyle had heard what that voice had been trained for. The windows hummed with it.

He says the two are staying and seeing, Jim said quietly. The mind stays where it is put. Then it sees what is there. He says when a man has gone all the way across in both of these, every knot he has comes undone. The rest is from a book the monks have by heart. There is an English one. A man in London made it, and the fathers had it at my school.

Lyle found the English one eleven years later, by mail, through the public library in Hastings. This is how the man in London had it.

Passion is overcome only by him who has won through stillness of spirit the perfect vision. Knowing this, I must first seek for stillness; it comes through the contentment that is regardless of the world.

— Śāntideva, The Path of Light, ch. VIII, tr. L. D. Barnett (1909)

Nothing happened in Classroom Three that Lyle could have put in a report. The men sat. The stove ticked. After a time one of them laughed, softly, the way a man laughs who has been looking for his hat and finds it on his head, and then Walt said something short, and they all got up and went to the mess hall. It was Saturday, when the cooks let them have the kitchen for dumplings.

It is finished for them, Jim said, on the path.

The course?

No.

In the camps in Mustang afterward, and in the settlements in Nepal and India after that, the men of Classroom Three were known. It was said of them that all thirty had crossed over on one night at the Garden, and that Gen-la had held the answer since October and was only waiting for somebody to ask. Nobody at the Ranch wrote it down. There was no form for it.

III. Loose Wrist

Camp Hale, 1963 · Hastings, Nebraska, 1974

Monday morning was the receiving test, and after it Lyle ran the class through one more hour of sending, for his own satisfaction. He had taught code for sixteen years. He knew the sound of a frightened hand, which clips the dits and leans on the dahs, and the sound of a hand that wants to be finished. He walked the rows with his eyes shut. He heard thirty keys that morning and there was no hurry in any of them and no drag, and when he opened his eyes every wrist in the room was lying loose on the desk as if it belonged to nobody.

They left that week, at night, in a bus with the windows painted over. There was no more jumping by then. They were flown back across the Pacific and went in on foot, some over the passes into Tibet and most up to Mustang. Lyle was not told where any of them went, and over the years he learned the ends of only five. Two were killed inside Tibet before the summer was out. One did nineteen years in a Chinese prison and came out of it, a visitor said, the calmest man in Lhasa. One kept a tea stall in Pokhara. It was said that Gen-la carried the radio for his group and never the rifle, and that he sent for six years from a ridge above the Kali Gandaki, slowly, and was never once asked to repeat.

Lyle left the government in 1971 and went home to Hastings. The book came in a brown mailer from the state library in Lincoln, a thin volume with a library plate from 1911, and he read the page at his kitchen table three times. He still had the practice key from the second row. He screwed it to the edge of the table that night and sent the sentence through once, start to finish, with a loose wrist.

Background

The verse

If the Brahmana has reached the other shore in both laws (in restraint and contemplation), all bonds vanish from him who has obtained knowledge.Dhammapada 384, tr. F. Max Müller (1881)

The commentary story is a single paragraph. Thirty monks from a distant country come to see the Buddha and sit down. The elder Sāriputta sees that they are ready for the last step, goes to the Buddha and, without sitting down, asks a beginner’s question on their behalf: people are always speaking of “the two states”; what are they? The Buddha answers that they are calm and insight (samatha and vipassanā; in Tibetan, zhiné and lhakthong), and speaks the verse. At the end of it all thirty are arahats. Müller, following another reading, took the two for restraint and contemplation. Read the full commentary story.

Camp Hale, 1958–1964

Camp Hale, in a high valley north of Leadville, was built in 1942 to train the ski troops of the 10th Mountain Division. From 1958 to 1964 the Central Intelligence Agency used a fenced corner of it to train some 250 to 300 Tibetan resistance fighters, in four contingents, the last of which arrived in October 1962. The program was secret; the cover story was atomic testing. The trainees, among them monks who had given back their vows to fight, called the camp Dumra, the Garden. Instructors went by first names and gave the Tibetans American ones, young English-speaking Tibetans interpreted, and by the instructors’ own account the longest part of the course was Morse code and radio procedure. On Saturday nights the trainees took over the kitchen. Lyle, Jampa, Gen-la and Classroom Three are invented, as are the fates given for them.

The earlier teams were parachuted into Tibet, and most of those men were killed or captured. After overflights stopped in 1960, graduates were sent overland or to the guerrilla base in Mustang, in Nepal, which held out until 1974. In 2010 a plaque was placed at Camp Hale, and in 2022 the valley became part of a national monument. The great monastery at Lithang, in Kham, was besieged in 1956 with bomber aircraft overhead; Tibetan accounts say it was bombed. The lines Gen-la chants are from the eighth chapter of Śāntideva’s Bodhicaryāvatāra, which Tibetan monks commonly learn by heart; the English is L. D. Barnett’s abridged version of 1909.
