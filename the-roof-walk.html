The Roof Walk

But oh! shipmates! on the starboard hand of every woe, there is a sure delight; and higher the top of that delight, than the bottom of the woe is deep.
— Moby-Dick, ch. 9

The way it was spun to me, on the wharves below Johnny Cake Hill.

I. Eight Bunches

County Street, New Bedford · June 1857

Joseph Sumner grew flowers on a lot at the top of the town that belonged to Captain Barzillai Hathaway, and every morning of the summer, before the Captain's household was up, he cut eight bunches and carried them in a flat basket on his head to the kitchen door of the Captain's house on County Street. Roses, mostly, the red ones the town was proud of, and a few white, with honeysuckle and mignonette for the smell. The cook gave him a dollar on Saturdays. He lived in a two-room cottage on the same lot with his wife Ann and their two boys, and the Captain owned the cottage too, and the well, and very nearly the ground the boys walked on to school.

The Captain was called the Commodore along the wharves, though he had never commanded anything but whaleships, because he walked down to his counting room every morning as if he were taking a squadron out. He had made three voyages as master and come home rich from the third, and he had shares now in eleven ships. Men who had sailed under him said he once kept a boatsteerer in irons in the run for fifty days off the coast of Japan, for answering back. Men who owed him money crossed the street. He had buried his wife and a son, the son lost at sea, and there was a cenotaph to the son in the Bethel on Johnny Cake Hill, a marble tablet with a broken mast cut in it, and the roses were for the dining room, where the Commodore ate alone.

On the morning this happened Joseph was coming down County Street at half past five with the basket on his head when he met Uncle Simeon Coombs coming up.

Everybody in New Bedford knew Uncle Simeon. He was a Gay Head man, from the cliffs at the west end of Martha's Vineyard, and he had gone whaling at fourteen and been a boatsteerer for thirty years, and come ashore at last with one hand stiff from a line that ran out through it off the Line Islands. He and his wife had kept a boardinghouse for colored seamen below Water Street, and since she died he kept it alone. He was past seventy. He met every ship that came in, whatever the hour, and took the men who had nowhere to go up to his house and fed them, and when the agents' runners came round to sign green boys for the next voyage he stood in his doorway and read the articles aloud so the boys would know what they were signing. Sailors of every color and nation knew his name in every port from Talcahuano to the Bay of Islands. He walked up through the town every morning to sit with whoever was sick in it. He did not say much. His great-niece Lyddy, who was twelve, walked behind him and carried a covered basket.

That morning, people said afterward, there was a light on him. The ones who saw him at the bottom of the street said the sun must have been coming off the harbor behind him. The ones who saw him at the top, where the sun was not up yet, could not account for it. It was the color of the water at noon in the Pacific, a whaleman said, the color it goes when you look down into it from the masthead and can see a hundred feet. It did not hurt to look at. It came off his old black coat and his white hair and his hands.

Joseph Sumner stopped in the road with the basket on his head and looked at him, and something in him wanted very badly to give him something, and he had nothing to give him but the Commodore's roses.

He stood there and thought it through, because he was a careful man. They are not mine, he thought. They are the Commodore's, or as good as. If they do not come to the kitchen door this morning he will turn me off the lot and out of the cottage, and nobody on County Street will buy a flower from a man the Commodore has turned off. Ann and the boys will be in the road. Then he thought: The most the Commodore can give me is a dollar on Saturday, as long as I last. And the most he can take from me is the same. He took the basket down off his head.

Joseph threw two handfuls of roses toward the old man, the way you would throw rice at a wedding, and they did not come down. They went up over his head and stopped there and hung, like an awning over a shop door. He threw two more, and they went round to his right side and hung there in the air, from his shoulder down to his boots, like a curtain. Two more went behind him and stayed. The last two went to his left. The stems were all turned in toward him and the flowers all turned out, red and white, and in front of him there was a space left open, as though for a door.

Uncle Simeon looked at him for a long moment, and nodded, and went on up the street. The roses went with him. When he walked they walked. When he stopped at the corner of Union Street to let a dray go by, they stopped and hung still around him, and not a petal fell.

II. Union Street

The same morning

A girl shaking a rug out of a window on County Street saw it first and screamed, and then the street was full. Nobody ran away. People came out of the houses half dressed, the Irish girls from the kitchens and the masters of the houses in their dressing gowns, and they went up to him and walked all the way around him, slowly, the roses and all, as if it were the only proper thing to do, and some went round him three times, and then fell in behind. By the time he reached the top of the town there were a hundred people following him, and by the time he came back down Union Street toward the water there were a thousand. Whalemen came off the ships in the harbor in their boats. Coopers came out of the shops with their adzes in their hands. Quaker women in grey bonnets walked beside Cape Verdean sailors with gold rings in their ears, and a Gay Head boatsteerer carried a white child on his shoulders so she could see. A brass band that had been practicing for the Fourth of July came out of its hall in its shirtsleeves and fell in ahead with the brass and the big drum and played whatever they knew, and people sang whatever they knew, and everybody waved whatever they had, handkerchiefs, hats, a Portuguese flag, a pair of drawers off somebody's line.

Uncle Simeon walked through it all as if it were an ordinary morning and went where he went every morning. He sat an hour with a boatsteerer from Barbados who had come home from the Arctic with his feet frozen, while the crowd waited in the lane and the roses hung around his chair. He went to a widow on Middle Street whose husband's ship had been posted missing. He went to the jail, where a green hand who had deserted at Fayal was being held for the agent. All day he walked, three miles or more up and down the town, and the drum went before him, and the roses never fell.

Joseph Sumner walked behind him a little way, as far as the head of Union Street. He was so happy he did not know what to do with his hands. He looked into the light that came off him, through the open place where the roses made a door, and he felt as if he had dived off a ship's rail into warm red water a long way down. Then he went home to his cottage with the empty basket on his arm.

III. The Commodore's Yard

Forenoon

Ann was at the washtub. She looked at the basket.

Where are the flowers?

I gave them to Uncle Simeon.

The Commodore's flowers.

He can turn us off the lot, Joseph said. I know that. I thought about it in the road. I would do it again. Listen. You can hear them from here.

She could hear the drum down in the town and the shouting. She did not believe a word of the rest of it. Ann Sumner had been born free in Providence and had seen what happened to people who crossed men like the Commodore. She had seen a whole family put out of a house near the docks with their bedsteads in the snow, and she had seen what became of a man the merchants agreed not to hire. She told Joseph so. She told him she had the boys to think of, if he had not. Then she dried her hands, put on her good bonnet, took a boy by each hand, and walked up County Street to the Commodore's front door, which she had never in her life gone near, and asked for the Captain.

He came out into the hall himself. He was a big grey man with a face like a shut door.

Captain Hathaway, my husband gave your roses away this morning to Uncle Simeon Coombs, every one of them, and came home with the basket empty. I told him what I thought. I have come to tell you that whatever he has done, good or bad, it is his doing and not mine. I have left him. That is all I came to say.

The Commodore had seen Uncle Simeon go by his window an hour before in his pavilion of roses, with the band in front. He had stood at the window with his coffee going cold and his hand flat on the glass. He looked at the woman in the hall and the two little boys staring at the iron harpoons worked into his front fence, and he understood her perfectly. He made his face as hard as it had ever been on a quarterdeck.

He gave my flowers to somebody else?

Yes, sir.

Then you did right to leave him. Go home, madam. I will know how to deal with Joseph Sumner.

When she had gone he took his hat and went out by the side door, and down through the town by back lanes, faster than anyone had seen him go since he came ashore, until he found the crowd in Middle Street. It opened for him because it always had. He went in through the door the roses made and took off his hat to Uncle Simeon, and what he said to him nobody heard. Nobody in New Bedford knew that on the night the news came of his son's ship, nine years before, the Commodore had walked down below Water Street in the dark and knocked at the boardinghouse door, and that the old harpooneer had sat with him in his kitchen until morning without saying anything at all. He walked beside him after that, the two of them alone inside the flowers, and the crowd behind.

At noon Uncle Simeon came to the Commodore's gate on County Street. The Commodore held it open and asked him to come into the house. Uncle Simeon shook his head. He went to the bench under the horse-chestnut tree in the front yard, where the street could see him over the fence, and sat down there, and the roses settled around the bench and hung. Lyddy sat on the grass at his feet.

The Commodore understood that too, after a minute. If the old man went inside, the people in the street would see a closed door. Out here they could see what Joseph Sumner had done. He called the cook and the girls and had every chair in the house carried out to the yard, and the dining table, and the cold dinner that had been meant for him alone, and then the bread in the house and the cheese and the ham and the pies, and when that was gone he sent to the baker's. He served Uncle Simeon himself, on his wife's Canton plates, and he stood behind his chair while he ate, the way a steward stands behind a captain. The street ate in his yard until the middle of the afternoon. Then Uncle Simeon thanked him, and got up, and went down the hill toward the water with the roses around him and the band and the people behind, and the Commodore walked with him as far as the corner and came back.

IV. The Roof Walk

Evening

He sent for Joseph Sumner at five o'clock. Joseph came to the kitchen door, as he had come every morning for six summers, and the cook sent him round to the front.

When you threw my roses at him, the Commodore said, what did you say?

I said to myself, sir, let the Commodore turn me off the lot. I said the most he can give me is a dollar on Saturday, and the most he can take is the same.

The Commodore looked at him for a long time.

You are a bigger man than I am, he said.

Then he gave him eight of everything he could think of. He gave him the deed to the lot and the cottage, made out that evening by a lawyer who was fetched from his supper. He gave him eight hundred dollars. He gave him eight cords of seasoned oak and eight barrels of sperm oil, enough to light the cottage the rest of his life, and eight shares in the bark that was fitting out at his wharf, and a standing order for eight bunches every summer morning at twice the price, as long as either of them lived. Ann came back from wherever she had gone to wait and found her husband sitting on the step of a house that belonged to him, with a piece of paper in his hands, laughing.

•••

Walking home down Union Street in the long June evening, with the crowd thinning out behind them at last, Lyddy asked her great-uncle what Joseph Sumner was going to get for it.

You saw what he got. The Commodore gave him the house.

I mean after. In the end.

Uncle Simeon did not answer right away.

Don't you think it was a little thing he did, he said. He gave the whole of what he had, and he thought it would cost him the roof over his children, and he gave it anyway and was glad. A man who has done that once is not going all the way down again. Not ever. And one of these days he will find the whole of it by himself, the way some men find a passage nobody ever charted, with nobody to show him and nobody behind.

His house below Water Street had a roof walk on top, a little railed platform between the chimneys, where he had watched for ships when he was ashore and his wife had watched for his when he was not. When he came to his own door and went in, the roses that had hung around him all day did not come in after him. They went up past the eaves, slowly, all together, and came down on the roof walk, and lay there along the rail in a heap, red and white, as if somebody had come home from sea and thrown them up.

That night the boarders at his long table could talk about nothing else. There were eleven of them, from Nantucket and Fayal and Barbados and Bonny and the Sandwich Islands, and two Gay Head men, and they went over it and over it. A gardener had given away his master's roses and got a house for it the same day, and money, and oil, and shares in a ship. It was the luckiest throw anybody had ever heard of. One of them had been to the Bethel that afternoon to see the son's tablet, and complained that the pulpit there was only a plain box and not built like a ship's bow, the way it was in the book.

Uncle Simeon came out of the kitchen with the coffeepot and asked them what they were talking about. They told him.

It was not the house he was glad about, he said. He was glad on Union Street, with nothing in his basket. Lyddy, get the book.

The book was a whaling story a boatsteerer had left in his front room six years before, with his name in it and a smell of the forecastle still on the pages. Nobody else in New Bedford seemed to have read it, but his boarders always did, because it began in their own town and in their own chapel, and had a Gay Head harpooneer in it, which Uncle Simeon said was the best thing about it. Lyddy brought it, and Uncle Simeon found the place without looking for it, near the end of the sermon, and gave it back to the girl to read, because his own eyes were tired. Lyddy read it out over the coffee, in the lamplight, with the roses outside on the roof.

Delight is to him—a far, far upward, and inward delight—who against the proud gods and commodores of this earth, ever stands forth his own inexorable self.

And eternal delight and deliciousness will be his, who coming to lay him down, can say with his final breath—O Father!—chiefly known to me by Thy rod—mortal or immortal, here I die. I have striven to be Thine, more than to be this world’s, or mine own.

— Moby-Dick, ch. 9

The Gay Head men laughed at commodores, and then nobody laughed.

In the morning Joseph Sumner cut eight bunches of roses before it was light, the way he always had, and put them in the flat basket and carried it on his head down County Street to the Commodore's kitchen door. The dew was still on them. On the roof walk below Water Street the others lay where they had come down, and stayed fresh, Lyddy said, all that week, and the gulls would not stand on the rail.

Background

The verse

No, that deed is well done of which a man does not repent, and the reward of which he receives gladly and cheerfully.Dhammapada 68, tr. F. Max Müller (1881)

In the commentary, the garland-maker Sumana brings King Bimbisāra eight measures of jasmine every morning. One day he meets the Buddha entering Rājagaha in a blaze of light and, reckoning that the king can at worst kill or banish him, throws all eight handfuls over the Teacher instead. The flowers hang in the air on four sides like a pavilion and travel with him, and the whole city follows to the sound of drums, waving cloths. Sumana's wife, terrified, takes the children to the king and disowns her husband. The king, a secret disciple, feigns anger and sends her home, then joins the Buddha, who chooses to sit in the palace courtyard rather than indoors so that everyone can see what the garland-maker has done. Afterward the king gives Sumana eight of everything. Asked by Ānanda what the man's reward will be, the Buddha says he will not fall into a state of suffering for a hundred thousand aeons and will one day become a solitary Buddha; the flowers fall onto the roof of the Buddha's chamber, and that evening he speaks the verse. New Bedford, 1857

By 1857 the harbor of New Bedford held 329 vessels worth more than twelve million dollars, and the town counted as the richest per head in North America. Its whaling fortunes built the mansions and gardens along County Street; its crews were drawn from everywhere: free and escaped African Americans, Wampanoag men from Gay Head on Martha's Vineyard and Pequots, Cape Verdeans, Pacific Islanders and Europeans. The town was an important stop on the Underground Railroad; by 1853 it had 1,008 African American residents, some 700 of them escaped from slavery, and Frederick Douglass had worked on its wharves for three years after his own escape. The Port Society dedicated the Seamen's Bethel on Johnny Cake Hill in 1832; its walls carry cenotaphs for whalemen lost at sea. Today the old waterfront is the New Bedford Whaling National Historical Park.

Herman Melville sat in the Bethel before he shipped out on the whaler Acushnet in January 1841, and ten years later opened Moby-Dick in New Bedford, with Father Mapple's sermon on Jonah preached from a pulpit shaped like a ship's bow. The book sold poorly and was out of print by the end of Melville's life. The real pulpit in 1841 was most likely a plain box; the prow now standing in the Bethel was inspired by the 1956 film of the novel and first put up in 1961. Uncle Simeon, the Sumners and Captain Hathaway are invented; Melville's own harpooneer Tashtego is a Gay Head man.
