Lilac Slips

I have the goats. And I have a few sheep among my goats, but they are very ragged.
— Narrative of Sojourner Truth

This is how the old people told it, in Calvin Township.

I. The Mill Branch

Calvin Township, Cass County, Michigan · spring 1858

Mother Ruth Ann Hollins had come out from Northampton County, North Carolina, in a covered wagon in the fall of 1844, with her husband and four grown children and her free papers sewn into the lining of her bonnet, and by 1858 she had outlived the husband and two of the children and was the nearest thing to a bishop the colored Methodists of Calvin Township had. She was not ordained to anything. The Discipline did not ordain women. She was a class leader, which the Discipline allowed, and she prayed and testified and exhorted whether the Discipline allowed it or not, and she had been doing all three every Sunday and Wednesday for fourteen years in a log meetinghouse on the edge of a tamarack swamp. Most of the families round about had come out the same road, from Virginia and the Carolinas, free people of color with a little Indian in most of them and a lot of Indian in some. The fugitives came later, at night, by way of the Quaker farms, and some of them stayed. She did not ask them anything when they came to meeting. She asked them their names and said them back, and that was all.

Two men did most of her preaching for her, and the township called them her right hand and her left. Silas Porter was a cooper, thin and quiet, who could take a hard place in Paul and lay it open like a stave. Mose Gallion was a blacksmith from Halifax County, as big as his anvil, and when Mose prayed at a camp meeting, men who had come to laugh went down in the straw like steers that have been hit between the eyes and lay there an hour and got up weeping. People said he could see the inside of a man the way he saw the color of iron in the fire. Nobody doubted it.

Each of them had a young man he was bringing along. Silas had Asa Freeland, who had a voice like a bell and could line out a hymn so that the whole swamp sang it back. Mose had Prentiss Booker, whom everybody called Bass, who was handsome and quick and could make any congregation laugh and then cry inside of a quarter hour. Two years before, when the families out at the Rocky Fork, eight miles north where Kimmel's sawmill was, asked for a class of their own so they would not have to walk in every Sunday, Mother Ruth Ann sent Asa and Bass to lead it.

It went well for a season. Then it went another way.

The first thing anybody noticed was the lilacs. Asa and Bass planted a row of them all along the front of the Rocky Fork schoolhouse where the class met, and rose bushes at the corners, and flowering crab apples down the lane, and by the second spring the schoolhouse looked like a rich man's front yard. Then they started cutting slips. They rooted lilac slips in wet moss and carried them round to the houses where there were daughters, and roses in May, and crab-apple blossom in a pail, and the mothers set the table for them, and the fathers gave them a ham or a sack of meal or a dollar for the preaching, and they went home heavy. They held play-parties in the schoolhouse on Saturday nights, with the benches pushed to the walls and the girls going round in Weevily Wheat and Skip to My Lou, and people said that once or twice the play-party had had a fiddle in it, which made it a dance, and the Discipline was plain about dancing. There was card playing out behind the sawmill with the mill hands, for money. There was talk about Bass and a girl at the Wilkins place, and the girl's father had stopped speaking to anybody. Old Widow Tate, who had nothing, had given them her last two dollars for a love offering because they told her it would be credited to her in glory, and now she had nothing and two dollars less.

The steady ones at the Rocky Fork, the old people who had come to be Methodists and not to play Skip to My Lou, stopped going to the schoolhouse. They walked the eight miles in to the log meetinghouse on Sundays instead, and back again in the dark. When the walk got too hard for them, some of them stopped going anywhere.

II. The Book

Mother Ruth Ann's kitchen · May

It was the old ones who came to her finally, three of them, Brother and Sister Anthony and old Lige Day, who had walked in from the Rocky Fork in the rain. They did not want to make trouble for anybody. They sat at her table with their hats on their knees and told it, and she listened, and when they were done she thanked them and gave them supper and sent her grandson to hitch the wagon and drive them home.

Then she sent for Silas Porter and Mose Gallion.

They came in from their work, the cooper with shavings in his hair and the blacksmith with his apron still on. They had heard most of it already. They knew why they had been sent for, and they did not like it, because the two young men were theirs.

You will go out to the Rocky Fork, she said, you two, Saturday week, and stay the Sunday. Talk to them. Teach them. Any that will hear you, keep them, and keep teaching them. Any that won't hear you, put them out of the class. I'll stand behind you at the quarterly meeting.

Mose rubbed his face. Bass will hate me for it.

He will, she said. The man that tells a fool the truth gets hated by the fool. That's how it is. The ones with any sense in them will love you for it, maybe not the same day.

She had a book on the shelf by the clock, a thin one, bound in paper, and she sent her grandson for it. She had bought it in the fall from the woman who made it, at a meeting in Battle Creek, where that woman had lately come to live: a tall Black woman about Mother Ruth Ann's own age, who had been a slave in New York and named herself Sojourner Truth and went about the country speaking, and sold the book of her life at the end of every meeting to keep herself. Mother Ruth Ann could not read much. Her grandson could. She had him find the place, near the middle, about a camp meeting in Massachusetts years before where a hundred wild young men came to break it up with clubs and set the tents on fire, and the woman went out alone and stood on a little rise in the moonlight and sang, and they came and stood around her with their sticks.

The boy read it out slowly by the lamp. Silas and Mose listened with their elbows on the table.

She looked about her, and with her usual discrimination, said inwardly—‘Here must be many young men in all this assemblage, bearing within them hearts susceptible of good impressions. I will speak to them.’

‘Well, there are two congregations on this ground. It is written that there shall be a separation, and the sheep shall be separated from the goats. The other preachers have the sheep, I have the goats. And I have a few sheep among my goats, but they are very ragged.’

— Narrative of Sojourner Truth

Mose Gallion laughed in spite of himself, the way the young men in the book had laughed, and then put his head down on his arms.

Look for the ones with the hearts in them, Mother Ruth Ann said. Speak to them. And don't you be afraid of the goats.

III. The Schoolhouse

The Rocky Fork · the last Saturday in May

The lilacs were in full bloom when they drove up, the whole front of the schoolhouse purple and white, and the smell of them came down the lane to meet the wagon. There was a play-party starting inside. They could hear it from the road, the clapping and somebody calling the figures, and Bass Booker's laugh over all of it.

Mose Gallion got down and went and stood in the schoolhouse door. He did not say anything. He did not need to. He filled it from the sill to the lintel, and the clapping stopped, and the girls stopped going round, and the boys from the mill who had been leaning on the walls stood up off them. Silas Porter came in past him and set a chair in the middle of the floor where the play had been, and sat down on it, and took out his Testament, and waited until the room was quiet. Then he talked to them.

He talked until after dark, the way he would have talked to a barrel that was leaking, a stave at a time. He did not shout. He named the widow's two dollars, and the Wilkins girl, and the cards behind the mill, and he named them in front of the mothers who had set the tables and the fathers who had paid for the preaching, so that it went round the room like a draft. Then he read them a long piece out of the second chapter of James, about the man with the gold ring and the poor man in vile raiment, and asked them which one Widow Tate was. Mose stood in the door the whole time with his arms folded and his eyes going from one face to another, and people said afterward that every one of them felt him looking inside, and found out what it was like to be a piece of iron in the fire.

It did not go one way. It went three.

Asa Freeland broke first. He had been standing by the stove with his hymnbook in his hand, and somewhere in the middle of James he sat down on a bench and put his face in his hands. At the end he got up and went out in the dark, and came back with the widow's two dollars and four more of his own and put them in Silas Porter's hand, and asked if he might go back to carrying Silas's tools a while, and lead the singing and nothing else. Some of the young people went with him, the ones with the hearts in them. A Wilkins boy cried. Two of the girls went home with their mothers and came to class the next morning with their heads tied up plain.

Some of the others said nothing at all, got their hats, and went out. A few of them were never seen in a Methodist class again. They went back to the mill and the plow and their own business and lived their lives, and married, and some of them got rich in a small way, and nobody could say it was the worst thing that ever happened to them. In the language of the Discipline, a preacher who quits the traveling work and goes home to his farm is said to have located. The township took up the word for anybody who left that night. He located, they said, and it was not unkind.

Bass Booker did neither. He stood up in front of the room and told Silas Porter what he thought of an old cooper who had never been out of the township coming to tell him how to preach, and told Mose Gallion what he thought of a big ignorant smith standing in a door like a hog in a gate, and he laughed, and a few laughed with him, and he walked out past Mose into the lilacs. At the quarterly meeting in June his name was read out and he was suspended from the Society, and Mother Ruth Ann stood up in the back of the log meetinghouse when it was read, as she had said she would, so that everyone could see whose doing it was. Bass told it all over the county that summer that Mose Gallion was a jealous old fool, and he hated Mose until the day Mose died, and some say after.

He was the only one who took anything with him when he went. On his way out that night, in the dark, he broke off an armful of lilac from the bushes he had planted, and walked off down the mill road with it over his shoulder, and the smell went along with him a long way after you could not see him anymore.

Silas Porter rooted a slip from the same bushes the next spring, in wet moss, the way Asa had shown him, and carried it out to Widow Tate's, and set it by her door.

Background

The verse

Let him admonish, let him teach, let him forbid what is improper!—he will be beloved of the good, by the bad he will be hated.Dhammapada 77, tr. F. Max Müller (1881)

In the commentary, two monks who are pupils of the Buddha's chief disciples settle with their followers at Kīṭāgiri and run the place into disrepute: they plant flowering trees and give flowers to lay families to win their favor, live off what the families give, and behave so badly that well-conducted monks can no longer stay there. The Buddha sends Sāriputta and Moggallāna, telling them to expel those who will not obey and to admonish and teach those who will, since the one who admonishes is hated by fools and loved by the wise, and speaks the verse. The two elders go. Some of the monks accept correction, some return to lay life, and some are formally suspended. Read the full commentary story, or about Sāriputta and Moggallāna.

Cass County, Michigan, 1858

From 1829 Quakers from Ohio and Indiana and free Black families settled the southwestern Michigan county of Cass, above all Calvin Township, where by 1850 a quarter of the people were African American, many of them families of mixed Native ancestry as well. The county was a station on the Underground Railroad, and freedom seekers who came through on their way to Canada sometimes stayed. In August 1847 Kentucky slaveholders raided the Quaker farms and seized nine people; a crowd of white and Black neighbors stopped them at a mill in Vandalia, and a local court set the captives free along with dozens of others (the Kentucky raid). The Methodist society, its people and the Rocky Fork are invented.

Sojourner Truth, born into slavery in Ulster County, New York, dictated her life to Olive Gilbert, and the Narrative of Sojourner Truth was printed in 1850; she sold it at her meetings to support herself. Its account of an 1844 camp meeting near Northampton, Massachusetts, where she faced down a mob of young men by singing, is the passage read in Mother Ruth Ann's kitchen. In September 1857 Truth moved to Michigan, near Battle Creek, some forty-five miles from Calvin, and made Michigan her home for the rest of her life.
