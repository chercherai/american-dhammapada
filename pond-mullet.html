Kō kiita yo, in Camp Seven.

I. Moxa

West Kauai, Territory of Hawaii · 1908

The temple in Camp Seven stood at the end of the Japanese row, past the bathhouse and the store, on posts high enough that the dogs slept under it. It was board and batten with a tin roof. The plantation had paid for the lumber, the manager having been told in Honolulu that a camp with a temple had fewer fights on payday. Inside, behind a low rail, hung a scroll of Amida Buddha standing on a lotus, and below him two brass vases and a candle stand that the women of the camp polished on Saturdays with ash from the bathhouse fire.

Nishiyama Ryōun had come out from Kyoto in the spring of 1907 to take charge of it. He was twenty-six. He had a good voice for the sutras and a quick laugh, and he could write a letter home for a man in a hand that made the man’s mother proud of him. And he had one thing more, which the camp found out in his first month. His father was a village doctor in Aki, in the Hiroshima country, and the son had rolled moxa and ground powders in his father’s back room since before he could write his own name.

Most of the camp was from Hiroshima and Yamaguchi, from the old Shin villages where every house had its altar and a man who had never read a book could say whole letters of Rennyo Shōnin by heart. They knew the teaching as well as any priest did on this one point. Followers of Shinran keep no charms. They buy no prayers for a cure and ask no one for a lucky day. Shinran himself had written that it was a sorrow to see priests and laymen alike counting auspicious days and calling on the gods for help. A Shin priest said the Name, buried the dead, wrote letters, settled quarrels, and left the curing to the doctor.

But the plantation doctor was a haole who rode up from the mill twice a week and spoke no Japanese. It was said he had sent a man back to hāpai kō with two cracked ribs. So people came to the temple instead, at night, by the back steps.

Nishiyama burned moxa on the shoulders of the cane carriers, where the bundles had worn the skin to leather. He gave powders out of the lacquered chest he had brought from his father’s house, wrapped in twists of red paper. And when a mother asked him, as they did ask, he wrote the Name on a strip of paper and folded it in three and told her to put it under the pillow. Then he chanted over the child, low, with his eyes closed, words he had been taught were for giving thanks and for the dead and for nothing else.

He was paid in what the camp had. Eggs. A hen with her legs tied. Two bottles of sake from Japan at New Year. Men cut cane in Camp Seven for eighteen dollars a month, and nobody paid a priest in money.

Fujita, who kept the camp store, had a boy of seven who ran a fever for five days in March. On the fifth night Nishiyama burned three cones of moxa on the soles of the boy’s feet and wrote the Name for under his pillow, and in the morning the fever had broken. It might have broken anyway. Fujita did not think so. On the following Sunday his wife sent up a three-tiered box of black lacquer, tied in a purple cloth. In the top tier was red rice with black sesame. In the second were pickled ginger and sweet black beans. In the third, laid on a ti leaf, was a whole fat mullet from one of the Hawaiian ponds by the sea, broiled with salt until the skin had blistered gold.

He sat on the temple step and looked at it a long while, and was happy.

He was not a wicked man. He thought, when he thought about it, that the people needed what he gave them and would be worse off without it, and that Amida’s Vow had been made for foolish men exactly like himself, so where was the harm. Then he did not think about it. He slept well. In the mornings the mynahs woke him.

They had been brought from India forty-odd years before to eat the cutworms out of the young cane. They had eaten the cutworms and then everything else. They walked into the cookhouse when the door was open. They took rice out of a lunch tin while the man it belonged to was still bending over it. They had no fear of anybody, and they shouted all day long about whatever they had. The Hawaiians called them pihaʻekelo, full of voice. Every night at dusk they came in from the fields by the hundreds to the monkeypod tree beside the store and quarreled there until dark, and every morning they went out again, and nobody in Camp Seven had ever seen one go hungry.

II. The Ditch Road

That noon

He had a memorial service that afternoon at Camp Nine, across the gulch, and he took the box with him to eat on the way.

The road ran along the top of the ditch bank. On one side was the water, brown and quick, going down to the fields in its plank flume. On the other was cane taller than a man on horseback. At the place where a footbridge crossed the flume, an old woman sat on the planks in the shade of the cane, eating her lunch.

Her name was Higa Ushi. She was from Okinawa, from a village near Nago, and she was the oldest woman in the hoeing gang. The Hiroshima women called her Obaa, grandmother, because they did not know her name. In their worse moods they called the Okinawans in the gang pig-eaters, not always out of her hearing. The backs of her hands were tattooed in dark blue, little crosses and dots and bars, from the knuckles to the wrist, the way the women of her island had been marked for centuries. Her husband had died the second year. She had stayed. She sent money home to a grandson in Nago and kept a hoe whose blade had been filed down to a crescent.

She had a lunch tin on her knees, and in it two boiled sweet potatoes and a pinch of salt.

Nishiyama sat down on the planks beside her and untied the purple cloth.

Obaa-san, look at this. I got this for treating Fujita’s boy. Mullet out of a Hawaiian pond. You won’t eat like this anywhere on Kauai, I promise you. Take it, eat. He lifted off the top two tiers and held the fish out to her on its leaf. And from now on, whenever they pay me like this for a treatment, I’ll bring you some. Every time.

She listened to all of it. She looked at the mullet, and then at him, for no longer than it takes to look at anything. Then she put the lid on her tin and stood up, which at her age took a moment. She took up the hoe and went back down into the cane without a word, and the leaves closed behind her.

He sat there holding the fish.

Two women of the gang had been eating at the end of the next row, Mrs. Ōta and Mrs. Hamada, from Yamaguchi. They had seen the whole of it. They looked at him and then at each other and then down into their tins.

He laughed, because there was nothing else to do, and he said something about Okinawan pride, and ate the mullet there on the flume with his fingers. It was very good. Two mynahs came down onto the planks while he ate and walked right up to him, and he threw them the skin, and they fought over it and shouted.

III. The Gate

That night

The women told Mukai Jisaburō.

They had not decided to. They meant to tell nobody. But Mrs. Ōta saw him that evening outside the store with his lantern and his shovel, going up to the night water, and she found she had stopped him and was telling it, and Mrs. Hamada was nodding beside her.

Jisaburō was sixty-three. He had come out from Aki in 1885, the first year the emigrant ships came, and he had never gone home. He was the night man on the Number Four ditch. All night he walked the lines with a lantern, opening one gate and closing another so that the water went down every furrow in its turn and none of the cane went dry, and at every gate he lifted he said the Name under his breath. He was the head of the temple board because nobody would have it otherwise. Some people in the camp called him a myōkōnin, one of the wondrous good people, behind his back. He would have been ashamed to hear it. He spoke very little.

He listened to the women. He thanked them. Then he set his lantern on the store step and walked down to the temple instead of up to the ditch.

Nishiyama was sitting on the temple step in the dark with the empty lacquer box beside him and a cup of sake. In the monkeypod tree by the store the mynahs were settling, a thousand of them, all shouting.

Jisaburō sat down a step below him. For a while he said nothing.

Listen to them, he said at last.

They make a racket.

An easy life, the mynah’s. They came for the cutworms. Now they eat out of everybody’s tin. Nobody gives them a thing. They take it, and then they tell the whole valley, and then they sleep well. He was quiet a while. There’s nothing easier than to be a mynah, Sensei.

Nishiyama poured himself another cup and did not drink it.

Higa-san is in the cane at six, said Jisaburō. She comes out at half past four. She buys nothing at Fujita’s that she can’t pay for that day. She has never eaten a mullet like that in her life, and she will not eat yours. He looked at his own hands. They were split across the knuckles from the shovel. That is a hard way to live. She gets up every morning and does it again.

She didn’t have to walk off like that.

No.

The mynahs were quieting. A few still shouted, here and there in the tree.

I’m only a ditch man, said Jisaburō. You’ve read the books. I never did. I only know what the priest in my village used to say, when I was a boy. In Shinran Shōnin’s time there was a man who reasoned that the Vow was made to save evil men, and so he might as well do evil. And when the Shōnin heard about it he wrote a letter. He said the words carefully, as if lifting a gate board out of its slot.

Though a remedy may be at hand one must not take poison.

— Tannishō XIII, tr. Tosui Imadate (1928)

You have the remedy, Sensei. You’re the one who taught it to this camp. Every soul in Camp Seven knows it’s there. He stood up. I’m saying it to myself as well. I’m an old fool. I drank away my first three years on this island, and I know how easy the easy life is.

He bowed, and went back up to the store for his lantern, and Nishiyama watched the light go along the ditch bank into the dark and stop, and stop again, at every gate.

•••

The next noon at the footbridge Mrs. Ōta came along the bank with her lunch tin and sat down on the planks beside Higa Ushi. She did not say anything about the day before. She opened her tin. She had white rice and a pickled plum and a strip of dried fish, and she set the tin down on the plank between them.

The old woman looked at it. After a while she took one of her two sweet potatoes and broke it in half and laid the larger half in Mrs. Ōta’s tin, beside the plum.

Mrs. Ōta asked her where she was from. Nago, said Higa Ushi. Near Nago. And she said her name.

Two mynahs came down onto the flume and walked up and down the planks in front of them, shouting, and waited, and got nothing.

Background

The verses

Life is easy to live for a man who is without shame, a crow hero, a mischief-maker, an insulting, bold, and wretched fellow.Dhammapada 244, tr. F. Max Müller (1881)
But life is hard to live for a modest man, who always looks for what is pure, who is disinterested, quiet, spotless, and intelligent.Dhammapada 245, tr. F. Max Müller (1881)

In the commentary, Culla Sāri, a monk who lived with the elder Sāriputta, gave medical treatment to a lay household and was paid with a portion of choice food. That kind of livelihood was against the rules the Buddha had laid down for monks. On his way back he met an elder on the road and offered him some: nowhere else would he get food like this, and from now on, whenever Culla Sāri was paid like this, he would bring him some. The elder heard him out and walked on without a word. When the monks told the Buddha, he said that a man without shame, bold as a crow, lives easily, and a man with a sense of shame lives hard. Kauai, 1908

The first Japanese contract laborers reached Hawaii on February 8, 1885. Most of those who followed came from Hiroshima, Yamaguchi and Kumamoto, to work in the sugar fields. Okinawans began arriving in January 1900, and by 1908 more than 8,500 lived in the islands. Other Japanese looked down on them, called them pig-eaters, and mocked the hajichi tattoos on the women's hands. The Gentlemen's Agreement of 1907–08 ended new emigration of Japanese laborers. When Japanese workers struck on Oahu the next year, their pay was eighteen dollars a month, well below what Portuguese and Puerto Rican workers earned.

Most of the emigrants were Jōdo Shinshū Buddhists. The first Hongwanji temple in the islands was dedicated in 1889, and Bishop Yemyo Imamura led the Honpa Hongwanji Mission from 1900 to 1932. Its ministers traveled the camps on foot and on horseback, held funerals, wrote letters home for the workers, and settled disputes. Some plantations helped pay for the temples. On Kauai the Līhuʻe Hongwanji began in 1901 with support from the Lihue Plantation Company. Shin Buddhism rejects charms, spells and prayers for worldly benefit. The words Jisaburō recites come from the Tannishō, a late thirteenth-century record of Shinran's sayings. The common myna was brought to Hawaii from India in the 1860s to eat armyworms and cutworms in the cane, and people complained of it for its noise and its nerve. The Hawaiian name, pihaʻekelo, means "full of voice."
