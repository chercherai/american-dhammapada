One Footprint

Keep thou thy horses, keep dance and song for thyself.
— Katha Upanishad 1:26

Ī vidhaṅgā nēnu vinnānu, on the cul-de-sacs off Eldorado Parkway.

I. The Fire

Frisco, Texas · October 2004

Gopalakrishna Sarma lit his fire every morning a few minutes before sunrise, and for six years he lit it on the back patio, until the homeowners' association sent the third letter. The first letter said open burning. The second said a neighbor has expressed concern regarding odor. The third had a fine in it, a hundred and fifty dollars, and a photograph of his own backyard taken from somebody's upstairs window.

So he carried the fire to the end of the street. Past the last cul-de-sac the builder had graded the land for Phase Four, poured the streets and the sidewalks and set the fire hydrants, and then stopped to wait for the market. There were no houses yet. There were curbs that went around nothing, and street signs with names on them, Calder Court and Bandera Way, standing over black clay and Johnson grass. Every morning in the blue dark Sarma walked out there in his white dhoti and a Texas Instruments windbreaker, with a copper pot shaped like an upside-down pyramid in one hand and a tote bag from Patel Brothers in the other, and set the pot down on the bare ground where the corner lot of Calder Court would one day be.

He laid the dried cakes of cow dung in it, the ones the Indian grocery sold in plastic sleeves for the pujas, and dabbed them with ghee and lit them. He had printed the sunrise times for the whole year from a website and kept the page folded in his wallet. At the minute the sun cleared the tree line along Panther Creek he dropped two pinches of unbroken rice into the flame with ghee on his fingers and said the words. His father had said them every day of his life in a village on the Krishna delta, and his father's father, and nobody knew how many fathers before that. The smoke went up straight on a still morning and sideways on a windy one. Then he walked home and showered and put on khakis and drove to Richardson, where he was a director of hardware engineering at a company that had laid off a third of its people in 2001 and kept him.

He had come to College Station in 1979 with a scholarship, two suitcases and the few dollars the Reserve Bank allowed, and now he had a house with three garages and a daughter.

Her name was Madhuri and she was twenty-five, and the Telugu families from Plano to McKinney agreed she was the most beautiful girl any of them had seen on this side of the ocean. She had danced Kuchipudi since she was six, on the brass plate with a water pot on her head, at every Ugadi and every Diwali in every rented school auditorium in Collin County. She was a pharmacist at a hospital in Plano. The proposals had been coming since she was nineteen. They came as envelopes from aunties in Hyderabad with a photograph and a horoscope inside, as phone calls from friends of friends in New Jersey, as printouts from the matrimony websites that her mother read at the kitchen counter with her reading glasses on her head.

Sarma said no to all of them. A cardiologist in Houston: Niyogi, not Vaidiki, and the family ate onions on Ekadashi. An engineer at Microsoft: Redmond was too far, too cold, and his father had married twice. A boy with a Stanford degree whose father owned three cinema halls in Vijayawada: cinema halls, Sarma said, and that was the end of that. A boy from their own district, from a good family, with a green card and a house in Irving: Sarma looked at the photograph for a long time and said the boy's chin was weak. He said the same words about each of them. He folded each biodata in thirds and laid it in a drawer of the desk in his study, and the drawer was full.

Not good enough for her.

On a cold morning in late October, with Bush–Cheney signs still standing in half the yards on Eldorado Parkway, a man came walking along the empty sidewalk of Phase Four from the direction of the highway, at the hour when nobody in that town walked anywhere.

He was thin and brown and perhaps forty, in a grey sweatshirt and old running shoes, with a canvas pack on his back and a steel bowl tied to the pack with a shoelace. He stopped a little way off from the fire, not too close, and stood with his hands at his sides and watched. He did not shift his weight. Sarma finished the words and dropped the rice and the ghee hissed, and the man was still standing there, and something in the way he stood made Sarma, who had stood before very important men in his life, want to get up off his heels.

You are Telugu, Sarma said in Telugu. He could tell from the face. Everybody could always tell.

My people are from Guntur, the man said. His Telugu was a Houston boy's, careful, with English underneath it. I grew up in Houston. Lately I'm not from anywhere.

Sarma looked at him. He looked at the hands, which were long and very clean though the man had plainly slept outdoors, and at the shoulders and the eyes and the stillness. He thought that in twenty-five years in America he had not seen a man like this one, and in India not either, and that this was what he had been waiting for. He did not ask the man's gotram. He did not ask what his father did. It would occur to him later that it was the first time in six years that he had not asked a single question.

I have one daughter, he said. Only one. I have looked for a man for her for six years and I have not found one good enough. You are good enough. I want to give her to you. Wait here, right here, and I'll bring her.

The man listened to all of it. He did not say yes and he did not say no.

II. The Footprint

Calder Court · the same morning

Vasundhara was at the stove boiling milk for coffee when her husband came in through the garage still in his dhoti, which he had never done, and said he had found him.

Found who?

The one. For Madhuri. He's standing at the end of the street. Wake her up.

Madhuri had worked until midnight and came down the stairs in a T-shirt from the University of Texas with her hair over her face, and her father told her to put on the Dharmavaram silk, the mango-yellow one with the green border that she had worn for her cousin's wedding, and the gold, and to hurry. She laughed at him. Then she saw that he was serious and stopped laughing, and because she was twenty-five and curious and a little afraid of him, she went and put it on. Her mother put on a cotton sari and a cardigan over it. Sarma did not change at all.

It was a quarter past seven. On that street at a quarter past seven the aunties were out in their sneakers doing their morning walk in pairs, and Reddy uncle from the corner house was in his driveway in a bathrobe picking up the Dallas Morning News, and the children were at the corner with their backpacks waiting for the bus, and every one of them saw the Sarmas go by. They saw Madhuri in yellow silk and gold at seven in the morning, holding her pleats up off the sidewalk, and Sarma in front of her walking very fast in his dhoti. Nobody had to ask. By the time the Sarmas reached the end of the street there were thirty people behind them, the walking aunties and Reddy uncle in his bathrobe and two grandmothers visiting from India for the winter and Mrs. Halvorsen from across the street with her dachshund, and half the children, because the bus was late. A concrete crew was unloading at the far end of Phase Four, and the men stopped unloading to watch them come.

The fire had burned down in the copper pot to grey ash with a little red under it. Nobody was there.

Where is he? said Vasundhara.

I told him to wait right here. Sarma turned all the way around, once. Then he pointed down at the sidewalk. There. That's him. That's where he was standing.

In the concrete of the sidewalk, a few feet from the fire, there was a footprint. A bare right foot, a man's, pressed a half-inch deep, every toe and the ball and the arch and the heel, as clean as if someone had set it there with a mold.

The foreman of the concrete crew came over to see what everyone was looking at. He was a short heavy man named Rubén with a tape measure on his belt. He crouched and pressed his thumb on the edge of the print and then knocked on it with his knuckle. That's August, he said. We poured that in August. That's been hard two months. He looked up at them. Nobody steps in that.

The children wanted to put their own feet in it, and their mothers held them back.

Vasundhara bent down, holding the end of her sari against her chest, and looked at the footprint for a long time.

Her grandmother in Bapatla had read feet. When there was a marriage being arranged in the family, the old woman would ask to see the boy walk across the swept courtyard in the morning, and afterward she would look at the dust and say what kind of man he was. She had taught Vasundhara a little of it on the back step, when Vasundhara was a girl, the way you teach a girl to tell good tamarind from bad, and Vasundhara had not thought about it in thirty years.

This is not a man who wants anything, she said.

Vasu.

Look at it. My grandmother used to say, a man full of wanting walks on his toes, because he's always reaching for what's in front of him. You'd see the toes deep and the heel light. An angry man comes down hard on his heels. A confused man drags his feet, you can see where he slid. This one, she said, and touched the edge of it with one finger, put the whole foot down even and lifted it clean. Nothing pulling it forward, nothing holding it back. This is the foot of someone who has put everything down.

Sarma made the noise he always made. For thirty years he had made the same joke to her out of the books he had learned as a boy, about the man in the dark who sees a coil of rope on the path and takes it for a snake.

You see a snake in every rope. I told him I would give him our daughter and he agreed.

He said yes?

He didn't say no.

Emandi, say what you like, she said, and stood up. That is not the foot of a man who wants a wife.

Don't chatter, he said. Come with me and be quiet.

They found him a few hundred yards on, where Bandera Way ran out at the retention pond the builder had dug for the runoff. There was a fountain in the middle of the pond that had not been turned on yet, and a sign that said NO SWIMMING NO FISHING, and the man was sitting on the concrete headwall with his pack beside him and his hands in his lap, looking at the water.

There, Sarma said, and went to him with the whole street behind him. Sir. Here she is. I am giving you my daughter.

The man looked at Sarma, and at Vasundhara, and for a moment at Madhuri in her yellow silk with the gold at her throat, and at the thirty people and the dachshund. Then he looked back at Sarma.

Sarma garu, he said. Before you say that again, there's something I want to tell you. Will you listen to it?

Tell it, said Sarma. I'm listening.

III. The Gate

Richardson, Texas · March 1998

His name was Kiran Vemuri. He had been born in Houston in 1964 to a doctor from Guntur and a mother who taught him the Telugu alphabet at the kitchen table on Saturday mornings, and his grandfather, visiting for a winter when Kiran was nine, had made him learn the story of the boy Nachiketas, who went to the house of Death and would not go home without an answer. Kiran had studied electrical engineering at UT Austin. In 1993, with a friend from school named Danny Ochoa, he had started a company in a strip of offices off Campbell Road in Richardson that made switches for fiber-optic lines, the glass threads the whole world's telephone calls were starting to go through. They called it Ninefold. By 1998 it had two hundred and forty employees and offices in three countries, and in the second week of March the bankers in New York set the date for the public offering.

Seven days. He worked it out on a legal pad one night, alone on the fourth floor. On the day the shares went on sale, on paper, he would be worth more than every person he had grown up with in Houston put together.

He had seen some things that winter. In January he had flown to Houston and sat with his father, who did not know him anymore and asked him politely what he did for a living. In February the head of sales, a man of forty-one with two little girls, had died at his desk on a Tuesday afternoon with his phone in his hand, and nobody noticed for an hour. At the funeral, in a big Methodist church in Plano, Kiran looked at all the people in their good coats and could not make it make sense. And driving home late one night on the Tollway he had seen a man walking on the shoulder in the rain with nothing in his hands and nothing on his back, not hurrying, and had watched him in the mirror until he was gone. It was the only time in years he had wanted to trade places with anybody.

At three in the morning, six nights before the offering, he called Danny at home and asked him to come get him at the office and drive him somewhere.

Danny came in his old Camry, in sweatpants. The garage under the building was empty and the lights hummed. At the exit the gate arm was down, and there was a man in the booth, though there was never anybody in the booth at that hour. The man was in a grey suit, a very good one, and when the Camry pulled up he slid the window open and leaned out with his forearms on the sill, the way a man leans on a fence to talk to a neighbor.

Go back upstairs, Kiran, he said. Seven days. Seven days from now you're going to have everything. You know that.

I know it, Kiran said. I don't want it.

Then where are you going at three in the morning?

To find out.

Find out what?

All of it, Kiran said.

The man looked at him a long time. Then he reached down and the gate arm went up.

Go on, then, he said. But I'll tell you something. The first time you want something, anything, I'm going to know about it. And I'll be right there.

Danny drove south. He drove through Dallas and out past the last of the lights, and somewhere south of Waxahachie Kiran asked him to pull over. On the shoulder he gave Danny his watch and his wallet and his phone and the keys to his condo and a letter for the lawyers, signing his shares over to the company's employees in equal parts, from the receptionist up. Danny cried and begged him and said he was having a breakdown and that they could get him help, and Kiran held Danny's head against his own shoulder for a minute, the way you hold a child's, and then got out and walked.

Danny drove the Camry home. He told people for years that it never ran right after that, that it was broken-hearted, and he sold it that summer for three hundred dollars.

The offering went ahead. Ninefold closed its first day at forty-one dollars a share and two years later touched ninety, and the receptionist bought a house. Then the bubble broke, and by the end of 2001 the stock was worth less than a dollar. Kiran was on foot by then, in the west.

IV. Seven Years

The Edwards Plateau · 1998–2004

The man in the grey suit kept his word. He followed Kiran for seven years.

He was at the counter of a truck stop outside Brownwood in the first summer, eating pie, and he slid a business card down the counter: a firm in Austin that wanted Kiran to run its engineering for any salary he named. He was in the parking lot of a motel in San Angelo the next winter, leaning on a white Lincoln, with a cousin from Guntur Kiran had not seen since childhood who wanted to talk about a girl. He was at a feed store in Sonora. He was on the radio. Once, outside Ozona, in the August heat, he was nothing but a cold bottle of Coca-Cola standing by itself on a fence post with the sweat running down it. Every time, he was waiting for Kiran to want something. Every time, Kiran walked on.

In those years Kiran was very hard on himself. Nobody made him. He thought that was the road. He slept in a borrowed deer blind in Sutton County through one whole winter. He picked pecans, and cleared cedar for ranchers for the price of a meal, and then stopped taking the meal, and then ate one handful of rice a day, and then a handful every other day. He sat in the sun on the limestone until the skin of his face cracked. Sheriff's deputies stopped him on the farm roads, a brown man with a pack in the years after the towers fell, and asked him for identification he did not have, and some of them were kind and some were not, and he sat in the backs of their cars and in their holding cells without complaint until they let him go. By the spring of 2004 he weighed ninety-eight pounds. He could count his own ribs from across a room. His hair came out in his hand.

He fell down one April morning by a fence on a goat ranch outside Rocksprings and could not get up.

The ranch belonged to a widow named Graciela Tijerina, who ran Angora goats for mohair on fourteen hundred acres of rock and live oak, and when she found him by her fence she thought at first he was dead. She went back to the house and came out with arroz con leche in a Cool Whip tub, still warm, with a cinnamon stick in it, and a plastic spoon, and fed him with her own hand. He ate it. It was the first full meal he had eaten in two years, and he felt the strength go down into his arms and legs like water into a dry tank.

Out on Highway 55 the man in the grey suit saw it from the shoulder and laughed out loud. There, he said. There it is. He thought Kiran had given up.

But that was only hunger. Hunger is a different thing.

Below Mrs. Tijerina's house there was a live oak older than Texas, so wide that the whole herd lay down in its shade in the afternoons. Kiran went and sat under it with his back against the trunk. He sat there all that day and all that night, and the goats came and lay down around him and got up and went and came back, and toward morning, with the sky going grey over the plateau and a mockingbird starting up in the cedar, something that had been running in him his whole life finished running. He would never be able to say it any better than that. It stopped, the way a motor stops. He sat under the tree for seven more days, and Mrs. Tijerina brought him a plate every noon, and did not ask him anything, and he ate what she brought.

On the shoulder of Highway 55 the man in the grey suit sat down on the guardrail with his tie pulled loose. He had followed for seven years and found nothing to take hold of, not one thing, and now there was nothing left to follow. He picked up a stick and drew lines in the gravel with it, one line and then another one beside it, and rubbed them out with his shoe and drew them again.

V. Three Sisters

Edwards County · April 2004

He had three daughters.

Kiran told Sarma their names and what the names meant. One was called Thirst. One was called Restless. One was called Heat. They came along the highway in a white car with the windows down, and when they saw their father sitting on the guardrail they pulled onto the shoulder in a spray of gravel and got out.

Daddy, what's the matter with you? Why do you look like that?

He told them. Seven years, and the man under the tree, and not one thing.

Oh, Daddy, they said. Don't you worry about it. We'll go get him and bring him to you.

You can't. Nobody can bring that one anywhere.

Daddy. We're women, they said. Leave him to us.

They came down through the goats to the live oak and stood in front of him in their summer dresses. Let us take care of you, they said. We'll be yours. We'll cook for you, we'll keep house for you, we'll do whatever you say. You'll never have to want for anything.

Kiran did not answer them. He did not open his eyes.

They went off a little way and talked it over. Men like different things, said Thirst. Some like a young girl. Some like a woman. Some like them older. Let's give him all of it and see.

So they came back. Graciela Tijerina saw them from her kitchen window and told it afterward at the Catholic church in Rocksprings, and some people believed her and some did not, and she said she did not care which. She said they came up from the highway in a crowd, too many to count, a hundred and then another hundred, women of every age there is. They came first as girls of sixteen in cutoff jeans. They came as young brides who had never had a baby, with their wedding rings on. They came as young mothers with one baby on the hip, and then with two children by the hand. They came as women of fifty in reading glasses carrying covered dishes, and last of all as old women, white-haired, slow, leaning on canes. Six times they came down through the goats to the tree, and every time they knelt in front of the man sitting under it and said the same thing. Let us take care of you. We'll be yours.

He did not open his eyes. He told Sarma he did not need to.

But they would not go away, and finally he opened his eyes and looked at them, all of them, and he said, Go on home. What are you looking at me for? Go find somebody who still wants something. You can't catch hold of a man who's let go of everything. There's nothing on him to hold.

And then he said to them, he told Sarma, what the boy said to Death in the old story, when Death offered him herds and gold and the whole wide earth, and beautiful women with chariots and music, if he would only stop asking.

These things last till to-morrow, O Death, for they wear out this vigour of all the senses. Even the whole of life is short. Keep thou thy horses, keep dance and song for thyself.

— Katha Upanishad 1:26

Sarma's lips had been moving for a while. He had learned the Katha by heart at twelve in his father's house, in Sanskrit, sitting cross-legged on a mat, and he had chanted it at a thousand ceremonies since, and he went on with it now without meaning to, out loud, the verses from near the end where the teaching stops. He said them in Sanskrit and then, because the walking aunties and Reddy uncle and the children and Mrs. Halvorsen were all looking at him, he said them again in English the way he had first read them as a student, in the old Oxford translation, in the library at College Station, in another life.

When all desires that dwell in his heart cease, then the mortal becomes immortal, and obtains Brahman. When all the ties of the heart are severed here on earth, then the mortal becomes immortal—here ends the teaching.

— Katha Upanishad 6:14–15

He stopped. He had said those words, he thought, ten thousand times. He was hearing them now for the first time.

Mrs. Tijerina said that when she looked out the window again, the women were gone. All of them, every age, and no car on the highway in either direction as far as she could see, and no man sitting on the guardrail. She said the goats were lying down around the tree as if nothing had happened, and the dogs were asleep on the porch, and the whole ranch was quieter than she had ever heard it, so quiet she could hear the windmill turning on the far side of the hill. She said she knew then she had been feeding somebody, she was not sure who.

VI. The Sole of the Foot

The retention pond · the same morning

Kiran was quiet for a moment. The fountain in the pond was not running. Somebody's sprinklers had come on behind them, ticking.

Sarma garu, he said. When those three came to me, they looked like gold. They looked like nothing you ever saw. And I didn't want them, not for one second. He looked at Madhuri. He did not look at her unkindly. He looked at her the way the foreman had looked at the footprint. Your daughter is beautiful. Everybody here can see that. But a body is a bag. It's skin over what's in a body. Blood and bile and phlegm and what the bowels carry, all of it. If my foot were dirty and she were lying across my doorway, I would not touch her with the sole of it.

Every Telugu person at that pond drew in a breath at the same time. You do not touch a person with your foot. A child learns it before it can walk. If your foot so much as brushes someone by accident on a crowded bus, you touch their arm and then your own eyes, to take it back. Two of the aunties put their hands over their mouths. One of the grandmothers visiting from India began, very quietly, to pray.

Vasundhara looked at her daughter.

Madhuri had gone perfectly still. Her face did not change, and that was how her mother knew. It went still the way milk goes still in the pan just before it turns. She stood in her yellow silk with her mother's gold at her throat and looked at the man on the headwall, and then she gathered her pleats up off the concrete in one hand and turned and walked back up Bandera Way alone, through the crowd, which parted for her. When she came to the footprint in the sidewalk she did not step on it. She stepped around it, wide, as if it were a hole.

Sarma sat down on the headwall beside the man. He sat down heavily, like somebody whose legs had been taken out from under him, in his dhoti and his windbreaker with the ghee still shining on his fingers, and he did not say anything for a long time. Vasundhara watched him. She had been married to him for twenty-seven years. She had watched him at a hundred kitchen tables turn a stranger's photograph to the light and look for what was wrong with it. She watched his face now and saw that he was not looking for anything.

Something opened in her too, standing there. She could not have said what. She thought of her grandmother on the back step in Bapatla, reading the dust. A whole foot set down even and lifted clean.

Kiran picked up his pack and put it on. He touched Sarma once on the shoulder. Then he walked out along the edge of the pond to where the frontage road ran south, and down the frontage road, not hurrying, and the people of Calder Court watched him go until the light came up behind the billboards and they could not see him anymore.

VII. Phase Four

Frisco · 2005–2011

Madhuri asked her mother's brother in Edison, New Jersey, to find her a husband, and he did it in five months. She married a man in Saratoga, California, who had sold a software company to a bigger one for more money than any boy her father had ever turned down, and the wedding was at a hotel in San Jose with eleven hundred guests and two priests flown in from Tirupati. Her father gave her away and wept. She never came home to Frisco again without asking her mother on the phone, first, in a voice that was very light, whether that man had been seen. She kept what he had said the way some people keep a coin from a country they will never go back to, in a drawer, and take it out and turn it over.

Sarma retired the next spring. He lit his fire every morning until the end, at the end of the street, but those who walked past said he did it differently. He did it the way you rinse a cup. In 2006 the market came back and the builder came back with it, and the first slab went down for a house on the corner lot of Calder Court, right where he had kept his fire, and that week he sold the big house on the cul-de-sac. He and Vasundhara gave away most of what was in it. He fed the drawer full of biodatas into a shredder, one by one. Then they went back to his father's village on the Krishna delta and lived there in two rooms by the canal, the way the old books say people should when their children are grown, at the edge of things. Vasundhara wrote to her friends in Frisco for a few years. In the last letter anybody remembers she said that Emandi had stopped telling her she saw snakes in ropes, and that neither of them saw much of anything in anything anymore, and that it was very peaceful.

Phase Four filled in. By 2011 there were brick houses on every lot, with live oaks staked in the yards, and a family from Lagos on the corner of Calder Court and a family from Ohio next door, and the fountain in the pond ran all day. The builder had wanted to grind the footprint out of the sidewalk before the houses sold, and then a man from the homeowners' association wanted to, and in the end nobody did.

The children of Phase Four still put their feet in it, on the way to the bus. It is the first thing a new child on that street is shown. Every one of them tries it, left foot and then right, and their fathers try it, and nobody's foot has ever fit. After a rain it holds water, and you can stand over it and see the sky in it, and your own face.

There is only the one. People have walked that whole sidewalk looking, and all the sidewalks after it, out to the frontage road. Nobody has ever found the next.

The verses

He whose conquest is not conquered again, into whose conquest no one in this world enters, by what track can you lead him, the Awakened, the Omniscient, the trackless?Dhammapada 179, tr. F. Max Müller (1881)
He whom no desire with its snares and poisons can lead astray, by what track can you lead him, the Awakened, the Omniscient, the trackless?Dhammapada 180, tr. F. Max Müller (1881)

In the commentary, a Brahmin named Māgandiya refuses every suitor for his beautiful daughter as not good enough. One morning, at the place where he tends his sacred fire, he meets the Buddha and offers her to him, and runs home to fetch her. When the family returns with a crowd behind them, the Buddha has moved on, leaving a single footprint. The Brahmin's wife, who knows the lore of bodily signs, reads it as the foot of a man free of desire; her husband tells her she sees a crocodile in a drop of water. When they find him, the Buddha tells the story of his Great Renunciation: how Māra met him at the city gate and promised him a world empire in seven days, followed him for seven years, and at last sat defeated by the road, until his three daughters, Taṇhā, Aratī and Ragā (Craving, Discontent and Lust), came to tempt the Buddha in the forms of women of every age. He sends them away with these two verses. Then he tells the Brahmin that he would not touch his daughter's body even with his foot, a remark the daughter never forgives. North Texas and the Edwards Plateau, 1998–2004

Frisco, north of Dallas, was the fastest-growing city in the United States from 2000 to 2009, as subdivisions spread north from Plano onto blackland prairie. It grew as a bedroom community for professionals who worked across the Dallas–Fort Worth area. Telugu Americans, from Andhra Pradesh and Telangana, came first as students, doctors and scientists, and then, from the late 1980s, in a large wave of software engineers that grew around the turn of the century. The dot-com bubble that made and unmade fortunes in those years peaked in March 2000; by October 2002 the Nasdaq had lost 78 percent of its value, and many telecommunications companies went under with it. Sarma's morning fire is the agnihotra, the Vedic offering of ghee into the fire at sunrise and sunset, which the heads of Brahmin households were once obliged to make every day. Vasundhara's grandmother practices Sāmudrika Śāstra, the old Indian lore of reading character and fate from the marks of the body.

The Katha Upanishad tells of the boy Nachiketas, who goes to the house of Death and asks what happens after dying. Death tries to buy him off with herds, gold, long life, a kingdom and beautiful women, and the boy refuses all of it. The verses here are from F. Max Müller's translation in volume 15 of the Sacred Books of the East (1884); Müller numbered the Upanishad's six sections straight through, so his “6, 14” is often cited elsewhere as 2.3.14. Kiran's ranch is on the Edwards Plateau near Rocksprings, the seat of Edwards County, named for the springs in its limestone, which calls itself the Angora goat capital of the world.
