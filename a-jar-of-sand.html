I. The Black Bird

A rancho on the road down to Stockton · August 1850

That summer three parties of Chileans set out, each without knowing of the others, to see ño Bartolo at San Francisco. The first was five men walking down out of the southern mines. That spring the Americans had put a tax of twenty dollars a month on every foreigner who dug, and these five had paid it once and could not pay it twice.

Two days above Stockton they came to a rancho where a Sonoran woman fed travelers under a ramada thatched with tule. She would not take their money. She sat them in the shade and gave them beans and tortillas, and while the meat was frying she asked for the news from the mines, and they gave her all they had.

The fat caught. The flame went up the post and into the tule faster than anyone could stand. It was the hour when the wind comes up the valley, and the wind took one whole bundle of the thatch off the roof, burning, and carried it out over the yard as high as a mast.

A black bird was crossing just then. They had no such bird at home. In two years none of them had found a better name for it than cuervo, out of the story of Noah. It was flying straight and low the way those birds do, going somewhere. It put its neck directly into the burning bundle. The tule closed around it. For a moment it was still flying, and then it came down in the middle of the yard, all black, and smoking.

The five men stood up from the table. The woman was beating at the ramada with a wet sack, and they helped her. Afterward they went and looked at the bird.

The whole sky to fly in, said the oldest, a man named Olegario. What did it do, to die like that?

Who would know?

Ño Bartolo would know.

They had meant to ask him only whether to go home.

II. Dead Calm

The bark Carmen, eleven degrees north · June 1850

The second party came by sea. The bark Carmen was forty days out of Valparaíso with ninety passengers and a cargo of flour when the wind left her. For nineteen days she lay in the same water. The sails hung. The pitch came up soft between the deck planks, and the water in the casks went down to a hand’s depth and was served out by the cup. Sharks lay in the shadow of the hull the whole time and did not trouble to move.

By the twelfth day the passengers were saying that somebody on board was unlucky. By the nineteenth they had a hat and ninety-odd dry beans in it, all white but one.

The captain was a man named Rozas, and he had his wife aboard. She was nineteen. He had married her in March and would not leave her behind. Her name was Mercedes, and she was the prettiest thing any of them had seen since the hills above the harbor went under the horizon. She put her hand in the hat with the rest, and her hand came out with the black bean.

Again, the passengers said, and nobody argued. They put the bean back and shook the hat. She drew it a second time. They shook it a third time, and held it higher, and she reached up and drew it again.

Then they went aft in a body and looked Captain Rozas in the face.

Well, patrón?

He was a long time answering.

It is not right that ninety should die for one, he said. Put her over.

When they laid hands on her she screamed. The captain shut his eyes at it. Then he opened them and spoke again.

Wait. There is no sense in her gold going down with her. Take the earrings off, and the chain, and the rings. Wrap her in a piece of sailcloth. And I will not watch her fight on top of the water. Tie something to her neck that will take her straight down.

There was sand in the hold for ballast. They filled one of the earthen jars the wine had come in, a botija, and stopped it, and made it fast around her neck with a length of line. They did what he said in the order he said it. She went under at once, and the sharks came out of the shadow of the hull.

The wind came three days later, as wind does.

Among the ninety were a baker from the Almendral named Tadeo Briones, and his brother, and a laundress called Rosa who had held the hat the third time. On the day the Carmen anchored in the cove among the rotting ships, these three did not go up to the mines with the others. They had begun asking themselves a question at night.

What had she done? Rosa said. A girl like that. What could she have done?

Somebody on the beach told them there was an old man under the hill who weighed gold for the Chileans and knew the reasons of things.

III. Seven Sleeping Places

A limestone gulch in Calaveras County · July 1850

The third party was seven men from the valley of the Choapa, above Illapel. They had come up as peons for a patrón who had since died of fever, and they had worked their way from camp to camp with one wooden batea among them. They also were going down to San Francisco to see ño Bartolo, who was from their country.

One evening they came to a camp of Sonorans in a gulch, and asked for a place to lie. The Sonorans kept a cave for people passing through. It was dry, and there was a shelf at the back with room for exactly seven men to sleep side by side. The seven thanked them and went in and slept.

In the night a rock as big as a chapel came down the opposite slope. It rolled across the gulch and stopped in the mouth of the cave like a stopper in a jar.

The Sonorans found it at daylight. We gave them that cave, they said. We will get them out. They sent up and down the country, and men came from seven camps. There were Chileans and Sonorans and Frenchmen, and a Kanaka sailor with a ship’s tackle, and a dozen of the same Americans who had gone round in May with the tax collector. They brought bars and mules and powder. They dug under it and it settled. They blasted it and it blackened. The seven inside pushed until their shoulders bled, and the hundred outside hauled, and the rock did not move the width of a finger.

That went on for seven days. There was a drip of water at the back of the cave, so the seven lived. They had nothing to eat. By the fifth day they had stopped talking, and lay on the shelf in the dark with their knees drawn up.

On the seventh day, at about noon, the rock rolled away. Nobody was touching it. The men outside were sitting down to eat. It turned over once, slowly, and went on down the gulch and lay still. The seven came out into the light on their hands and knees.

When they could stand, the one called Lindor said what they were all thinking.

That was meant for us. For what, I do not know.

They met the five from the mines on the Stockton road, and the three from the ship on the beach at San Francisco. All three parties went up under the hill together.

IV. The Scale

Chilecito, under Telegraph Hill · the end of August

The Chileans lived in tents and board shanties at the foot of Telegraph Hill, and the Americans called the place Little Chile. Bartolo Araya had a tent there with a table in it. He was past seventy. He had gone down the silver mines of Atacama as a boy with a hide sack on his back, and afterward he had spent forty years alone in that desert as a cateador, looking for veins. Now he kept a scale. The storekeepers’ scales in San Francisco were known to lie, so the Chileans brought their dust to him. He weighed it for nothing, wrote the weight on a paper, and put his mark under it.

He was weighing when they came in, fifteen people into a tent made for four. He finished. Then he heard the five, and the three, and the seven, in that order. He did not interrupt any of them. Rosa was the only one who wept.

He turned to the five first.

In Colchagua there was a man who had a young ox. It would not take the yoke. It went a little way and lay down. He beat it, and it got up and went a little way and lay down again. He was a whole day at it. At the end of the day he said, Very well, lie there as long as you like. He went and got wheat straw. He bound it around the ox from head to tail the way you bind a sheaf, and he put fire to it.

And the bird was that man? said Olegario.

My grandmother would say he has been that bird seven times. I only keep the scale. I am telling you what was put in one pan.

He turned to the three from the ship.

There was a young wife who did all the work of her house with her own hands. She had a dog. It sat and watched her grind and cook, and when she went for wood or water it went behind her. One day some boys saw the two of them on the road and called out, Here comes the hunter with her hound, tonight we eat meat. She was ashamed. She beat the dog with sticks and stones to drive it home. It ran off a little and came back, because it could not do otherwise. That afternoon she took a rope and an empty botija and went down to a pool. She filled the jar with sand. The dog came up to her wagging. It had not had a good word from her all day. She tied one end of the rope to the jar and the other around its neck, and she rolled the jar down the bank into the water.

Nobody spoke. Then Tadeo Briones, the baker, said slowly, The cook had a yellow dog on the ship. It followed her about. She could not have it near her. She would go white, and call for somebody to tie it.

Bartolo did not answer that. He looked at the seven.

You are from the Choapa. When you were boys you kept the goats, a week each, turn about.

The seven looked at one another. It was Lindor who said it.

The lizard.

Then they told it themselves, all seven, each taking it from the last. They had been coming home with the goats and had started a lizard as long as a man’s forearm. It outran them and went into an old heap of stones. The heap had seven gaps in it. They said, We will get him tomorrow, and each boy took a fistful of thorn twigs and stopped one gap. The next day they took the goats up the other side of the valley, and they forgot. On the seventh day they came by the heap and remembered. They pulled out the twigs. The lizard came out so thin they could see the light through it. It was shaking, and it did not seem to care what they did. One of them said, Do not kill it, it has had nothing for seven days. They stroked its back with one finger and said, Go with God.

You let it go, said Bartolo. So the rock let you go.

The tent was quiet. The scale stood on the table between them with its pans hanging level.

Taita, said the baker. If a man has done a thing like that, is there nowhere he can get clear of it? If he went far enough?

You went to the middle of the sea, Bartolo said. These went inside a mountain. That bird had the whole sky. There is a saying older than I am.

No hay plazo que no llegue ni deuda que no se pague.

— Refranes de la lengua castellana (Barcelona, 1815)

No term fails to come due, and no debt goes unpaid. And he said the other one, the one the crier calls in the street when a man is led out to be punished: Quien tal hace, que tal pague. Who does it, pays the same.

And us? said Rosa. I held the hat. And the captain, with her earrings in his pocket?

It is one scale, said Bartolo. It does not tell me the day.

They went out one at a time. It is said of those fifteen that none of them afterward lifted a hand against anything living, and that the baker fed whoever came to his oven door.

The cook’s yellow dog had come ashore from the Carmen with nobody, and had followed Rosa up from the beach. It was sitting by the tent pole. She stood and looked at it for some time. Then she broke her bread and held out the half of it, and when she went up the hill it went behind her.

Bartolo put the weights back in their box, the smallest last. The pans swung a little each time someone walked past the tent, and came back to level.

Background

The verse

Not in the sky, not in the midst of the sea, not if we enter into the clefts of the mountains, is there known a spot in the whole world where a man might be freed from an evil deed.Dhammapada 127, tr. F. Max Müller (1881), as printed; the Project Gutenberg text drops this ending and runs the verse into 128

In the commentary, three parties of monks travel to see the Buddha. The first, eating in a village, sees a kitchen fire catch the thatch; a burning bundle of grass floats into the air and a crow flies into it and is burned. The second is aboard a ship that stops dead in mid-ocean. The passengers cast lots for the unlucky one, and three times the lot falls on the captain’s young wife; the captain has her jewels removed and a jar of sand tied to her neck so that he need not watch her struggle, and she is thrown overboard. The third party, seven monks, is shut in a cave for seven days by a fallen rock that no one can move and that at last rolls away by itself. The Buddha explains each by an old deed: the crow was a farmer who burned a balky ox in straw; the woman had drowned her faithful dog with a pot of sand; the seven monks, as cowherd boys, had stopped up a lizard in an anthill for seven days and then let it go. Asked whether there is anywhere a person can escape a deed, he speaks the verse. Read the full commentary story.

Little Chile, 1850

News of California gold reached Valparaíso in 1848, and Chileans were among the first to arrive by sea. Many were experienced miners; they brought the wooden gold pan, the batea, and the ore-crushing “Chili mill.” Chilean companies often worked claims with hired peons, which American miners resented. In San Francisco they settled at the foot of Telegraph Hill in a quarter called Chilecito, Little Chile, which a nativist gang, the Hounds, attacked in July 1849. In 1850 the new state passed the Foreign Miners’ Tax of twenty dollars a month, and many Chileans and Mexicans left the diggings. The limestone country of Calaveras County, where Chili Gulch keeps the Chileans’ name, is full of caves; one near Cave City was already being shown to visitors in 1850. The Carmen, her captain, the rancho and everyone in the story are invented.

There are no crows or ravens native to South America, so the bird was new to these men. The proverb ño Bartolo quotes is given as it stands in the anonymous Refranes de la lengua castellana printed at Barcelona in 1815. The second saying, the old formula of the public crier, closes Tirso de Molina’s El burlador de Sevilla (about 1630), where the stone guest speaks it twice. Both are quoted in the original Spanish; the English beside them is this story’s own.
