Castle Rock

Dweller and dwelling are rivals in impermanence, both are fleeting as the dewdrop that hangs on the petals of the morning-glory.
— Kamo no Chōmei, Hōjōki

Nyoze gamon, in Block 54.

I. The Laundry Room

Tule Lake Segregation Center, Newell, California · January 1946

The war had been over for five months, and the camp was emptying the way a lake goes down in a dry year, from the edges in. Whole blocks stood shut. The stoves were cold in barracks where families had lived for three years, and the wind came down off the Oregon line and blew the tarpaper loose and nobody nailed it back. The ones still inside the wire were the ones the government had not decided about yet: men who had answered no on the questionnaire, families who had come from other camps to stay together, young people who had signed away their citizenship in the winter of 1944 and 1945 and now wanted it back and had a lawyer from San Francisco trying to get it for them. Hearings were held. Lists were posted. Some names went on a list for a ship to Japan, and some went on a list for the Justice Department camp at Crystal City, Texas, and some were let go.

Hirasaka-sensei’s name came through on the twenty-eighth of January. He had been the priest of the Buddhist church in Florin, in the strawberry country south of Sacramento, for nineteen years before the war, and of the Buddhist church in Block 4 at Tule Lake for three and a half. He was sixty-one. He had a soft voice and very little hair and a black robe that his wife had turned twice, and he said the Nembutsu so quietly that people sitting in the back of the mess hall on Sundays could only tell he was saying it by watching his mouth.

That evening after service he stood up at the front, where the altar was a packing crate draped in purple cloth with the scroll hung above it, and said that he had been released, that he was going home to Florin to open the temple again, and that anyone else whose papers had come through might go with him. The train would be on the eleventh of February. He sat down. That was the announcement.

Eleven people had their papers. All the next week they got ready. They built crates out of scrap lumber from the empty blocks and stenciled their names on the sides with shoe blacking. They took down the curtains they had sewn from mattress ticking and folded them into the crates. The Yamane brothers sold their radio. Mrs. Okada gave away her potted geraniums one by one to the women who were staying, with instructions.

Goro Tanikawa’s papers had come through with the rest. He was forty-one, born in Florin, sent to Hiroshima at six to be raised by his grandparents and sent back at sixteen with no English, so that he spoke a careful Japanese the young people laughed at and a careful English the old people could not follow. He had worked the coal crew all three years at Tule Lake, shoveling coal off the cars at the siding and carrying it block to block in winter, and he wore the same navy pea coat from the clothing allowance that he had been issued in 1943, patched at both elbows with feed sacking, and slept in it in the cold months. He ate whatever the mess hall gave him and did not go back for more. When the other men on the crew sat down in the lee of the coal pile to smoke, he went on shoveling, and when anybody asked him why, he said the coal did not know it was break time.

He did not build a crate. On the Saturday before the train, while the others were nailing lids down, he was in the laundry barrack of Block 54 with his sleeves pushed up, washing his two shirts and his other pair of trousers in the concrete tub, and his socks, and the pea coat, which took the whole afternoon and an inch of the soap.

Look at him, said Shig Yamane, at the window. He’s not going anywhere.

It was not an unkind thing to say. Everybody knew the Tanikawas. Goro’s mother was in Block 54, and his three sisters and their husbands, and nine nieces and nephews, and his father’s cousin’s family from Elk Grove, and two old aunts who had come from Rohwer in Arkansas so as to be with the rest. They filled four barracks. One of his sisters’ husbands had renounced in 1945 and was on the list for Crystal City, and the sister was going with him, and the children. The mother was sixty-nine and had a cough that did not go away.

He’ll walk us to the gate, Shig said. That’s as far as he’ll get.

He’ll get as far as Castle Rock, said his brother. Then he’ll remember something.

II. The Gate

February 11, 1946

The trucks came for the crates at seven and for the people at nine. It was very cold and very clear. Out past the fences the old lake bed lay flat and white with frost to the bluff of Castle Rock, which stood up from the plain like the prow of a ship, and on top of it, small and black, the cross the first people in the camp had carried up in 1942, before Tule Lake was made the place for the disloyal.

Goro walked out with the others. He carried a cloth bundle about the size of a bed pillow, which was everything he had, and his mother walked beside him as far as the administration building and then stopped, and he bowed to her, and she went back.

At the gate the soldier checked their names against a sheet on a clipboard. Hirasaka-sensei waited until all the names had been checked. Then he turned around to Goro.

There will be funerals, he said. There are old people here who will not see the end of this. Somebody has to say the sutra for them, and they would rather it were somebody who knew them. On the fifteenth it is Nehan-e. Somebody has to put out the flowers. When the Crystal City list goes, somebody has to be at the train. He looked past Goro at the long rows of tarpaper going back toward Block 54. Most of the people who are left in there are yours. Go back and stay with them until it closes.

Goro bowed and went back through the gate with his bundle. He did not say anything. He did not look back at the trucks, and the soldier wrote something on the clipboard.

In the truck Shig Yamane leaned over to his brother.

What did I tell you.

Hirasaka-sensei heard him. He did not say anything until the truck had turned onto the highway toward the train and the camp had gone down behind the frost into a long gray line. Then he said, not loudly, Shig-kun, what is in his bundle?

Shig did not know.

Two shirts, two pairs of trousers, the socks, a razor, the sutra book I gave him when he was nine, and that coat. He washed them because he was ready to go. He went back because I asked him to. The old man took his gloves off and folded them. If I had asked you, you would have gone back. You would have gone back angry. He went back the way he would have come.

Nobody in the truck said anything for a while.

When he was nine years old, the sensei said, before I ever came to this country, he was in the Sunday school at my father’s temple in Hiroshima, and I asked the children what they wanted to be. The others said a doctor, a farmer, a baseball player. Goro said he wanted to be the moon. I asked him why. He said, Because the moon goes into everybody’s house and nobody owns it. He smiled. I thought that was a strange thing for a child to say. I have been watching him go in and out of his family’s rooms for three years now, and he goes in like the moon going into a bucket of water. It is in there. It is not wet.

III. Swans

The Southern Pacific, north along the lake · the same day

North of the camp the railroad ran up the east side of the old lake bed toward Klamath Falls, and off to the west, a mile or two across the fields, lay the open water that was left of Tule Lake, where the refuge was. It had a skin of ice at the margins and open blue in the middle. The swans were still on it. There were hundreds of them, white on the blue, so many that from the train they looked like a long drift of snow that had not yet melted. Every winter of the camp they had come down in the fall, and every spring the people had stood at the north fence to watch them go.

As the train came even with them the swans began to lift. They did not all go at once. A few at a time they ran along the water on their black feet, beating, and pulled up into the air with their necks out straight, and turned north, calling to each other in a high soft voice like people far off talking, and the others followed. They did not circle over the lake. They did not look back at the water. They had eaten there all winter, and slept there, and it had held them up, and they went.

The Yamane brothers had their faces at the window. Mrs. Okada held her last geranium on her lap.

Hirasaka-sensei watched the swans with the others until they were gone. Then he took out of his coat pocket a thin paperbound book, the one he carried everywhere, the account of a ten-foot-square hut that a court poet had built for himself on a hillside outside Kyoto more than seven hundred years before, after the fires and the famine and the earthquake had taught him what a house was. Most of the older people in the car had learned the first lines of it in school, in Japan or in the Japanese school at Florin on Saturdays. The sensei did not read the first lines. He turned to the end and read the place where the old poet, very content in his hut at last, catches himself being content.

What the Buddha has taught to men is this—Thou shalt not cleave to any of the things of this world. So ’tis a sin even to grow fond of this straw-thatched cabin, and to find happiness in this life of peace is a hindrance to salvation.

— Kamo no Chōmei, Hōjōki XVII, tr. Minakata Kumagusu and F. Victor Dickins (1905)

He built that hut so he could take it apart, the sensei said. Two carts would carry it. He did not fasten it to the ground. He put the book away. He still had to tell himself not to love it.

Shig Yamane looked out the window a long time. There was nothing on the lake by then but the ice at the edges and the blue water and a few feathers going round in the current.

IV. Not Fastened

Tule Lake · March 1946

Goro said the sutra at four funerals in February and March. One of them was his father’s cousin from Elk Grove. He put out the flowers for Nehan-e on the fifteenth, paper chrysanthemums the women folded from old mess-hall menus because there was nothing growing yet, and he swept the Block 4 church every morning after the coal was in, even after there was no one left to come to it but his mother and the two aunts from Rohwer.

On the nineteenth of March the last renunciants went to the train for Crystal City, four hundred of them and their families, and his sister and her husband and their children with them. He carried his sister’s crate. He stood at the siding until the train had gone south out of sight, as the sensei had asked, and then he walked back.

The camp closed the next day. He left with his mother and the aunts on the last trucks. He had his bundle, and the coat, which he had washed again.

The next year the government began giving the barracks away. Veterans who had drawn homesteads in the old lake bed, in a lottery held at the armory in Klamath Falls the week before Christmas, came with flatbed trucks and jacks and took them in halves and thirds, a barrack to a family, and set them down out on the bare reclaimed ground among the potato fields to live in while they built houses. For a year and more the road out of Newell was full of them, long tarpaper buildings riding north and west on trucks, windows rattling, the numbers still painted on their ends.

The Block 4 church went to a man from Merrill who had been in the Navy. He told people afterward that it had come apart easier than any building he had ever seen, as if it had never been nailed down, and that when he got it set on blocks on his place and went in to sweep it out, there was nothing to sweep. The floor was clean to the boards. Somebody had left a broom standing in the corner, and on the wall above where the altar had been, a pale square where a scroll had hung, and he never could get the paint to cover it.

Background

The verse

They depart with their thoughts well-collected, they are not happy in their abode; like swans who have left their lake, they leave their house and home.Dhammapada 91, tr. F. Max Müller (1881)

In the commentary, the Buddha announces at Rājagaha that he will set out on a journey in two weeks. While the other monks fire their bowls and dye their robes, Mahā Kassapa, the elder most given to austerities, washes his robes, and the others say he will never leave a city so full of his relatives and supporters; he will go no farther than a nearby cave. On the road the Buddha does send him back, because someone must stay for the city's festivals and misfortunes, and most of the city are Kassapa's people. Kassapa turns back without a word. When the monks say they were right, the Buddha tells them he went back out of obedience, not attachment: he goes among households like the moon, and long ago he had wished for exactly that. He is like a royal swan that leaves a lake and does not look back. Read the full commentary story.

Tule Lake, 1946

Tule Lake, on the old lake bed just south of the Oregon line, opened in May 1942 as one of the ten camps where the government held Japanese Americans removed from the West Coast. In July 1943 it became the segregation center for people judged disloyal, many for their answers to the loyalty questionnaire, along with family members who chose to come with them; it got a double fence, twenty-eight guard towers and a stockade. After a 1944 law made it possible to renounce citizenship in wartime, and the government announced that the camps would close, thousands at Tule Lake renounced, many of them under pressure from inside and outside the fence. In November 1945 the San Francisco lawyer Wayne Collins won a court order that stopped mass deportations two days before a ship was to sail, and hearings followed. On March 19, 1946, the last 406 renunciants and 43 family members went by train to the Justice Department camp at Crystal City, Texas, and the next day Tule Lake, the last of the camps, closed. Its barracks were given away and sold to the homesteaders, many of them veterans chosen by lottery, who settled the reclaimed lake bed. It is now part of Tule Lake National Monument. Collins's lawsuits took until 1968 to restore citizenship to nearly five thousand people.

What was left of the lake became the Tule Lake National Wildlife Refuge in 1928; tundra swans are common there in winter and pass through in early spring and late fall. The book the priest reads is the Hōjōki, finished in 1212 by Kamo no Chōmei, a poet who had lived through the fire, whirlwind, famine and earthquake that struck Kyoto in the 1170s and 1180s and retired to a hut ten feet square that he could take apart and carry away in two carts. The English here is the 1905 translation by Minakata Kumagusu and F. Victor Dickins. Shin Buddhist temples keep Nehan-e, the memorial of the Buddha's passing, on February 15.
