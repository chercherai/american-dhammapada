Walking the American WayCommentary 16.8 · Anāgāmitthera

White River

I do not say that I have already won the race or have already reached perfection.
— Philippians 3:12

Here is how it was told, in the kitchens on Jackson Street.

I. The Boarder

Muncie, Indiana · November 1924

The Keevers took a boarder that fall for the money, the way half of Jackson Street did, and because Mrs. Keever's husband had been dead four years and the house had a front bedroom nobody slept in. The boarder was a Miss Agnes Thayer from New York. She was twenty-six and wore her hair bobbed and her skirts at the shin, and she came with the people from Columbia University who were studying the town.

Nobody on Jackson Street could make out what there was to study. Muncie was Muncie. It had the Ball jar works and the Warner Gear and the wire mill and forty-some churches, and it had had gas once, when the boom was on, enough to burn flambeaux on the street corners day and night, and then the gas ran out and the jar works stayed. In the summer the Klan had paraded down Walnut Street in their robes, and a good many men from the churches had marched in it. But the people from Columbia were all over town that winter with notebooks. They sat in the back of the churches and the Rotary lunches. They went into the kitchens of the wives of men at the Ball works and the wives of men at the bank and asked them what time they got up, how often they washed, whether they had a bathtub, what they read, how much the car had cost. Mrs. Keever said if a woman came into her kitchen and asked her how often she washed she would tell her Monday, the same as God intended, and show her the door.

But Miss Thayer paid four dollars a week in advance and made her own bed, and Mrs. Keever came round to her.

Her father was a Congregational minister in Rutland, Vermont. She said it at supper the first night, the way a person says where they were born, and did not say anything else about it, and she did not go to church on Sundays except for the work, when she sat in the back and wrote. She kept a little blue Weymouth Testament on her dresser anyway. Mrs. Keever saw it when she changed the towels.

There was one other person in the house, and that was Grandfather Keever.

He was eighty-one. He had been a Methodist Episcopal preacher in the North Indiana Conference for forty-four years, on circuits in Jay County and Randolph County and Delaware County, and had married some of the fathers of the men now running the town and buried most of their grandfathers. He had been superannuated since 1912. He lived in the back bedroom off the kitchen, over the cistern, because the stairs were too much for him. He had not gone downtown to see the Klan go by. He was thin and very clean and very quiet, and he read his Bible and the Indianapolis Star, and every morning of the year, unless there was ice, he walked out to the White River and along it upstream as far as the Walnut Street bridge and back.

Miss Thayer had a list of old people she was supposed to see, to find out what the town had been like in 1890. He was on it. She took her notebook back to his room after supper and they talked by the stove for six evenings in November, about the revivals of the eighties and the gas boom, about the class meetings and the love feasts and the little printed tickets the class leader used to hand out each quarter to show you were in good standing, which nobody gave anymore. He kept his in a White Owl cigar box, sixty years of them. He let her see them.

II. The Question

Jackson Street · Christmas 1924

Paul Keever came home from Taylor University for Christmas, and he brought his roommate, Harold Tull, from up by Fort Wayne, whose people could not have him that year.

Taylor was a holiness college up in Upland, an hour north, Methodist in everything but the deed, and the old holiness was still strong there. The boys had been to the meetings that fall. They had gone to the altar, both of them, and come away believing they had been sanctified wholly, that the second blessing had been given and the root of sin taken out of them. They talked about it at supper, Paul with his ears red and Harold steadily, and Mrs. Keever said that was very nice and passed the potatoes.

On Christmas afternoon, while she did the dishes and Miss Thayer dried, the boys went back to the old man's room and asked him.

It was a fair question among Methodists. It had been put to him once, in public, when he was a young man standing before the bishop to be taken into full connection, in the words Mr. Wesley had written: Are you going on to perfection? Do you expect to be made perfect in love in this life? He had said yes and yes. Now his grandson stood in front of him with a Taylor pennant on the wall behind him and asked him the next thing.

Grandfather, have you received it? Are you made perfect in love?

The old man looked at them a long time. Then he looked at the stove.

He was thinking of Mother Lykins. She had been in his Albany class in 1874. She took in washing for the railroad men and could not read, and she had stood up at a love feast in a brush arbor with her red hands folded and said that God had taken everything out of her heart but love, and nobody in that arbor doubted it, and nobody who knew her afterward ever had cause to. There had been a good many like her on his circuits. Women mostly. A hired man in Randolph County. A girl of fourteen who died of the lung fever. People who had it plain and said so in four words and went back to the wash.

What was there in that for a preacher to make a speech about? Any washerwoman in Albany had it. A man who had been forty-four years in the ministry ought to have something more to show before he stood up and talked about himself. Mr. Wesley had said there was no perfection in this life that did not admit of a continual increase. Let him get farther along. When he was finished, he would tell them.

So he said nothing. He was shy of it, the way an old man is shy of a new suit.

After a while Harold said, Well, and Paul said, Yes sir, and they went out, and he heard them on the back porch talking low, and then he heard them go off down the street toward Walnut to see the pictures.

III. Upstream

The White River · February 1925

He took cold in the middle of January, walking. The river was open in the middle and shelved with ice along the banks, and he went out anyway, as far as the bridge, and came back with his face grey. Dr. Bowles came twice. It went into his chest the first week of February, and on the night of the eleventh, with Mrs. Keever on one side of the bed and Miss Thayer on the other, because Miss Thayer had been sitting up with him nights and would not let anyone else take her turn, he died.

He did not say anything at the end. He looked at the window and breathed out and that was all.

They buried him from High Street. The church was full of old people from the country who had driven in on the frozen roads in Model T's and in a few buggies, and men from the Ball works who had been married by him, and the district superintendent preached, and the choir sang O for a heart to praise my God. Paul and Harold came down on the train. They sat in the front pew with Mrs. Keever and wept through the whole of it, and that night, in the kitchen, they were weeping still.

Mrs. Keever told them to stop it. He was eighty-one, she said. He was ready. Everybody knew it was coming.

We know that, Paul said. That isn't it.

It isn't that he died, Harold said. We asked him. At Christmas we asked him plain if he had it, and he never said. He never said one word. Now he's gone and we'll never know if he got there.

They sat at the kitchen table with their faces in their hands, the two young men who had been sanctified wholly that fall, and wept because an old man had kept quiet.

IV. The Card

Jackson Street · that night

Miss Thayer had been standing in the door of the front room. She came in and sat down at the table with them.

She had a white card in her hand, a five-by-eight from the study, ruled, with the old man's name at the top in her small upright hand. She laid it in front of Paul and put her finger on a line near the bottom.

There's a question I ask every woman I sit with, she said. I've asked it of a hundred and some women in this town. If you had more money, what would you buy? I asked him too, the last night in November. It wasn't on his schedule. I just asked.

Paul looked. Every woman in Muncie had answered it, she said, and she could tell him what they had said without looking, because they had all said the same things. A car, if they had none. A better car, if they had one. A bathroom. A washing machine. A radio. A house on the north side. Shoes for the children. Not one of them had said nothing.

On his card, after the question, she had written one word and underlined it. Nothing.

He thought about it a long time, she said. He said he was trying to be fair to me. Then he said he couldn't think of a thing.

She took the card back.

You asked him if he had it, she said. I'm not a Methodist, and I'm not anything anymore. But I was raised in a parsonage, and I can tell you why he didn't answer. My father was the same. It's the preachers who won't say it. They're ashamed to have only what the washerwomen have.

He had it, she said. You only had to look at him.

And then, because she was a minister's daughter after all, and because it was the only way she knew to say the rest of it, she went upstairs and came back down with the little blue Weymouth and read them two places, standing by the stove where the old man used to sit.

Give your minds to the things that are above, not to the things that are on the earth. For you have died, and your life is hidden with Christ in God.

— Colossians 3:2–3

I do not say that I have already won the race or have already reached perfection. But I am pressing on, striving to lay hold of the prize for which also Christ has laid hold of me. Brethren, I do not imagine that I have yet laid hold of it. But this one thing I do—forgetting everything which is past and stretching forward to what lies in front of me, with my eyes fixed on the goal I push on to secure the prize of God's heavenward call in Christ Jesus.

— Philippians 3:12–14

He was still going, she said. That's all. He hadn't stopped to tell anybody.

She shut the book. Mrs. Keever had put her dishrag down. The boys sat a long time without saying anything, and the clock on the kitchen shelf went on, and neither of them was weeping anymore. Harold said afterward, when he had a church of his own in Kokomo and was asked about that night, that he had come into the Keevers' kitchen sanctified and gone out of it a Christian, and he did not care to explain the difference.

V. The Tickets

The White River · March 1925

Miss Thayer went back to New York in the summer, when the study was done. She sent Mrs. Keever a Christmas card for several years from an address on Morningside Heights, and then the cards stopped. The book came out in 1929. Paul looked in it for his grandfather, and he was not in it, and Paul thought that was about right.

The White Owl box came to Paul. Inside were the class tickets, sixty years of them, small as playing cards, each with the quarter and the year and a verse printed on it, and the name of the class leader in ink. The oldest was for the first quarter of 1864, and the newest was for the last year anyone on his circuits still gave them out. Under them, at the bottom of the box, folded once, was the paper of his ordination questions, with no mark on it anywhere to show what he thought.

The ice went out of the White River the third week in March. Paul came down from Upland on a Saturday and walked out along the bank in the morning, upstream, the way the old man had gone every day, against the water. Big plates of it came down past him, grey, turning slowly, knocking against the piers of the Walnut Street bridge and going on under. He went as far as the bridge. Then, because there was no reason to stop there except that his grandfather always had, he went on.

Background

The verse

He in whom a desire for the Ineffable (Nirvana) has sprung up, who is satisfied in his mind, and whose thoughts are not bewildered by love, he is called urdhvamsrotas (carried upwards by the stream).Dhammapada 218, tr. F. Max Müller (1881)

An elder monk has reached the third stage of awakening, that of the non-returner, who has cut off sensual desire and will not be born into this world again. When the monks who live with him ask whether he has attained anything, he says nothing, from embarrassment: even laypeople reach that stage, and he means to speak only once he is fully awakened. He dies first, and is reborn in the Pure Abodes, from which non-returners pass on to final release. His pupils weep, not because he died but because he never answered them. The Buddha tells them their teacher had freed his heart from sensual desire, and speaks of one who has “gone upstream.” Read the full commentary story, or about the non-returner.

Muncie, Indiana, 1924–25

In January 1924 Robert and Helen Lynd and a small team of researchers began studying Muncie as a typical American small city, through documents, old newspapers, surveys and interviews. Their book, Middletown (1929), never named the town. It found 42 churches of 28 denominations, with the Methodist church the most prestigious, and described the line between the working class and the business class as the town’s deepest division. Muncie had grown on the Indiana gas boom of the 1880s, which brought Ball Brothers and their glass fruit jars in 1888; the Ku Klux Klan, strong in the town early in the decade, held parades there in 1922 and 1924. Miss Thayer, the Keevers and everyone else in the story are invented.

John Wesley taught that a believer could be made perfect in love in this life, and Methodist preachers are still asked at ordination whether they are “going on to perfection” and expect to be made perfect in love. The nineteenth-century Holiness movement made this “entire sanctification” or “second blessing” an instantaneous experience received by faith, often at the altar of a camp meeting. Taylor University in Upland, founded by Methodists, moved there in 1893 and ended its formal tie to the Methodist church in 1922. The verses are from Richard Francis Weymouth’s New Testament in Modern Speech, first published in 1903.
