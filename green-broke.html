The way I heard it, on the minimum yard.

I. Mister

Sixmile Conservation Camp, near Carson City, Nevada · August 2014

Odell Pruitt called everybody Mister. He had been calling everybody Mister since 1979, when he came into the Nevada State Prison at thirty-seven with a life sentence for killing a man in the parking lot of a Reno card room, and it had gotten him into fights the first ten years, because the men thought he was mocking them, and the officers thought he was mocking them, and some of the time he was. After that it stopped being mockery. It was just what he said. Mister Ochoa. Mister Bledsoe. Mister Officer Grant. By 2014 he was seventy-two years old, with a white beard trimmed close and reading glasses on a cord, and he had been moved down through the custody levels one at a time over thirty-five years until he arrived at Sixmile, a minimum camp in the sagebrush south of Carson City, where there was a chain-link fence you could have climbed in a minute and nobody did.

On Tuesday nights after chow he held a reading group in the education trailer. It was not on any list of programs and it did not earn anybody any good time. There were eight or nine men in it, sometimes twelve, sometimes four. They read one book, the same book, over and over, a library copy of Epictetus with the spine held together by packing tape and a Carson City Library stamp from 1971, which Pruitt had carried with him through four institutions and was the only thing he owned that he would have fought for. Epictetus had been a slave. He walked lame all his life, and some said it was because his master had broken his leg. He had taught that a man could be chained at the ankle and be free, and the men in the Tuesday group did not think this was a nice idea. They thought it was either a lie or the only true thing anybody had ever said to them, and they came back on Tuesdays to find out which.

Royce Etcheverry had been coming since May.

He was sixty-one, the oldest after Pruitt. He had a buckaroo’s walk, a little bowlegged and very straight, and hands like old harness, and a burn scar down the left side of his neck that went under his collar. His grandfather had come from Navarre in 1913 to herd sheep for a Basque outfit out of Winnemucca and had stayed. Royce had grown up on the big ranches of Humboldt County and had been, for thirty years, the best colt starter in northern Nevada. People said so who did not like him. He could get on a four-year-old that had never been touched and have it carrying a rider through a gate in a week, and the ranches paid him for it, and he was proud of it the way some men are proud of a son.

In November of 2010 he had left the bar in Winnemucca at closing with a gooseneck trailer behind his pickup and three of a rancher’s colts in the trailer, and on Interstate 80 east of Lovelock, in a crosswind, the trailer had come around on him. The truck rolled. A woman named Diane Harbin, forty-four, a dental hygienist from Fernley, driving home from her sister’s in a Subaru, died in it. So did two of the colts. Royce lived. He was doing eight to twenty.

He did not talk in the Tuesday group. He sat at the end of the table with his arms crossed and listened, and when the book went around and it was his turn to read a paragraph out loud he read it slowly and correctly and passed it on. Once, in July, Pruitt asked him what he thought a thing meant, a passage about what is in our power and what is not, and Royce said, I’d say it means what it says, and Pruitt said, Mister Etcheverry, I believe that’s right, and did not ask him again.

II. The Round Pen

The horse pens · September

The education trailer stood at the south end of the camp, and to get back to the units from it you walked along the fence of the horse program.

The program had been running in Nevada since before Royce came in. The Bureau of Land Management gathered wild horses off the ranges every year, more than anybody wanted to adopt, and some of them came in trailers to the prisons, where the inmates gentled them and started them under saddle for about four months, and then the horses went through a public adoption a few times a year, a competitive bid, with an auctioneer, and families from Reno and Gardnerville and California bid on them and took them home. Each horse had a freeze mark on the left side of its neck, white hair in a row of angled symbols that told the year it was born and its number. Each one came off a herd management area with a name, Fish Creek, Little Owyhee, Triple B, the names of dry country. The men in the program got paid a little, worked outside all day, and came back to the unit smelling of horse, and there was a waiting list to get in.

Royce was not on the list. He had put in for it when he came to Sixmile and been told no, on account of the colts in the trailer. He worked on the hay crew instead, moving the wheel lines on the camp’s alfalfa, and he walked past the round pen twice a day and did not look at it, and walked past it on Tuesday nights and looked.

That September there was a gray gelding in the round pen that nobody could saddle. He was a four-year-old off the Fish Creek range, fourteen hands and some, dark dapples on his rump going to white, with a white blaze, and he would take the halter now, and lead, and stand to be brushed, but when the saddle pad came near his back he exploded. The kid who had him was named Danny Portillo. He was twenty-four, from East Las Vegas, the son of Salvadorans who had come up through Mexico in 1989 and cleaned rooms at the Stardust until the day they blew it up. He was doing three years on a burglary. He had never touched a horse in his life before May, and the horse program was the best thing that had ever happened to him, and he would have told you so, and he was afraid of the gray in a way he had not been afraid of anything on the street. He called the horse Humo. Smoke. The adoption was the second Saturday of October, and the catalog was due to the BLM in a week, with a photograph of every horse under saddle and a paragraph about it written by its trainer, and Humo would not take a saddle.

Royce and five of the Tuesday men came along the fence after group that night, in the last light, with the mountains going purple over Carson City. Danny was in the round pen alone, past the time he should have been, with the pad in his hands and Humo at the far side of the pen with his head up and the whites of his eyes showing. Danny walked toward him with the pad held out low, the way they had taught him, slow, and stopped, and waited, and came on, and Humo went straight up and spun and hit the panels so hard the whole pen rang.

The men stopped at the fence. Bledsoe, who had been in the group longest after Royce, said, That boy is going to get killed.

Royce had his hands on the chain-link. He watched the gray come down off the panel and trot the rail, blowing, and he watched Danny stand in the middle with the pad, and his face did something none of them had seen it do at the Tuesday table. It woke up.

He’s going about it backwards, Royce said. That horse has been bluffing him for a month and he knows it. What you do with a horse like that, you take a soft cotton rope and tie up that off hind foot to his neck, so he’s standing on three legs and can’t go anywhere, and then you sack him out. Slap that pad on him, on his back, his belly, his legs, everywhere, and keep at it. He’ll fight it. Let him fight it. He’ll go down, maybe. Let him lie there and keep on. Twenty minutes, half an hour, and he’ll quit. You’ll see it in his eye when he quits. And then you can set a saddle on him and he’ll stand there like a sawhorse.

He said it the way a man says something he has said a thousand times to young cowboys in a corral, easily, with a kind of pleasure, and when he had said it he looked surprised at himself and took his hands off the fence.

Danny Portillo was forty feet away, in the round pen, with his back to them. He did not turn around. But he had heard every word.

III. Green Broke

The round pen · the next evening

The program supervisor was a civilian from Dayton named Hal Nygaard, who had trained horses all his life and believed in going slow. He went home at four. Danny waited until he was gone, and the officer at the barn was busy with a count sheet, and the other trainers had put their horses up. Then he went into the tack room and got a soft cotton rope.

He had trouble getting the loop around the hind pastern. Humo kicked him once in the thigh, hard, and he limped for a week afterward. But he got it, and ran the rope up through a loop around the gray’s neck and pulled the foot up under the belly and tied it off, the way he had heard it, and then Humo was standing on three legs in the middle of the pen with his nostrils open and his whole body shaking.

Danny picked up the saddle pad.

The two men who saw it from the hay barn told it afterward, and so did Danny, much later. Humo fought. He hopped on three legs and threw himself sideways and went down in the dirt of the pen and lay there groaning and thrashing his head, and got up, and Danny kept on with the pad, slapping it on his back, his neck, under his belly, down his legs, over and over, the way he had heard it, while the dust came up gold in the low sun. The horse went down again. He lay on his side a long time. Danny slapped the pad on his ribs where they heaved. He said afterward that he talked to the horse the whole time, said ya, ya, ya, ya está, ya está, it’s done, it’s done, and that he was crying, and that he did not stop.

Then the horse got up and stood. He stood on three legs with his head down and the pad on his back and did not move. Danny took the pad off and put it on and took it off. Humo did not move. Danny untied the foot. Humo set it down and did not move. Danny walked to the rail and got the saddle and swung it up onto the gray’s back and pulled the cinch, and the horse stood there like a sawhorse, the way Royce had said.

Danny rode him the next morning in front of Hal Nygaard, walk and trot, both directions around the pen. Nygaard was amazed and said so. He said he had been about to send that horse back to the BLM short-term corral as unadoptable and he did not know what Danny had done but whatever it was, keep doing it. They took the catalog photograph that afternoon: Danny in the saddle in his prison blues and a ball cap, Humo standing square with his head low.

That night in the unit Danny wrote his paragraph for the catalog, three tries, on lined paper, and turned it in.

Humo is a quiet gelding who stands well to saddle and gives no trouble. At first he was nervous but he is a fast learner. He will make somebody a good horse.

Royce saw the horse two days later, walking back from the alfalfa. Danny was leading him around the pen on a loose rope and the gray followed with his head at Danny’s shoulder, low, and when Danny stopped he stopped. Royce stood at the fence a long time. He knew that walk. He knew the eye. He had made it in a hundred horses and been paid for it, the eye that has stopped expecting anything, and he had always called it quitting, and he had always thought it was the beginning of a good horse.

He did not say anything. He went back to the unit and lay on his bunk until count.

IV. Is It True

The education trailer · Tuesday

It was Bledsoe who told Pruitt. He was not a talker either; he told it because it had been sitting on him all week. He told it before group, when it was just the two of them setting out the chairs, the whole thing, the fence and the rope and what the hay crew saw from the barn and the paragraph in the catalog, which one of the clerks had shown around. Pruitt listened with his reading glasses in his hand. Then he put them on and opened the book and waited for the others.

When they were all there, nine of them, and Royce at the end of the table with his arms crossed, Pruitt did not open the book. He looked down the table.

Mister Etcheverry.

Royce looked at him.

On Tuesday last, at the round pen, did you tell those men how to tie up that gray horse’s foot and sack him out until he quit?

Nobody moved. Somebody’s chair creaked and stopped.

Yes, Royce said. I did. It’s true.

Pruitt nodded slowly, as if a man had told him the time.

Mister Etcheverry, he said, what have you got to do with horses?

He did not raise his voice. He never had to, in thirty-five years. He said it quietly and it went down the table like cold water.

You are a man in blue in a camp in the desert with eight to twenty and a woman named Diane Harbin dead on the interstate. You came in here in May and sat at that end of this table and read this book like a man reading the instructions on a can. You never said a word in four months. And the first time anything in this place wakes you up, the first time, it’s that boy out there with a horse he can’t handle. And what you have to give him is how to tie it up so it can’t move and beat on it till it gives up. That’s what you’ve got. That’s the thing you were best at in the world.

He opened the book now. He did not need to look for the place.

You think that horse is going to carry you somewhere? Out of here? Any horse? A man can ride all the horses in Nevada, Mister, he can start every colt from here to Elko and do it in a week, and never once go anywhere he hasn’t already been. He turned the book around on the table so Royce could see the page. Read that.

Royce uncrossed his arms and pulled the book to him and read it out loud, slowly and correctly, the way he always did.

Be not elated at any advantage (excellence) which belongs to another. If a horse when he is elated should say, I am beautiful, one might endure it. But when you are elated, and say, I have a beautiful horse, you must know that you are elated at having a good horse. What then is your own? The use of appearances.

— Epictetus, Encheiridion 6

What’s your own, Mister Etcheverry? said Pruitt.

Royce sat with his hand flat on the page.

Not the horse, Pruitt said. Not that gray and not those two colts in the trailer either, God rest them, and not your hands, and not your name in Humboldt County. There is only one animal in this trailer you’ve got any business breaking, and you haven’t laid a rope on him in sixty-one years. He took the book back and turned some pages, near the end, to the long chapter about freedom that the group had read in June, and read it himself this time, to all of them.

Your body then is another’s, subject to every man who is stronger than yourself. It is. But your estate is it in your power to have it when you please, and as long as you please, and such as you please? No. And your slaves? No. And your clothes? No. And your house? No. And your horses? Not one of these things.

— Epictetus, Discourses 4.1

Tame that one, said Pruitt. And then you can go someplace.

Then he said, Mister Bledsoe, page forty, if you would, and they went on with the group, and nobody looked at Royce for the rest of the hour, which was the kindest thing they knew how to do.

V. The Adoption

The horse pens · the second Saturday of October

The adoption came on a cold bright Saturday with the first snow on the Carson Range. The public came in through a side gate at the south end of the camp, a hundred and fifty people in down vests and good boots, with horse trailers lined up along the road outside, and walked along the pens with their catalogs reading the paragraphs. Nobody in blue denim. No cell phones. No photographs. The trainers rode their horses around the arena one at a time while the auctioneer talked them up, and the bidding started at a hundred and fifty dollars.

The hay crew was not allowed down there. Royce watched from the far edge of the alfalfa, beside the wheel line, a quarter mile off, where he could see the arena small and bright with the people around it like a ring of birds.

When Danny rode Humo into the arena the gray went around once at a walk and once at a trot, head low, ears neither forward nor back, and stopped when Danny stopped him, and stood. A family from Minden bought him for four hundred and twenty-five dollars, a man and his wife and a girl of about eleven in a pink parka who had been standing at Humo’s pen all morning reading the paragraph out loud to her parents. Danny led the horse out to their trailer himself, because the trainers were allowed to do that. He told Royce about it months later in the chow line, the only time they ever spoke of it. He said the girl asked him if Humo was a nice horse. He said he told her Humo was a good horse, which was true, and that he would make somebody a good horse, which was true, and that she should go slow with him, very slow, slower than she thought, and if he ever got scared, she should just stand there with him and wait until they both quit being scared.

I don’t know if he’ll ever come back, Danny said. In his eye. You know?

Royce said he knew.

VI. Wheel Lines

The camp alfalfa · that fall and winter

Some of the men in the room that night said afterward that something happened to them, sitting there, that had not happened in any program or chapel or court. Bledsoe said it was like hearing somebody say your name in a place where you didn’t know anybody knew it. A young man named Teo Maldonado, who had come to the group twice before and not liked it, came every Tuesday after that until he was paroled in 2016, and wrote Pruitt letters from Reno for years. They said things changed for them from that night. They did not say how.

Royce did not come to group the next Tuesday. He came the one after, and sat in the same place, and when the book came to him he read his paragraph, and then he said, without being asked, I don’t know how to do it. And Pruitt said, No, Mister. Nobody does. You do it anyway.

He did not go along the fence by the round pen anymore. He went back the long way after group, around by the kitchen, and he moved the wheel lines in the alfalfa in the mornings, twelve hundred feet of aluminum pipe on wheels taller than a man, that you uncoupled and rolled forward by a crank, one set at a time, to put the water on the next strip of the field. It was work you could not hurry. If you hurried the line bowed and the couplings bent and you spent the afternoon fixing them. He learned to stand at the mover with his hands on the crank and wait until his breathing came down before he started it, and then the whole quarter mile of wheels rolled forward together, slowly, straight, and stopped where he stopped it, and the sprinklers came on and the water went out over the new strip in long arcs and soaked into the ground and ran a little way down the furrows in the low places, and the old men on the hay crew said that was where the water wanted to go.

At night he lay on his bunk and said her name. Diane Harbin. He had said it at his sentencing, reading from a paper, and not since. He said it now every night, once, before count, not as a prayer, because he did not know any, but the way you set a foot down on ground you are not sure of. Some nights he said the names of the colts too. He had not known them. He gave them names.

He found that the thing in him that had wakened at the round pen, the thing that had reached for the rope, woke up all the time, a dozen times a day, at the chow line and the phone and the officer who said his name wrong, and that it had always been waking up, every day of his life, and he had always called it being good at something. Now when he felt it he did what he did at the wheel line. He stood with his hands where they were and waited until his breath came down. It did not always work. Once in December he put a man against the wall of the laundry for cutting in line and was written up and lost thirty days’ good time, and he went to group that Tuesday and said so, at the table, before anybody else could, and Pruitt said, Mister Etcheverry, that is the first true thing I have heard all week, and gave him the book to read from.

One morning in January he was out on the wheel line at six, in the dark, before the rest of the hay crew. There was frost on the alfalfa and the pipe was so cold it burned his hands through his gloves. He set them on the crank of the mover and stood there and waited, and watched his breath go out white in front of him and come back in, out and in, until it had slowed down to nothing much, and the mountains were coming up grey over Carson City, and somewhere back at the pens a horse he had never seen nickered once for its feed. Then he turned the crank, and the whole long line of wheels began to roll forward together across the field, slowly, all of them at once, straight, and stopped where he stopped it.

Background

The verse

For with these animals does no man reach the untrodden country (Nirvana), where a tamed man goes on a tamed animal, viz. on his own well-tamed self.Dhammapada 323, tr. F. Max Müller (1881)

In the commentary, a monk who had been an elephant trainer before he was ordained stands with other monks on the bank of the Aciravatī river, watching a trainer fail to teach an elephant a trick. He remarks that if the man pricked the elephant in a certain place it would learn quickly. The trainer overhears, does it, and the elephant submits. The other monks report it to the Buddha, who calls the monk in, asks whether it is true, and on hearing yes rebukes him: what has he to do with elephants or any tamed animal? No such vehicle carries a man to the place he has never been; only his own well-tamed self can. Many who heard the teaching were set on the path. Nevada, 2014

Wild horses on Nevada's public ranges were hunted from airplanes and trucks for pet-food plants until a Reno woman, Velma Johnston, known as Wild Horse Annie, followed a bleeding truckload to a slaughterhouse in 1950 and spent the rest of her life campaigning. A 1959 law banned hunting them with motor vehicles and aircraft, and the Wild and Free-Roaming Horses and Burros Act of 1971 made them "living symbols of the historic and pioneer spirit of the West" under federal protection. The Bureau of Land Management gathers horses off the ranges and offers them for adoption, each freeze-marked on the left side of the neck with its birth year and number; since about 2001 removals have outrun adoptions.

Sixmile Conservation Camp is fictional; its program is modeled on the real one at the Northern Nevada Correctional Center in Carson City, which began in October 2000 and since 2007 has trained only BLM wild horses and burros. Inmates, often with little or no experience of horses, give each animal about 120 days of training; the horses go out “green broke” through public competitive-bid adoptions several times a year, with bids starting at $150 and a catalog paragraph written by each trainer. Visitors may not wear blue denim. Royce's people are the Basques of northern Nevada, who came from the Spanish and French Pyrenees to herd sheep and settled around Winnemucca, Elko and Battle Mountain, which still have some of the highest shares of Basque ancestry in the country. The camp's hay is watered by wheel lines, rolling sprinkler pipe moved forward one set at a time.

After the story of the monk who had been an elephant trainer, from the Dhammapada commentary (verse 323). The original. Epictetus in the translation of George Long. Written by Claude Opus 5.5.
