Neither East nor West

What is to be done, O Moslems? for I do not recognise myself.
I am neither Christian, nor Jew, nor Gabr, nor Moslem.
— Rumi, Divani Shamsi Tabriz XXXI

Chenin shenidam, on Route 60.

I. Seven Weeks

The upper Kanawha Valley, West Virginia · December 1979

There were forty-one Iranians at the college that fall, most of them in engineering, and after the fourth of November there was no way for any of them to be only a student. The Americans wanted to know whose side they were on. Somebody hung a bedsheet from the third floor of the men’s dormitory with a thing about the Ayatollah painted on it, and in the cafeteria line a boy from Beckley asked Kamran every day, not unkindly, when he was going to let those people go. The Iranians wanted to know the same thing from one another, and were harder about it. Shahi, Tudeh, Mojahed, Hezbollahi. Khatt-e ki hasti, they said. Whose line are you. In Tehran the ones who had gone over the embassy wall called themselves the students who followed the Imam’s line, and since then the word was in everything.

Kamran Yazdani kept his answer short. He was a junior in civil engineering, the son of a cloth merchant in the Yazd bazaar, and he believed more or less what his father believed. A man could no more work out the whole of religion for himself than do his own surgery, so he chose one learned man and followed him, and Kamran followed the same grand ayatollah his father did, whose book of rulings his mother had packed between his shirts. For the rest there was what was written. Before an examination he opened his mother’s Hafez with his eyes shut and put his finger down. He had done it on the night of the fourth of November, and the verse had been one of the dark ones.

The money stopped in the middle of that month, when the President froze what Iran had in American banks and nothing more came through from home. By the middle of December every Iranian on a student visa was to have shown his papers to the immigration men, who came to the campus for two days with a folding table and a camera. Then a judge in Washington said the order was against the Constitution, and nobody knew yet whether that would stand. The dormitory closed for Christmas on the twenty-second. Kamran’s lab partner, a boy named Randy Skaggs from up one of the creeks, said his daddy had room, and to come on.

The one nobody could place was Siavash. He was older than the rest of them, twenty-six, a mining man from Kerman who had been in the country since ’seventy-four and in everything. He had marched in Washington against the Shah in a paper mask. He had been a Marxist for two winters and after that had prayed in the front row, and each time he had gone all the way in, and each time he had come out the far side and said it was not there. He had shared a house on Fourth Avenue with five others as serious as he was. In the spring they told him he was a man who finished nothing, and they went down to the university at Huntington, the five of them in one car.

After the fourth of November he quit going out. He had a room over the hardware store with a radiator and a hot plate, and he was seen at the window. It was said that he sat. It was said that when the immigration men asked him at the folding table which group he belonged to, he said none, and that the officer wrote none and looked up at him, and stamped the paper. That was all anybody had. It went on seven weeks.

II. Whose Line

Route 60, below Smithers · December 23

Randy’s father’s place was up Peddler’s Run, four miles of dirt off the highway, and Randy had said to wait at the mouth of the run and he would come down with the truck when he was done feeding. Kamran stood by the mailboxes with his suitcase. It was a gray noon, two days past the longest night of the year, which he had kept alone with a pomegranate from the Kroger and his mother’s Hafez. The river was the color of a nickel. Coal trucks went by, and their wind moved his coat.

A man was walking toward him down the shoulder from the direction of town, carrying a gym bag. He walked like someone with a long way to go who was not thinking about the way. When he was near enough Kamran saw who it was, and for a moment did not believe it, because of the face.

Everybody Kamran knew that month had the same face. It was gray under the eyes from the news, and tight at the mouth from being asked. Siavash’s was clear. The skin was clear and the eyes were quiet. He looked like somebody who had slept and eaten and owed no one, in a month when no Iranian in America slept.

Salaam, Kamran said. Don’t be tired. Your color has come back. You look well, brother, you look better than any of us. Where are you going on foot?

Huntington.

That is seventy-five miles.

About that. There are five men there I have something to tell.

Kamran laughed, because it was the kind of thing Siavash had always said, and then stopped, because of the face. He asked what any of them would have asked.

Tell me, then. Whose line are you now? Who is it you follow? You have found a teacher. One of the grand ayatollahs, or some shaykh, somebody here. A man does not come to look like that by himself.

I have no line, Siavash said. I follow no one. There is no teacher. He said it without any heat. Then he began an old poem, the one that is sung as Mowlana’s. Kamran knew it. When it came to the third line he heard it start the way every wall in Tehran had started that year. Na sharqiyam, na gharbiyam. Neither of the East nor of the West. He waited for the rest of the slogan, the Islamic Republic. The poem went the other way.

What is to be done, O Moslems? for I do not recognise myself.
I am neither Christian, nor Jew, nor Gabr, nor Moslem.
I am not of the East, nor of the West, nor of the land, nor of the sea;
I am not of Nature’s mint, nor of the circling heavens.

— Rumi, Divani Shamsi Tabriz XXXI

That is his, and I am only borrowing the sound of it, Siavash said. Here is mine. I have come through all of it. I see how it goes, every part, and none of it sticks to me now. I put everything down, Kamran. The Shah and the Imam and the Party and my own name for myself. The wanting went out, and when it went I was free. I found that out in a room over a hardware store, and nobody handed it to me. So whose name do you want me to give you?

A coal truck went by. Kamran looked at him a while longer. He did not say yes and he did not say no. He tipped his head back, the way people do at home, and clicked his tongue once against his teeth. Then, because that was rude to a man with seventy-five miles in front of him, he said, Inshallah. May it be so, brother. He was shaking his head while he said it.

Randy’s truck was coming down the run with its lights on. Siavash lifted the gym bag and nodded, as a man nods to someone he will see again or will not, and went on down the shoulder toward Charleston with the river on his left. Kamran got in the truck. It turned around at the mailboxes and went back up the dirt road.

III. Peddler’s Run

That afternoon

Randy’s father ran coonhounds and a trapline, and had put in thirty years at the alloy plant between the two. There were hides drying on boards along the porch wall, and a buck from Thanksgiving week cut and wrapped in the freezer on the back porch, and four Walker hounds that came out from under the house and sang at the suitcase. Mr. Skaggs shook Kamran’s hand and asked was he the Iranian. Kamran said he was. Mr. Skaggs said well, he didn’t reckon Kamran had took anybody hostage personally, and to come in out of the cold, there were beans.

A girl was splitting kindling by the smokehouse, Randy’s sister, home from nursing school. She did not look up yet.

At the supper table Kamran opened his mouth twice to tell about the man on the highway, and could not find where it began. Later, in the room they gave him, he took his mother’s Hafez out of the suitcase and held it shut for some time with his finger along the edge of the pages. Then he put it back between the shirts without opening it. From the window he could see a little of the highway at the mouth of the run, where the lights of the trucks went by, heading down the valley.

Background

The verse

‘I have conquered all, I know all, in all conditions of life I am free from taint; I have left all, and through the destruction of thirst I am free; having learnt myself, whom shall I teach?’Dhammapada 353, tr. F. Max Müller (1881)

In the commentary the Buddha, newly awakened, has spent seven weeks near the tree and sets out on foot for Benares, eighteen leagues off, to teach five former companions. On the road he meets Upaka, a follower of the Ājīvikas, a rival school of ascetics remembered for teaching that everything is settled by fate. Upaka admires his calm senses and clear complexion and asks in whose name he has gone forth, who his teacher is and whose doctrine he holds. The Buddha answers that he has no teacher, and speaks this verse. Upaka neither approves nor disapproves; in the older telling he says only, “It may be so, friend.” Shaking his head, he takes a side path and goes to the house of a hunter. Read the full commentary story. Later tradition has him marry the hunter’s daughter and, years afterward, come back.

The winter of 1979

On November 4, 1979, a group calling itself the Muslim Student Followers of the Imam’s Line seized the American embassy in Tehran and began the hostage crisis. Khatt, line, became the word for a person’s politics, and the revolution’s slogan was “Neither East nor West, Islamic Republic.” More than fifty thousand Iranians were then studying in the United States, many at small colleges. President Carter ordered them to report to immigration officers or face deportation, and on November 14 froze Iranian assets in American banks by Executive Order 12170, which cut many students off from money sent from home. A federal judge held the reporting order unconstitutional on December 11, and an appeals court reversed her on December 27 (Narenji v. Civiletti). In about two months 54,486 Iranians were interviewed; several hundred were eventually deported. The college, Kamran, Siavash and the Skaggs family are invented. U.S. Route 60, the old Midland Trail, follows the Kanawha River down to Charleston and runs on to Huntington.

Kamran’s religion is the ordinary practice of Twelver Shia Muslims, each of whom follows the rulings of a living senior jurist, a marja’; the practice is called taqlid. Opening the poems of Hafez at random for an omen is an old Iranian custom, kept especially on Yalda, the longest night of the year, with pomegranates. The poem is number XXXI in R. A. Nicholson’s Selected Poems from the Divani Shamsi Tabriz (1898), an early English selection of Rumi’s lyrics. It has long been sung as his, though scholars have not found it in the oldest manuscripts of his Divan, and it may be by a later hand.
