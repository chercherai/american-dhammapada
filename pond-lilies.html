Walking the American WayCommentary 4.12 · Garahadinna

Pond Lilies

The people that walked in darkness have seen a great light.
— Isaiah 9:2

So it was told to me, by the Mashpee River.

I. The Summerland

Mashpee, Massachusetts · summer 1878

Isaac Wicket and Moses Freeman had been friends since they were boys spearing eels through the ice on Wakeby Pond, and by the time they were fifty they were the two most comfortable men in Mashpee. Isaac had cranberry bog on the Santuit side and an oyster grant in Popponesset Bay. Moses had bog of his own and two hundred acres of oak and pitch pine that he cut into cordwood and sent up to Boston by the schooner load. Their houses stood a quarter mile apart on the Falmouth road, and they had been in and out of each other’s kitchens for forty years.

Isaac sat under Brother Simon at the Baptist meeting. Moses had sat there too, until his boy Charlie went over the side of a New Bedford whaling bark in a gale off the Azores, in the winter of 1876, and the sea did not give him back. Brother Simon had said at the memorial that the sea does not give back, that nobody could promise Moses otherwise and nobody should. Moses never went to meeting again.

In the summer of 1877 the Spiritualists had opened their great camp at Onset Bay, over the line in Wareham, with tents and a speakers’ stand and a grove full of mediums who would talk with your dead for a dollar. By the next summer some of them had begun to travel. A company of five came down the Cape in a hired wagon: Dr. Elias Pratt, clairvoyant physician and test medium, and four younger men who sang and kept the cabinet and passed the hat. Moses Freeman took them in.

Dr. Pratt sat in Moses’s front room in the dark and went stiff in his chair, and a voice came out of him that called itself Chief Thunder Cloud, and talked like a dime novel. Chief Thunder Cloud said the old sachems of Mashpee were gathered in the room. Chief Thunder Cloud said that Charlie Freeman was in the Summerland, where the sea could not reach him, and that he was well, and that he forgave his father. Moses wept like a child in the dark. In the morning he gave Dr. Pratt twenty dollars.

After that he could not leave Isaac alone. Wherever he found him, at the bog or the store or the landing, sitting or standing, he said it.

Isaac, what do you get from that blind man? What has he ever told you? He can’t give you your dead. Come hear the Doctor. Bring him to your house. Feed him. It would mean something.

Isaac kept still about it for a good many days. He had known Moses all his life and he had carried Charlie Freeman on his shoulders. Then one hot morning on the landing, with Moses at him again in front of the oystermen, he set down his basket.

Moses. Every day, standing, sitting. All right. Tell me. What does your Doctor know?

Know? Moses lit up. Isaac, there is nothing the spirits don’t know. What’s past and what’s now and what’s coming. Every thought in your head. They told me things about Charlie nobody living knew.

Is that so.

It is so.

Then you’ve done me a wrong, Isaac said, keeping it from me all this time. Go and ask the Doctor and his company to my house to supper. Saturday. Tell them I said so.

The Doctor was very pleased. Wicket, he said. The Baptist. Our work here is as good as done.

II. Supper

Isaac Wicket’s place · a Saturday in August

Isaac’s house and his late father’s house stood side by side with a long grassy space between them, and on Thursday and Friday Isaac and his three sons and two nephews dug a trench down the length of it, waist deep. They filled it with what you would expect: the slop of the hog pen, and fish heads and eel skins from the landing, and the black muck out of the bottom of an old ditch at the bog. Over the trench they set a long trestle table, and on the near side of it, five good chairs, with their front legs on the grass and their back legs resting on a rope stretched tight between two stakes. Over the chairs they spread Isaac’s wife’s best quilts, so nobody could see what the chairs were standing on.

Then he took every iron kettle and crock in both houses, scrubbed them out, and set them in a row behind the kitchen with cloths over their mouths. He smeared the outsides with chowder and dabbed them with succotash and Indian pudding, so that they looked like kettles that had been filled and stirred and slopped over. They were empty.

Early Saturday morning Moses came over to make sure of things.

Is there supper for them?

There’s supper.

Where?

Here, said Isaac, and walked him along the row. Chowder in these. Quahogs, these. Succotash. Eels. This is pudding. And there’s chairs set for them, and the good quilts.

That’s handsome, Isaac, said Moses, and went home happy.

The Doctor’s company came up the Falmouth road toward evening in their wagon, in black coats, very dusty. Isaac went out to the gate. He bowed to them, low, the way you would bow to a minister, and while he was bowing he said to them in his heart: So you know what’s past and what’s now and what’s coming. That is what my friend tells me. If you know it, don’t come through this gate. There is not one bite of supper in my house for you. And if you don’t know it, and come in anyway, you’re going in the ditch.

Out loud he said, Come in, gentlemen.

They came in. They saw the table and the quilts and went to sit, and Isaac’s oldest boy called out, Wait! Not yet, sirs, please.

What is it?

It’s the custom in this house. Each gentleman stands at his chair first, and then everybody sits down together at once. It’s respect.

Isaac had thought of that himself. If one of them went into the ditch alone, he would holler, and the rest would never sit.

Of course, said Dr. Pratt, who had found it paid to do what people asked in their own houses. Each man stood at his chair. Isaac’s nephews took hold of the corners of the quilts from behind.

All together now, sirs. Sit.

The nephews pulled the quilts away clean as they sat. The back legs of five chairs went down off the rope, and five clairvoyants went over backward with their boots in the air, head first into the ditch.

Isaac shut the gate. As fast as they crawled up out of the muck his sons laid into them with alder switches, and Isaac stood over the trench and called out to them, So you know what’s past and what’s now and what’s coming! So you know every thought in a man’s head! It went on longer than some of the neighbors who had come to watch could stand to see. A switch cuts. One of the nephews threw his down and walked off toward the road. At last Isaac said, That’ll do, and opened the gate.

They ran. But in the afternoon Isaac had slathered the lane outside the gate with whitewash, thick, a bucket and a half of lime, and in the dew it was as slick as an eel’s back. They went down, and got up, and went down, and the boys were waiting with the switches there too, until Isaac said, That’ll do for you, and let them go.

They went the quarter mile up the Falmouth road in the dusk to Moses Freeman’s door, filthy and bleeding and white to the knees with lime, crying out that they had been ruined. You have ruined us! He has ruined us!

III. The Trial Justice

Barnstable · August

Moses Freeman stood in his doorway and looked at them a long time. He had known Isaac Wicket for forty-five years and had never once in that time seen him do a cruel thing. Then he went white with anger, as white as their legs.

He’s ruined me, he said. They bowed to him. They came to his house as guests and he’s beaten them with sticks. My teachers. The men who brought me my son.

On Monday he drove to Barnstable and swore out a complaint for assault before the trial justice, and the justice sent a summons to Isaac Wicket.

Isaac came in his meeting coat and stood with his hat in his hands.

Your Honor, will you hear it before you fine me? Or fine me first?

I generally hear it first, Mr. Wicket.

Then hear it, and do what you think is right.

And he told it all from the beginning. Moses at him on the landing and the road and the store, standing and sitting, every day. What does your blind man know. The spirits know everything, the past, the present, the future, every thought in your head. The trench, the chairs, the kettles. He did not leave out the switches.

The trial justice was an old Barnstable lawyer who had sat through a good many lectures on table-rapping in his time and had believed none of them. He turned to Moses.

Is that the truth, Mr. Freeman?

It’s the truth.

Then why in heaven’s name did you take up with teachers who couldn’t see as far as the supper table, and go around telling a Baptist they knew everything? You brought this down on your own head, sir, and on your own head it can stay. He dismissed the complaint and laid the costs of the court on Moses. And he told the constable to see Dr. Pratt and his company onto the cars at Sandwich, and if they were found in Barnstable County again they would answer for it as common fortune-tellers.

IV. The Bake

Mashpee · late August

For two weeks Moses would not speak to Isaac. He passed him on the road and looked at the trees. He went the long way around to the landing.

Then he thought it over, sitting by himself at night in the front room where Chief Thunder Cloud had talked. Sulking would get him nothing. What he wanted was for Isaac Wicket and Isaac’s blind preacher to be laughed at in front of the whole town the way he had been laughed at, the way his teachers had been laughed at, crawling out of a ditch.

So he went to Isaac’s gate. Isaac.

Moses.

Folks are saying we’ve fallen out. Kin and friends fall out, and then they get over it. Why won’t you speak to me?

I don’t speak because you don’t, Isaac said. But what’s done is done. I’m not going to throw away forty years over it. And after that they stood together on the landing again and sat together on the store steps the way they always had.

One day on the steps Isaac said, Moses, what do you want with spirits? What did they ever give you but a bill at the court? Come back to meeting. Hear Brother Simon.

It was exactly what Moses had been waiting for. It was like being scratched where he itched.

And what does your Brother Simon know?

Moses, don’t talk like that. There’s nothing the Lord keeps from him. He knows what’s past and what’s coming. He knows what’s in a man before the man knows it.

Then you’ve done me a wrong, said Moses, not telling me all this time. Ask him to my place. Saturday week. Him and the whole church. I’ll put on a bake.

•••

Brother Simon Hosea was sixty-six that summer. He had gone whaling at fifteen out of New Bedford, like half the young men in Mashpee, and come home at twenty-two without his eyes, and old Blind Joe Amos, who could say whole chapters of the Bible by heart, had taken him in hand and taught him to preach the same way, by ear, chapter by chapter, from his own mother’s reading. He lived alone on the Cotuit road with a cat and a boy named Lemuel who led him where he needed to go.

Isaac went out to him that evening and told him about the bake.

Only, Brother Simon, I don’t know if his heart’s clean in it. I did a thing to his Spiritualists, a hard thing. He told it. He may want to pay me back on you. Think it over. If it seems right to you, go. If it doesn’t, don’t.

The old man sat in his chair facing the window he could not see out of, with the cat on his knees, for a long while.

People said afterward that he knew it all that night. That after Isaac had gone he told Lemuel what Moses Freeman meant to do, the pit and the fire and the cover over it, and what would come of it, the whole thing, and that Lemuel was twelve and did not believe a word. But Lemuel told it the same way for sixty years after.

We’ll go, said Brother Simon. For Moses’s sake.

•••

Moses dug the pit between his house and his barn. He dug it deep, deeper than any clambake ever needed, and lined it with round stones off the beach at Popponesset, cartload after cartload, and filled it to the top with his own cordwood, oak and pitch pine, a cord and a half of it. On Friday evening he set it alight, and borrowed the bellows out of the smithy in Cotuit and worked them himself all night long, until the pile had burned down to a bed of coals and the stones underneath glowed red in the dark, and you could feel the heat of it from the kitchen steps.

Before light he laid poles across the pit, the thinnest poles he had, and over the poles marsh hay, and over the hay wet rockweed from the shore, a thick heap of it, so that it looked like any bake that had been laid down and covered and was steaming. Across the far side of it he set a chair, the best chair in his house, for the preacher. And from the near side to the chair he laid a walk of old lath, the kind that breaks if you look at it.

Behind his kitchen he set out every kettle he owned in a row, empty, with cloths over their mouths, smeared on the outsides with chowder. Exactly the way Isaac had.

On Saturday morning Isaac came over early. Moses, is there dinner?

Come see. Moses walked him down the row. Chowder. Quahogs. Corn. Eels. Succotash. Pudding.

That’s handsome, Moses, said Isaac, and meant it.

V. The Lilies

Moses Freeman’s place · the Saturday

By noon there was a crowd. Word goes around a small town. Half of Mashpee came, and people from Cotuit and Waquoit and even from Falmouth, white and Indian and colored, in wagons and on foot. Some came because Moses Freeman always set a good table. Some came because they had heard about the ditch, and the trial justice, and they knew Moses, and they wanted to see what a man like that would do to a blind preacher. And some came because Brother Simon was going to speak at a bake, outdoors, in the summer, and they wanted to hear it.

Brother Simon came up the road at noon on Lemuel’s arm, with the whole Baptist church walking behind him in their Sabbath clothes, forty or fifty of them. Moses met them at his gate. He bowed to the preacher low, the way Isaac had bowed, and while he was bowing he said to him in his heart: So you know what’s past and what’s now and what’s coming, Brother Simon. So you know what’s in a man before he knows it himself. That’s what Isaac tells me. If you know it, don’t come through this gate. There’s no dinner for you. There’s a pit of fire, and you’re going in it in front of everybody.

Out loud he took the old man’s hand and said, Brother Simon, welcome. Only there’s a custom at a bake in this house. The guest of honor goes first, by himself, and takes the head chair. After he’s sat down, the rest can come.

He had thought of that himself. If they saw the blind man go through, none of the others would go near it, and the blind man would be in the fire alone.

All right, said Brother Simon, and let go of Lemuel’s arm.

Moses led him by the hand along the side of the house to the edge of the rockweed, and the crowd came after and stood around the yard. The heat came up through the weed so that the air above it shook. Moses put the old man’s boot on the end of the lath walk. Then he let go of his hand and stepped back, and back again, and stood off at a distance with the crowd.

Straight ahead, Brother Simon, he called. Six steps.

Brother Simon put his foot out over the pit.

The rockweed and the hay and the poles were simply gone, and the crowd saw the coals, a red floor of coals the size of a room. And up through the coals, pushing them apart the way a spring pushes up through sand, came lilies. Pond lilies, white, the kind that float on Wakeby and Mashpee pond on an August morning with their gold hearts open, only these were as wide as cartwheels, with pads under them as wide as wagon beds, rising on the heat as if it were water. The smell of them filled the whole yard, sweet and clean, over the smell of the fire.

Brother Simon set his foot down in the gold heart of the nearest one, and walked out across them in his Sabbath boots, one to the next, without hurrying, and sat down in Moses Freeman’s best chair on the far side. And the whole Baptist church walked across after him, the old women holding their skirts, Lemuel at a run, and Isaac Wicket, and sat down on the grass around him.

Moses stood where he was. It was as if he had swallowed one of his own coals.

Then he went to Isaac, who was sitting on the grass by the preacher’s chair, and knelt down in front of him.

Isaac. You have to help me.

What is it?

There’s no dinner. There’s nothing. Forty people and the whole town watching, and there’s not a bite in the house.

What have you done, Moses?

He told him. The pit, the bellows, the lath, all of it. I meant him to go in. I meant to shame him like you shamed them. And it’s lilies, Isaac. Look at it. What do I do?

This morning you walked me down a row of kettles, said Isaac. You said this one is chowder and this one is quahogs and this one is pudding.

I lied. They’re empty.

Never mind that. Go and look in them.

Moses went around behind the kitchen and lifted the cloth off the first kettle, and it was full of chowder, hot, with the steam coming off it. The next was quahogs. The next was corn. Every kettle he had called by a name that morning was full of the thing he had called it, chowder and eels and succotash and corn and Indian pudding, to the brim, and when he got to the end of the row he stood with the last cloth in his hand and shook.

Then he carried them out himself, one by one, and served every soul in the yard with his own hands, the Baptists and the curious and the ones who had come to see the old man burn, and he did not sit down until everyone had eaten.

VI. Eyes

The same afternoon

When the eating was done Moses came and stood in front of the preacher’s chair with his hat in his hands, the way men stood in Mashpee when they wanted the minister to return thanks.

Brother Simon turned his face toward the crowd. He could not see them, but he knew where they were, the way you know where the sea is at night.

A good many of you came out today to see a blind man fall in the fire, he said. You have eyes. You didn’t see what was in front of you. You didn’t see the Lord, or His mercy, or the poor folks of His church sitting here on the grass. You came to look, and you didn’t see. I’m the blind one here, and I tell you there’s not more than a handful in this yard with eyes. The ones that know Him have eyes. That’s all.

And then he said it the way he said everything, by heart.

He raiseth up the poor out of the dust, and lifteth up the beggar from the dunghill, to set them among princes, and to make them inherit the throne of glory.

— 1 Samuel 2:8

The people that walked in darkness have seen a great light: they that dwell in the land of the shadow of death, upon them hath the light shined.

— Isaiah 9:2

Some of the people in that yard said for the rest of their lives that they had seen something that afternoon. They did not mean the lilies. Moses Freeman and Isaac Wicket went down into Mashpee pond together at the end of September and Brother Simon baptized them both, standing waist deep among the pads, with Lemuel holding his elbow. Moses put a new roof on the meeting house that fall, and Isaac paid for the shingles, and between them, over the next ten years, they gave away most of what they had.

VII. The Try-Works

The meeting house steps · evening

That evening a crowd of them sat on the meeting house steps in the long light, still talking about it. Did you ever see such a thing. Lilies out of a fire. Did you ever in your life.

Brother Simon came up the path on Lemuel’s arm and asked what they were saying, and they told him.

Well, he said, and sat down on the bottom step. It’s not the first time.

And because they asked him, he told them.

When he was nineteen he was on a bark out of New Bedford, in the Indian Ocean. There was an old Portuguese cook in the forecastle with the fever who had not eaten in seven days, and the boy had a pannikin of soup for him, carrying it forward. They were trying out a whale that night, boiling down the blubber in the try-pots on deck, with the fire roaring in the brick try-works under them, and the ship pitched, and one of the pots went over. In a moment the deck between him and the forecastle was a sheet of burning oil, blue and yellow, running in the scuppers.

Every man on deck backed off from it. And Simon Hosea, nineteen years old, looked up, and saw a man standing in the rigging over the fire, laughing. Nobody else saw him. He said the man was well dressed.

I asked him did he light it, said Brother Simon. He said he did. I asked him why. He said to keep me from bringing the old man his supper, and to let the old man starve. So I told him he could do as he liked, but I was bringing the old man his supper. And I walked out onto it.

He said something came up under his feet. He did not know what. Something soft and cool, all the way across, and a dust came off it like gold and covered him from his head to his boots, and the man in the rigging was gone, and the old cook ate his soup.

That was the last thing I ever saw, he said. Gold. My eyes went after that, from the fire, the doctor in Honolulu said. I don’t know. I’ve been looking at it ever since.

•••

Moses filled the pit in that fall. The next spring the low place between his house and barn would not dry out, and by June there was standing water in it, a round black pond the size of a room, fed from somewhere underneath, and nobody could ever say from where.

In August there were lilies on it. Every morning that month Moses Freeman picked one with the dew still on it and walked the two miles out the Cotuit road and put it in the old man’s hand, and Brother Simon held it up to his face, and breathed it in, and said it was the best one yet.

The verses

As on a heap of rubbish cast upon the highway the lily will grow full of sweet perfume and delight, thus the disciple of the truly enlightened Buddha shines forth by his knowledge among those who are like rubbish, among the people that walk in darkness.Dhammapada 58–59, tr. F. Max Müller (1881)

In the commentary, two friends of Sāvatthī are divided by their teachers: Sirigutta follows the Buddha, and Garahadinna follows the Nigaṇṭhas, ascetics who claim to know past, present and future. Tired of being pestered, Sirigutta invites the ascetics to a meal of empty pots and tips them off rigged seats into a ditch of filth, then has them beaten. Garahadinna takes him to the king, who rules against Garahadinna. In revenge, Garahadinna invites the Buddha to a feast over a hidden pit of blazing charcoal and asks him to walk in first, alone. Lotuses as big as wheels rise from the coals, the empty pots fill with exactly what Garahadinna claimed they held, and the Buddha speaks of the lotus that grows from a rubbish heap by the road. Both friends enter the stream. That evening the Buddha tells of a past life in which he walked over a fiery pit made by Māra to feed a holy man, and a lotus rose beneath his feet. Mashpee, 1878

Mashpee on Cape Cod was a Wampanoag community governed for generations as a plantation and district under state control. In 1833 its people, with the Pequot preacher William Apess, staged the Woodland Revolt to win back some self-government. The town’s first Baptist congregation was gathered by Blind Joe Amos, a Mashpee preacher born in 1805 and totally blind, who learned the Bible by heart from his mother’s reading and was ordained in 1830. After Massachusetts passed its 1869 act making Indians citizens, the state incorporated Mashpee as a town in May 1870, over many residents’ objections, which opened its land to sale. Amos had told the legislature’s committee, we are not ready for it now. Mashpee men went whaling out of New Bedford, cut cordwood and worked the bogs and shellfish beds. The clambake, cooked on red-hot stones under rockweed, is an old Wampanoag way of cooking.

In 1877 Spiritualists held their first camp meeting at Onset Bay in Wareham, which soon became one of the country’s leading centers of the movement. Mediums there and elsewhere claimed to carry messages from the dead, and over the 1870s it became common for them to speak through “Indian” spirit controls, invented chiefs and maidens. The Onset camp later declined amid exposures of fraudulent mediums. More on Spiritualism.
