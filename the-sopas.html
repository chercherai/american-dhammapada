Walking the American WayCommentary 13.10 · Asadisadāna

The Sopas

To what purpose is this waste?
— Matthew 26:8 (Douay-Rheims)

Foi assim que me contaram, at the hall in Valeta.

I. The Domingas

Valeta, Merced County, California · spring 2022

Frank Mendonça milked fifty-eight hundred cows on two dairies on the west side of the county, and in the spring of 2022, for the first time in his life, milk was worth what it cost to make and then some. The checks that came from the co-op in April were the biggest anybody in Valeta had ever seen. Feed was dear and diesel was dear and the drought had the canals running at a third, but the price of a hundredweight of milk went past twenty-five dollars, and the dairymen who had held on through 2009 and 2015 and the years between drove to Mass in new trucks and did not apologize for it.

That was also the first spring in three that the hall could keep the festa the old way. In 2020 there had been no festa. In 2021 the ladies had put the sopas into foam boxes and handed them out of the kitchen window to cars. So everybody wanted to give. That was the trouble, if it was trouble.

Valeta was one of the last halls in California that still kept the domingas the way they kept them on Terceira, where Frank had been born and where his father had come from in 1971 with eleven cows' worth of savings and four children. From Easter to Pentecost the silver crown of the Holy Ghost, with the dove on top, went home each week with a different family. They made an altar for it in the front room with white cloth and gladiolas. Every night the neighbors came and prayed the terço in front of it, and on the Sunday the family that had the crown gave a dinner, and then the crown went back to church and out again to the next house.

Frank took the first week. Three hundred people ate tri-tip and sopas under the walnut trees at the home dairy. On the second Sunday the crown went to the Machados, who milked four hundred cows, and the Machados, the Silveiras, the Avilas and half the hall put their money together. The ladies of the auxiliary cooked for two days. Six hundred people ate. Frank's wife told him how many. He said nothing. The third week he had the crown back by arrangement and fed nine hundred. On the fourth the hall answered with twelve hundred and a band from San Jose. Frank fed fifteen hundred on the fifth, with a fado singer flown in from Ponta Delgada. On the sixth Sunday the hall, which by now meant everybody in Valeta who was not Frank, fed two thousand people and put on a bloodless bullfight in the arena behind the hall, with a cavaleiro from Portugal on a grey horse.

Nobody had planned it. It had simply happened, the way the price of milk had happened, and nobody could stop it now. The old women said it was a scandal and loved every minute.

On Memorial Day, the Monday after the bullfight, Frank Mendonça did not go out to the dairy. He lay on the recliner in the den with the blinds down and the television on with no sound. When the foreman called, he did not pick up.

II. São

The home dairy · Memorial Day

His wife found him there at noon. Conceição Mendonça was sixty-four and had been born in Valeta, in the house behind the hall where her father was caretaker, and in 1975 she had been the festa queen, at seventeen, in a cape of red velvet four yards long that her mother had embroidered with wheat and doves by hand. Everybody called her São.

What's this, she said. Are you sick?

You don't know?

I don't know anything. Tell me.

So he told her, lying in the recliner with his boots still on. That he was the biggest dairyman on the west side of the county and the president of the festa that year, and the hall had outgiven him every second Sunday since Easter. That there was one Sunday left, the big one, Pentecost, and he could not see how to beat two thousand people and a bullfight. That if Frank Mendonça could not outgive the hall in Valeta in the best milk year there had ever been, he did not know what he was for.

São looked at him for a while.

Have you ever heard, she said, of a king who lets the people beat him? Get up. I'll do the festa.

She sat on the arm of the recliner and told him how, counting on her fingers.

The dairy had shade cloth, the white cloth they stretched over the feed lanes so the cows could eat in July. He should have his men put it up on poles over the whole lot behind the hall, the parking and the arena and the lawn, so that fifteen hundred people could sit down at once out of the sun. The hall did not have that cloth and could not buy it by Sunday.

The copper kettles in the hall kitchen, the old ones, the ones from before the steam kettles, should be polished until they looked like gold, and set out under the shade where people could see them.

She would call the queens. Every festa queen of Valeta she could find, going back to 1950. Most of them were still alive and most of them still had their capes in garment bags in a back closet. They would come in their capes and wait on the tables themselves. They would pour the broth. Nobody had ever seen that. The hall could not make it happen because the hall did not have São, who had been the queen of 1975 and the mother of the queens of 1997 and 2001 and the grandmother of the queen of 2019, and had every one of their telephone numbers.

She would strip every mint bed in Valeta, and there would be a sprig of fresh mint on every plate of sopas, so the smell of it would be under the shade cloth all afternoon.

And in the Saturday procession every queen would walk beside a heifer from Frank's herd, washed and brushed, with paper flowers on her halter. One heifer for every queen. The Machados had four hundred cows. The Mendonças had fifty-eight hundred.

And the first tables, she said, don't go to us. They go to the milkers and their families. Ours and everybody's. And the men from the shelter in Merced, and the old people from the senior apartments, and the people who pick the almonds. They sit down first and the queens serve them. That's how the queen did it. The real one. She meant the Queen Isabel of the old story, who fed the poor out of her own kitchen in a famine. The hall has never done that either.

Frank sat up.

That's good, he said. That's very good.

He put his boots on the floor and called the foreman back.

III. The Bull

The road to the church · Saturday, June 4

Sixty-two queens came. The oldest was eighty-seven, the queen of 1952, in a cape of white satin gone the color of cream, with her granddaughter holding up the train. The youngest had been crowned the year before in an empty church in a mask. They came from Hilmar and Gustine and Turlock and Tulare and one from Boise, and they stood in the hall parking lot on Saturday morning in their capes, red and blue and white and gold, embroidered with doves and wheat and roses and crowns, while the dairy crew led the heifers down off the trailers.

On Friday night one of the heifers had gone lame. There were sixty-one.

Frank found São behind the trailers. One queen doesn't have a heifer, he said. What do I do?

What do you mean? There are cattle all over that dairy.

Gentle ones. Halter-broke. The rest are milk cows that never saw a crowd, and they'll go crazy. There's nothing left on the trailers but the bull.

Everybody on the home dairy knew the bull. He was a Holstein two years old, black and white, a ton already, bred from the best line in the herd and kept for that. In March he had put a milker named Rigoberto through a pipe gate and into the hospital in Merced with four broken ribs. The milkers called him Chamuco, which is a name for the devil. Nobody went in the pen with him alone.

I know a place he can walk, São said.

Where?

Beside Nelson.

Nelson Rocha was forty-three years old and worked in the calf barn at the home dairy, mixing milk replacer and feeding bottles to the newborn calves twice a day. In 1996, when he was seventeen, he had stabbed a boy to death in a fight behind the county fairgrounds, over nothing anybody could remember afterward, and he had been sentenced to twenty-five years to life. He had been in Folsom and Corcoran and Solano. After a law passed in Sacramento in 2013 gave men who had done their crimes before they were eighteen a special parole hearing in their twenty-fifth year, he had his hearing, and the board found him suitable, and the governor let it stand, and in the summer of 2021 he came home to Valeta. Nelson's father had milked for Frank's father for thirty years. Frank gave him the calf barn because nobody else would give him anything. The boy Nelson had killed was a Machado cousin. The Machados were in the parking lot that morning, and the dead boy's mother, and everybody knew it.

Frank looked at his wife a long time. Then he sent for the bull.

They brought him down off the last trailer with two men on the lead and the crew standing back. Frank's foreman put the lead in Nelson's hand. Nelson took it without a word and walked the bull out to his place at the back of the procession, beside the last queen, who was São, because São had given her own heifer to the girl from Boise.

The bull stopped beside Nelson and put his tail down between his legs. He dropped his ears. He closed his eyes. He stood there with his great head hanging, perfectly still, and did not move again until Nelson moved, and then he walked when Nelson walked, the whole mile to the church and the whole mile back, with São in her red cape on the other side of him, and stood with his eyes shut on the church steps while the band played. Children came up and put their hands flat on his side. He let them.

People along the road took out their phones. People who had known that bull since he was a calf stood with their mouths open. One of the old milkers from Michoacán crossed himself.

Look at that animal, people said. Look how he walks.

IV. Four Lines

The hall · Pentecost Sunday, June 5

After the Mass and the crowning and the parade, the people came in under the shade cloth.

Frank's crew had worked two nights stringing it. It covered the whole lot from the hall to the arena fence, white, a little translucent, and the noon light came down through the weave of it soft and even, so that it was like sitting inside a cloud. Under it were tables for fifteen hundred and the copper kettles on their stands, polished so that they threw the light back, and the smell of the sopas, which is beef and cabbage and bread and allspice and cinnamon cooked down all night, and over everything the smell of the mint. São had stripped the mint beds of half the town. A sprig of it lay on every plate.

The first tables went to the milkers and their wives and children, from every dairy on the west side, and the almond pickers, and the men from the shelter, and the old people from the senior apartments who came on the church van. They sat down and looked around them, not sure. Then the queens came down the rows in their capes, the queen of 1952 on her granddaughter's arm, the queen of 1975, the queen of the empty church, sixty-two of them, carrying pitchers of broth. They bent and poured it over the bread in the bowls and laid the meat on top with their own hands, then went back for more. The capes dragged in the gravel. Nobody minded. Some of the milkers' wives were crying. A boy from the shelter asked the queen of 1966 if she was a real queen, and she said, Today, honey, and gave him another piece of meat.

Before anybody ate, the priest blessed the sopas. That was always the custom. When old Father Goulart had been alive, he had stood in front of the kettles every Pentecost for thirty-one years and blessed the food in Portuguese for twenty minutes by the clock, the Holy Ghost and Queen Isabel and the founders of the hall by name, the dead of the year by name, the cattle and the rain and the price of milk, until the broth went cold and the children cried. People had complained about it every year. Everybody missed it.

Father Goulart had died in 2019. The priest now was Father Ikenna Nwosu, who was thirty-four years old and came from Nnewi, in the southeast of Nigeria, and had been lent by his bishop to the Diocese of Fresno for three years because the diocese did not have priests enough to go around. He had learned to say Mass in Portuguese from a book and from the old women of the parish, who corrected him after every Mass in the vestibule, and he said it slowly and very correctly with an accent nobody in Valeta had ever heard before. He was tall and very quiet. He did not talk much at the best of times. The old women had decided in his first month that they loved him, and he had not been able to do anything about it.

Frank took him by the elbow up to the kettles and stood beside him with his hat off. Everybody under the shade cloth went quiet.

Father Ikenna looked out at it all a long time. The white cloth, the copper, the queens in their capes, the milkers' children, the men from the shelter, the mint on the plates. He looked at Frank. Then he looked over Frank's shoulder at two men standing by the end kettle, and his face changed, and he looked from one of them to the other.

One of them was Darren Pimentel, who was the controller for Mendonça Dairies, a careful man from Fresno with a degree in accounting, who had worked for Frank six years and kept his books cleaner than any dairy books in the county. Darren was standing by the kettle with his phone in his hand, adding. He had been adding all week. The shade cloth alone, with the poles and the labor, came to more than forty thousand. The sixty-one heifers, which Frank had told the hall that morning were to be auctioned and every dollar given away and matched out of his pocket. The flights for the queens who came from far off, which Frank had paid. The beef and the bread. The tables. The overtime for the crew. Darren had the number on the screen of his phone, and it was more than three hundred thousand dollars, in one day, in one parking lot. And he was thinking: in the best year he will ever have, he has thrown this away. In one afternoon. And all these people will eat it and go home and lie down and take a nap.

The other was Frank's uncle Lucindo, who was eighty-two and had been secretary of the hall since 1979, a little dry man in a white shirt buttoned to the neck. Lucindo was standing a step behind Darren, and the tears were running down his face, and he was thinking: nobody but Frank could have done this. Nobody in Valeta has ever seen anything like it. And it is for everybody, every one of them, Portuguese or not, and the poor first. Thank God I lived to see it.

Father Ikenna looked at the two of them for a long moment. Then he lifted his right hand over the kettles and said, in English, the grace that every Catholic child in America learns before the first grade.

Bless us, O Lord, and these thy gifts, which we are about to receive from thy bounty, through Christ our Lord. Amen.

He made the sign of the cross over the food. He took the aspergillum from the altar boy and shook holy water over the kettles, once, twice, three times, and gave it back. He ate one bowl of sopas, standing, by the kitchen door. And then, before the auction, before the dancing, before anyone had thought to stop him, he got into his car and drove back to the rectory.

•••

That evening, after the auction, Frank stood up on the stage in the hall in front of everybody and said that whatever was left over in the hall that night, the meat and the bread and the broth and the kettles of it, belonged to whoever wanted to take it home, and the hall should open its kitchen door and let them. The heifers had brought two hundred and eight thousand dollars at the auction. Frank said he would match it, and it would all go to the food bank in Merced and the farmworker clinic and the shelter. The hall stood up and clapped for a long time.

Frank did not hear much of it. He went home and lay down again.

V. The Rectory

Valeta · Monday, June 6

In the morning, in the hall kitchen, the ladies scrubbing the kettles asked Nelson Rocha, who had come to help carry, whether he had not been afraid on Saturday, walking a mile with that animal on a rope.

No, Nelson said. I wasn't afraid.

One of the Machado men, who was stacking chairs, said loud enough for the kitchen to hear that a man who had done twenty-five years would say anything. Nobody answered him. Nelson went on carrying.

Later, when somebody told Father Ikenna about it, the priest said: He told you the truth. That man has nothing left in him to be afraid with.

•••

Frank had not slept. He had lain awake all night thinking: I gave the best gift anybody in this valley ever gave, and the priest said four lines over it, the same four lines a child says over a peanut-butter sandwich, and walked out. Father Goulart would have talked an hour. It must be that I did it wrong. It must be that I gave the wrong things, or gave them the wrong way, or that the priest is angry with me about something, because a priest always blesses a gift in proportion to the gift. Everybody knows that.

At eight he drove to the rectory. Father Ikenna was in the kitchen in his shirtsleeves making tea, and he made Frank a cup and sat down across from him.

Father. Did I do something wrong yesterday? Did I give the wrong thing?

Why do you ask?

Four lines. You said four lines and you left.

What you gave was right, the priest said. All of it. The cloth and the queens and the poor first. A gift like that a man gives once in his life, maybe. Nobody in this valley will see one like it again.

Then why?

Father Ikenna turned his cup on the table.

Because the company was not clean, he said. There were two men by the end kettle. One of them was so glad for what you did that he was crying. The other one was sick to his heart over the money. Sick. He stood there adding it up and hating every bowl. If I had blessed that food the way it deserved, the way Father Goulart would have, the whole hour, with all of them listening, I think it would have broken something in him that does not mend. I was sorry for him. So I said the short one.

He told Frank which two men.

Frank drove straight to the office at the home dairy. Darren was at his desk with the spreadsheet open.

Is it true, Frank said, what you were thinking yesterday by the kettle?

Darren looked at him and did not ask how he knew. He was an honest man in his way. Yes, he said. It's true. It's my job to think it, Frank. Somebody has to.

Frank stood in the doorway.

I never took one dollar that was yours, he said. Not one. What I gave yesterday I gave out of my own, with my wife and my children. What did I do to you? Take what's yours and go. I'll pay you through the year. I don't want to see you at the dairy, and I don't want to see you at the hall.

Darren went back to Fresno that week. He found other dairies to keep books for. He was good at it.

Then Frank drove out to the hall, where his uncle Lucindo was sitting in the little office off the kitchen with the festa ledger open, writing in the totals by hand the way he had since 1979.

Tio. Is it true what you were thinking yesterday?

The old man did not ask how he knew either. It's true, he said.

Good, said Frank. That's good, tio. I'm glad. He put the checkbook for the festa account on the desk in front of his uncle, and his own credit card on top of it. Then you do it. All this week. Every day, the way we did it yesterday. Whatever it costs. Whoever comes.

•••

He went back to the rectory before noon. The priest was in the garden with the hose.

Father, Frank said, look what that man did to me. I gave all that, and he stands there and does that. And he told him the rest, what he had said to Darren in the doorway, word for word.

Father Ikenna turned off the hose. He went into the rectory and came back with an old Bible with a cracked black cover, the Douay, that had been Father Goulart's and was still full of his holy cards, and turned the pages with his wet thumb, and gave it to Frank open.

You said it already, he said. Read it.

But he answering said to one of them: Friend, I do thee no wrong: didst thou not agree with me for a penny? Take what is thine, and go thy way: I will also give to this last even as to thee. Or, is it not lawful for me to do what I will? is thy eye evil, because I am good?

— Matthew 20:13–15 (Douay-Rheims)

Frank read it twice standing on the path. Then the priest took the book back and found another place and read it out himself, slowly, in his careful English.

Now this I say: He who soweth sparingly, shall also reap sparingly: and he who soweth in blessings, shall also reap blessings. Every one as he hath determined in his heart, not with sadness, or of necessity: for God loveth a cheerful giver.

— 2 Corinthians 9:6–7 (Douay-Rheims)

Not with sadness, he said. The man who stands beside a gift and cannot be glad for it, he is the poor one. He goes hungry at the biggest table in the world. And the one who can be glad for another man's gift, even if he gave nothing, he eats. He closed the book. Your uncle ate very well yesterday.

VI. The Octave

The hall · June 6 to 12

Lucindo Mendonça fed Valeta for seven days.

He kept the shade cloth up. He kept the kettles going. He called the ladies of the auxiliary and they came, and the milkers' wives came to help them without being asked, and on Tuesday some of the queens came back in their capes, and on Wednesday more. Every day at noon he opened the gates and fed whoever walked in, the same as on Pentecost, the poor first, and he sat at the end of the first table himself and ate with them, which in forty-three years as secretary he had never once done. People who had known him all their lives said he was changed. They could not say how. His sister-in-law said that he laughed now, at nothing, at the children, at the mint, and that he had never laughed before in his life that she could remember.

Nelson Rocha carried kettles all week, and at five every morning and five every evening he went back to the calf barn and fed the calves. On Thursday the Machado man who had called him a liar came into the hall kitchen and stood next to him at the sink and dried what Nelson washed. Neither of them said anything. On Friday the dead boy's mother sat at the first table. Nelson poured her broth. Her hand shook on the spoon, and his on the pitcher, and that was all.

On the last evening, Sunday, after the last people had gone, Lucindo locked the kitchen and went out under the shade cloth to look at it all one more time before Frank's crew took it down in the morning.

The sun was low over the Coast Range and coming in sideways under the edge of the cloth. The light through the weave of it lay across the empty tables and the swept gravel in a fine net of bright squares, thousands of them, each one with the same light in it, and when the evening wind lifted the cloth the whole net moved at once. In the gravel between the tables, everywhere, trodden flat by a week of feet, were the stems of the mint, and they were still green.

Background

The verse

The uncharitable do not go to the world of the gods; fools only do not praise liberality; a wise man rejoices in liberality, and through it becomes blessed in the other world.Dhammapada 177, tr. F. Max Müller (1881)

In the commentary, King Pasenadi of Kosala and the citizens of his capital try to outdo each other in feeding the Buddha and his monks, and the citizens keep winning. The king lies down in despair until Queen Mallikā devises gifts only a king could give: a pavilion of sāla wood, five hundred white parasols held by five hundred elephants, golden boats of perfume, noblewomen to fan and serve the monks. One monk lacks an elephant; the only one left is a rogue, and Mallikā has it stand beside Aṅgulimāla, the reformed murderer, where it drops its ears, shuts its eyes and stands motionless. These are the "Gifts beyond Compare," given once in each Buddha's lifetime, and always, the commentary notes, arranged by a woman. But of the king's two ministers, Kāla resents the waste while Juṇha rejoices, and the Buddha, out of pity for Kāla, gives only a four-line blessing. When the king learns why, he banishes Kāla, gives Juṇha the kingdom's means to give alms for seven days, and the Buddha speaks this verse. Read the full commentary story, or about King Pasenadi.

The San Joaquin Valley, 2022

Azorean immigrants brought the festas of the Holy Spirit to California in the nineteenth century. In the Azores the silver crown, topped with a dove, passes from house to house on the Sundays between Easter and Pentecost, with the rosary prayed before it each night. The devotion is traced to Queen Isabel of Portugal, who fed the poor in a famine. California festas add crowned girl queens and a free meal for everyone who comes. In Gustine, in Merced County, dairymen lead decorated calves in the procession, the animals are auctioned for the society's charity, and volunteers serve sopas of beef, cabbage and bread.

In 2022 U.S. dairy farmers saw record milk prices, averaging $25.39 per hundredweight, though high feed and labor costs ate into margins and prices fell the next year. California's youth offender parole hearings, created by SB 260 in 2014 for people whose crimes were committed before age 18, give special weight to youth and later growth; for those serving 25 years to life, the hearing comes in the twenty-fifth year. With fewer American vocations, U.S. dioceses have increasingly recruited priests from Africa, including Nigeria.
