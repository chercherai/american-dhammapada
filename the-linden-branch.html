Așa am auzit, on Detroit Avenue.

I. The Photograph

The Romanian West Side, Cleveland · 1910

On Detroit Avenue, a few doors from the church, there was a photographer’s studio with a window full of brides, and in the middle of the window, for three years, hung the photograph of Ileana Bratu.

She had come from Săliște, below the mountains in Transylvania, in the spring of 1907, to marry Ioan Bratu, who kept a boarding house for twenty-two men from Săliște and a grocery under it, at the corner of West Fifty-fourth Street. She was nineteen. She had brought with her one trunk, and in the trunk her mother’s best ie, the white blouse worked at the shoulders and cuffs in black and red, so close that the linen hardly showed, and the week after the wedding Ioan took her down to the studio in it and had her photographed, standing, with one hand on a carved chair and her hair down her back as a girl wears it, though she was a married woman. The photographer asked to keep a print for the window. Ioan, who was proud, said yes.

The men in the boarding house called her Cosânzeana, after the princess in the stories, Ileana Cosânzeana, who is so beautiful you can look at the sun but not at her. They said it at first behind her back and then to her face, and she let them. The men who came over in the next three years from Săliște had seen the photograph in the window before they ever saw her, and they said she was more beautiful than her picture, which was true. She knew it. On Sundays she stood at the front of St. Mary’s with the married women, in a black silk kerchief that she wore a little farther back on her head than the others did, and she knew where the men were looking, and that was where she wanted them to look.

There was only one person in the colony she would not go near.

That was Maica Agafia, an old nun who had come out from the convent at Văratec in Moldavia in 1908, the only nun any of them had seen in America. Nobody quite knew why she had come. Some said she had a nephew at the ore docks who had been killed, and she had come to see the place. Some said her abbess had sent her. She was small and bent, in black, with a black veil over her cap, and she went to every death in the colony, and sat up all night by the body reading the Psalter, and walked behind the coffin to the cemetery. At a parastas the winter before, at the long table after the memorial, somebody had said what a beautiful corpse the dead girl made, and Maica Agafia had said, not loudly, A face is lent. Ask the grave what it gives back.

Ileana had heard about it the same evening. After that, if she saw the black veil coming along Detroit Avenue, she crossed the street.

II. The Wood on the Rocky River

West Fifty-fourth Street · spring and summer

Ioan Bratu was not a priest’s son, but he had been raised to fear God and to give, and he did both. He had saved, and bought land, as men from Săliște did, and one of the pieces he had bought was eleven acres of beech and maple woods on the bluff over the Rocky River, west of the city, for which he had no use at all except that the beeches reminded him of home. In the summer of 1909 he had given Maica Agafia leave to live there. Some of the men from the boarding house went out on a Sunday and built her a hut of logs and boards under the trees, with a little porch and a shelf for an icon, and after that she lived in the woods like the old hermits in the mountains at home, and came into the city only for the dead.

People began to go out to her. They went out Detroit Avenue on the streetcar and walked the last of it, women mostly, at first, and then some of the men, on Sunday afternoons, and sat on the ground in front of her porch, and she talked to them a little, not much. They came back quieter than they went. Ioan went every Sunday he could. Ileana would not.

It grieved him. He did not know how to say so. He was a man who said things with money.

On Saturday nights a pair of lăutari played for the boarders in the big room over the grocery, a fiddler and a man with a cobza, Roma musicians from Sibiu who had come over in 1906 and played at every wedding on the West Side. In June Ioan gave them five dollars to make a song. He told them what to put in it. They made a doina, slow, the way the old ones are, about a wood of beeches over a river in a far country, so tall that the light came down through the leaves green, like water, and the moss was soft, and a spring came out of the bank, and the lindens were in flower and the bees were in the lindens, and an old woman sat there in the evening and anyone who came to her went home lighter. They played it every Saturday after that, last, when the men were quiet and had had their plum brandy.

Ileana heard it the first night from the kitchen. The second Saturday she came and stood in the door with her hands in her apron. The third Saturday she sat down.

It was the linden flowers that did it. There had been a linden at the end of her father’s garden in Săliște. She had not thought of it in three years.

What wood is that? she said to the fiddler, when they had finished. Where is it? Is it at home?

The fiddler looked at Ioan, and Ioan looked at the floor.

It’s your own wood, doamnă, the fiddler said. On the Rocky River. Your husband’s.

III. The Girl with the Branch

The bluff over the Rocky River · a Sunday in July

She went the next day, in her good kerchief and the ie from the photograph, with two of the women from the boarding house. She told herself she was going to see the wood.

It was everything in the song. The beeches went up gray and smooth and the light came down green through them, and there was a smell of leaf mold and of water, and the river was loud below the bluff. A spring came out of the bank. At the edge of the trees by the hut there was a linden in full flower, and the bees in it made a sound like a held note on the fiddle.

Twenty or thirty people were sitting on the ground in front of the porch. Maica Agafia sat on the step in her black with her hands in her lap, talking to them in her small voice, and beside her, standing, a girl was fanning her with a branch of the linden to keep the flies off.

Ileana stopped where she was.

She had never seen the girl in the colony. She would have known. The girl was perhaps seventeen, in an ie like her own but finer, the work at the shoulders so close it looked like a single piece of red and black, and her hair was down her back. She was more beautiful than anyone Ileana had ever seen, or ever imagined, more beautiful than the photograph in the window, more beautiful than Ileana. Ileana knew it at once, the way you know the step is missing on a dark stair. Next to this girl, she was a kitchen girl. She was not a sixteenth part of it.

And she thought: they lied to me about the old woman. A face is lent, she said. And here she keeps the most beautiful girl in Cleveland standing beside her with a branch.

She did not hear a word Maica Agafia said. She did not sit. She stood at the back with the two women from the boarding house on either side of her and looked at the girl.

The girl was fanning slowly. The linden branch went back and forth. Ileana watched her arm, and her face, and her hair.

Then the face was not seventeen. It was twenty-five, a married woman’s face, and the hair was under a kerchief. Then it was thirty-five, and there were two lines from the nose to the corners of the mouth, and the arm with the branch was thicker. The branch went back and forth. Then it was fifty, and the cheeks had fallen in where the back teeth had gone, and the hands on the branch had big knuckles and brown spots on them. The branch went back and forth, slower. Then the woman was very old and bent, older than the maica, and the eyes were cloudy, and she was trembling and could hardly lift the branch, and the fine red and black of the ie hung on her like a sack on a post. Then she sank down onto the porch step and lay there, and it was a body, the face the color of tallow, the mouth open. Then the flies came that the branch had been keeping away. Then it was not a face. Then it was a heap of bones in the rags of the ie, with the linden branch beside them, its leaves already curling in the heat.

Nobody else on the ground in front of the porch moved. Afterward the two women from the boarding house swore they had seen nothing but Maica Agafia on the step, talking, alone, and a fallen linden branch on the boards beside her.

Ileana stood in the green light and thought, very clearly, as if somebody had written it on a slate: All of it in the time it takes to say the Our Father. There was nothing in it. There was never anything in it. And I have been living in it like a house.

Maica Agafia had stopped talking. She was looking at her across the heads of the people.

Ileana, she said. She had never spoken to her before. You thought there was something there that would stay. Look at it now. She did not point. That is a sack. It is a sack full of what the doctor sees and the woman who washes the dead sees. It leaks, at every opening it leaks, every day of its life, and it smells, and it gets sick, and it is only a fool who wants it. Look at it until you have seen it.

Ileana looked. Something in her that had been held tight for three years let go, the way a fist lets go in sleep, and she sat down on the ground where she was, among the others, in her good ie, on the beech mast.

The maica waited a while. The bees went on in the linden.

Then she said, to all of them but to Ileana, that people who want things cannot get out of the wanting by themselves. They go down in it like a spider going down its own thread, she said, the thread it spun out of its own belly; it thinks it is going somewhere, and it is only going down its own thread. And then, because she had been a long time among the educated nuns of Văratec and knew things by heart that she had not learned in a village, she said some verses of Eminescu, the poet, who had died the year she took the veil, and whose lady had come to Văratec that same summer and died there too, of grief, and was buried by the church. She said them in the plain way she said the Psalter over the dead, without making anything of them.

Ca un cântec de sirenă,
Lumea-ntinde lucii mreje;
Ca să schimbe-actorii-n scenă,
Te momește în vârteje;
Tu pe-alături te strecoară,
Nu băga nici chiar de seamă,
Din cărarea ta afară
De te-ndeamnă, de te cheamă.

— Eminescu, Glossă

Like a siren’s song, the world spreads out its shining nets. Ileana understood every word. She had been in the net so long she had thought it was the sky.

Zică toți ce vor să zică,
Treacă-n lume cine-o trece;
Ca să nu-ndrăgești nimică,
Tu rămâi la toate rece.

— Eminescu, Glossă

Let them all say what they will say. So as to love nothing, stay cool to all of it. When the maica stopped, Ileana was sitting very straight on the beech mast with her hands open in her lap, and her face was perfectly still, and the two women from the boarding house, who had known her three years, said afterward that they had been afraid to speak to her. It was not that she looked unhappy. It was that she did not seem to be anywhere that they could reach.

IV. Ioan

West Fifty-fourth Street · the following week

She did not take off the kerchief. She did not go down to the studio and ask for the photograph, which hung in the window until the photographer took it down himself, in the fall, to make room. She cooked for twenty-two men and washed their shirts and swept, and did it all very well, and sat at night in the kitchen with her hands in her lap and the lamp out. She did not cry. She did not seem to be in any trouble at all. That was what frightened Ioan.

On the Sunday he went out to the wood by himself and sat on the porch step with Maica Agafia, two old Transylvanians, a long time without talking, the way men do at home.

What did you do to her? he said at last.

Nothing. She saw.

She doesn’t eat.

She will eat. The maica turned her prayer rope through her fingers. Listen to me, Ioan Bratu. You were good to me, so I will tell you plainly. She will not stay in your house now. It is not that she doesn’t love you. There is nothing in your house she wants, and nothing in this city, and nothing in her own face. A woman who has seen what she saw either goes to the monastery or she goes to the cemetery. I have seen it before. There is no third road for her.

Ioan sat with his big hands on his knees and looked down through the beeches at the river.

He was a man who said things with money. He took out his purse.

Not the cemetery, he said. Let her go, then. Let her go to Văratec. I’ll pay the ship.

The priest at St. Mary’s told him that no monastery would take a married woman without her husband’s leave, in writing. Ioan wrote it at the kitchen table, with the priest spelling the long words. He kept the boarding house for thirty-one more years and never married again, and the men from Săliște said he had become a strange one, giving money to everybody, and going out to the wood on the Rocky River every Sunday of his life, long after Maica Agafia was buried, to sit on the step of the empty hut.

V. Văratec

Neamț, Moldavia · after

She sailed from New York in October with a letter from Maica Agafia to the abbess, and her trunk, and in the trunk her mother’s ie, which she gave at Văratec to the sisters who did the embroidery, to unpick for the thread.

Văratec was a village of nuns, three hundred of them, in little whitewashed houses along lanes under the hills, each with its garden. She was given a garden. In it there was a linden.

She was tonsured as Monahia Agafia, at her own asking, after the old woman on the Rocky River. Visitors who came to Văratec in the years between the wars, when the convent was full of famous ladies from Bucharest going on retreat in good shoes, sometimes asked to be taken to the nun who had been in America, because they had heard she could answer anything. She could not answer everything. But she would let them sit on the bench under her linden and ask, and she would listen until they had heard their own question, and then say one thing. The ladies went home and quoted it at dinners, usually wrong.

There is a story that one of them, a beautiful young woman with a famous name, asked her once whether it was true that she had been the most beautiful girl in Cleveland, America, and that the old nun laughed, which she seldom did, and said, For three years. Then she reached up into the linden and broke off a little branch with the flowers on it and gave it to her to keep the flies off.

The young woman carried it as far as the gate. By then the flowers had begun to brown at the edges, and she stood a moment and looked at them, and did not throw it away.

Background

The verse

Those who are slaves to passions, run down with the stream (of desires), as a spider runs down the web which he has made himself; when they have cut this, at last, wise people leave the world free from cares, leaving all affection behind.Dhammapada 347, tr. F. Max Müller (1881)

In the commentary, Queen Khemā, wife of King Bimbisāra of Magadha, is so proud of her beauty that she will not go near the Buddha, because she has heard that he finds fault with beauty of form. The king has songs made in praise of his Bamboo Grove, which he had given to the Buddha, and when she hears them she asks what grove they are singing of; it is her own. When she goes, the Buddha makes a woman appear beside him, more beautiful than Khemā, fanning him with a palm-leaf fan, and Khemā cannot look away. The Buddha then ages the woman before her eyes to an old woman and at last to a heap of bones. Khemā sees that there is no core in the body, and the Buddha speaks a verse on the body as a sick, leaking thing that only fools desire, and then the verse of the spider and its web. She attains awakening. The Buddha tells the king she must now either go forth or pass away, and the king says, let her go forth. She became the nun the Buddha named foremost in wisdom. Read the full commentary story, or about Khemā.

The maica’s verses are from Glossă (1883) by Mihai Eminescu, Romania’s national poet, quoted in the Romanian; the English in the story is a plain gloss, not a published translation. Eminescu died in June 1889. His beloved, the poet Veronica Micle, withdrew after his funeral to the convent at Văratec, died there that August, and is buried in its cemetery.

Romanian Cleveland, 1910

By the First World War about 12,000 Romanians lived in Cleveland, most of them peasants from Transylvania, then part of Austria-Hungary. The largest settlement was on the West Side, between West 45th and West 65th streets on either side of Detroit Avenue, near the factories and docks where the men worked; most of the early immigrants were young men who lived in boarding houses run by families from their home districts. In 1904 they founded St. Mary’s, the first Romanian Orthodox parish in America, and built its first church on Detroit Avenue. See Romanians in the Encyclopedia of Cleveland History and St. Mary’s Romanian Orthodox Church on Cleveland Historical.

Văratec, in Neamț County, was founded in 1785 and grew into a village of nuns living in small houses along lanes around the church; in 1909 it had 304 nuns and sisters, and it was long known for its embroidery and weaving. In Orthodox tradition, as in Catholic, a married person may enter monastic life only with the consent of the spouse.
