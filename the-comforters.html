The Comforters

¡Vida, nada me debes! ¡Vida, estamos en paz!
— Amado Nervo, “En paz”

Así lo oí contar, in the section houses west of Newton.

I. The Crossing

Kesler siding, Harvey County, Kansas · July 1931

The Kesler section was eight miles of the Santa Fe main line west of Newton, double track on crushed rock across wheat country so flat that a man tamping ties at one end of it could see the water tank at the other end, wavering. Don Feliciano Arreola had been its foreman since 1919. He had a gang of eleven men, a motor car that started on the third pull, a tool house, and four old boxcars set off at the end of the siding on short rails of their own, with stovepipes through their roofs and little windows the men had cut with a keyhole saw and hung with flour-sack curtains. The married men lived in three of them with their wives and children. The fourth was the bunk car, where the single men slept, and the boys.

There were five boys that summer. Beto Ochoa was sixteen, and his father Tomás worked on the gang. Nacho Galván was seventeen. Rafa Lomelí said he was sixteen and was fifteen. The two Dávalos cousins, Chuy and Lalo, had come up from Laredo in April with a paper from a labor agent and nothing else. Don Feliciano had put all five in the bunk car with Chencho Bañuelos, who was fifty and snored and had carried a rifle for Obregón, because the boys were a little afraid of Chencho and would not stay out late in Newton.

Harvest came at the end of June. It was the biggest wheat crop Kansas had ever grown, and the trucks and wagons came to the elevators all day and half the night, and the price was thirty-three cents a bushel and going down, which was less than it cost to grow. The farmers hauled it in anyway, because it was in the field.

On the ninth of July, a little after four in the afternoon, a Mennonite farmer named Heinrich Voth stalled his Ford truck on the Kesler crossing with forty bushels of hard red wheat in the box and his wife beside him on the seat. The gang was spiking a joint two hundred yards east. Don Feliciano heard the starter grind, and grind, and stop. Then he heard, far off in the west, the whistle of the eastbound freight, and he said one word, and eleven men ran with their bars.

There was no time to push the truck. They set their lining bars under the frame and the axle, the way they set them under a rail, and Chencho called the count, and they lifted it and walked it sideways off the planks and let it down into the ditch on its own springs while the wheat slid in the box like water. The freight came through a moment after, sixty cars, and the wind of it took Beto's hat. Mrs. Voth had not got down from the cab. She sat with both hands flat on the dashboard and watched the cars go by her window as if she were counting them.

When it was gone the men stood in the ditch wiping their faces. Don Feliciano filled the dipper from the water keg on the motor car and gave it to Rafa first, and then to Lalo, and then to the other boys, and then to the men, and drank last. Mrs. Voth had got down by then. She watched him do it.

She spoke German to her husband and English to the foreman, a careful English with the corners turned up. She asked where the boys slept. He walked her to the bunk car and she looked in at the bunks, which were planks with straw ticks and one army blanket apiece, and she said that in January a boxcar was a cold place.

In January it is a cold place, Don Feliciano said.

Our sewing circle makes comforters, she said. For the relief. For Russia, for China, for whoever needs. We will make for your boys. For the winter. One each.

He told her it was not necessary. Her husband had just been offered thirty-three cents for his wheat. She said it was already decided, and she took an elevator ticket out of her apron pocket and wrote on the back of it with a pencil stub, 5 Decken, Kesler, Januar, and showed it to him, so that it was written.

Don Feliciano took off his hat to her. The men had never seen him do that for anybody but the priest who came out from Newton twice a year and the roadmaster's wife.

II. Night School

The tool house · autumn 1931

Amparo Ledesma came out from Newton on Tuesday and Thursday nights in her brother's Ford. She was twenty-three. She had been born in a section house at Emporia and finished the high school there and two years at the Teachers College before the money ran out, and now she dressed chickens on the line at the Fred Harvey plant on East First Street, eight hours a day, and twice a week she drove eight miles to sit at a plank table in the Kesler tool house under a hanging lantern with any man who wanted his letters. She did it for nothing. She was small and plain and said very little, and the men, who were twice her age and some of them three times, called her Señorita and stood up when she came in.

Don Feliciano came every time. He could read printed Spanish slowly with his finger and he could not write at all. For eighteen years he had signed the Santa Fe's time book with a mark. He sat at the end of the bench with his dinner pail on his knees, both hands around it, as if somebody might need it handed to them. By October he could write FELICIANO in capitals that leaned on one another, and then ARREOLA, which was harder, and he practiced on the backs of the forms the roadmaster sent out.

She taught from whatever there was. A first reader somebody's cousin had brought from Guadalajara. The Santa Fe rule book. Poems cut from La Prensa, the San Antonio paper that reached the Ranchito a week late and went from boxcar to boxcar until it fell apart. One Thursday she brought them a poem of Amado Nervo's, who had been dead twelve years and whom all their mothers had known by heart, the one in which a man near his sunset blesses Life and tells it they are even.

Chencho did not like it. Even, he said. Life owes me plenty. Ask me. I'll give you the list. The men laughed, and the Señorita let them laugh, and then she had Chencho read the line about the roses out loud with his finger under it, slowly, cuando planté rosales, coseché siempre rosas, and he read it and said it was a lie, but a pretty one.

Don Feliciano said nothing. He copied the last line onto the back of a form in his leaning capitals and folded it into his shirt.

The comforters began to come in October. Three came first, on the seat of a farm wagon, because the Voths had sold the truck. They were pieced from old dresses and the backs of worn-out shirts, in squares, and filled with carded wool, and tied every hand's width with a knot of red yarn whose two ends stood up like the ears of a rabbit. Mrs. Voth said the other two would come when the frame was free. There had been a death in the church, and then a wedding, and the frame was needed for both. Don Feliciano gave the three to the youngest, Rafa and Lalo and Beto. Nacho and Chuy would have the next.

The roadmaster came out on the first Monday in November. The Kesler section would be joined to the Halstead section on the first of December, under the Halstead foreman, with seven men. Four would be let go. Don Feliciano could stay on as a laborer, at a laborer's pay, if he liked. He thanked the roadmaster and said he would think about it. That night he told nobody what he thought.

All that fall, families had been leaving the Ranchito in Newton. The county had cut them off relief, and the big companies were saying what they said everywhere that year, that a Mexican would be better off among his own people, and a man from the consulate in Kansas City had sat two days at a table in the back of a grocery on West First taking names for the reduced fare to the border. On Tuesday Don Feliciano rode the local into Newton and gave his name. He was going to his sister in Tangancícuaro. He had not seen her since 1911.

Tomás Ochoa worked it out first, sitting on the bunk-car step with a cigarette. Seven was seven. If the foreman stayed on as a laborer, he was one of the seven, and one of the four was a boy.

III. The Address

The end of November

On his last Sunday he rode into Newton and walked out to the Ledesma house at the edge of the Ranchito to give back the pencil and the tablet. The Señorita would not take them. Her mother put him at the kitchen table and put coffee in his hand, because he would not ask for it, and he sat there two hours with his hat on his knee. Nobody remembered afterward what was said. Her mother said only that it was quiet, and that when he left he bowed to the girl as if she were the priest.

On Monday morning, the last day of the Kesler section, he called Chencho and the boys to the motor car before the whistle. He gave Chencho a page from the tablet. On it, in the leaning capitals, was his sister's name and the name of her street in Tangancícuaro, Michoacán, the first thing he had ever written that was not his own name.

When the señora Voth brings the other two, he said, take them. Give one to Nacho. Chuy is going with the Emporia extra gang, so send his on to him there, by the express. And if she does not bring them, write and tell me.

Chencho folded the page in four and put it in his hatband. Rafa asked if Don Feliciano wanted his own comforter back, for the train, because the trains to El Paso were cold. Don Feliciano looked at him a while and said no.

Then he picked up a cardboard suitcase with a strap around it and walked down the ties to the crossing to wait for the local, and the gang went out on the motor car to tamp, and nobody looked back at the crossing, because he had taught them that a man watches the track in front of the car.

IV. What the Señorita Said

The tool house · Thursday night

Seven men came to the night class that Thursday, under the Halstead foreman now, and they were early, and they talked while they waited.

Eighteen years, Chencho said, and what does he take home? A cardboard suitcase. And still. Send me word about the blankets. What does he want, that I mail him two comforters to Michoacán so he can sell them in the plaza?

He was always careful with things, said Amador Rentería.

A man doesn't change at the end, said his brother Lino. Even a good man. At the very end there is still a little something he wants.

The boys said nothing. Rafa sat on his hands. Tomás Ochoa looked at the floor.

The Señorita came in with the cold, unwound her scarf, and stood a moment at the end of the plank table looking at them, and they all stood up.

What are you talking about?

Chencho told her. He was a soldier and he told it straight, and he was a little ashamed by the end of it.

No, she said. He doesn't want anything. He was thinking about two people. The señora said she would make five. She wrote it down. It is hers to give, and if nobody is at the crossing to take it, or nobody tells her, she loses the giving of it. He did not want her to lose that. And the boys were promised. It is theirs. That is all he was thinking.

Then she sat down and took the clipping out of her coat, soft from folding, and put it in front of Chencho, and he read it aloud with his finger under it, slower than he had read it in October.

Cierto, a mis lozanías va a seguir el invierno:
¡mas tú no me dijiste que mayo fuese eterno!

Amé, fui amado, el sol acarició mi faz.
¡Vida, nada me debes! ¡Vida, estamos en paz!

— Amado Nervo, “En paz”

Winter comes after the green years, the old man had said; you never told me May would last. Life, you owe me nothing. Life, we are even.

Chencho sat with his finger on the last line for a long time. Then he said it was still a lie, and after a while he said, But not for him, and the Señorita opened the first reader to where they had left off.

The other two came in January, after the first big snow, on a bobsled behind the Voths' team, with the Voths' oldest boy driving and a hot brick wrapped in a sack under his feet. They were blue and brown, from a man's suits, and the red knots stood up out of them like the others.

Chencho carried one to Nacho in the bunk car. The other he took into Newton on the local, wrapped in brown paper and tied with twine, and at the Railway Express window in the depot he told the clerk he would write the tag himself. He wrote it slowly with the clerk's pen, in capitals that leaned on one another, CHUY DÁVALOS, SECTION HOUSE, EMPORIA, and the clerk read it back to him twice to be sure.

Then he bought a stamped envelope at the counter, and took the page out of his hatband, and copied the address in Tangancícuaro onto the envelope letter by letter, the way it was written, and inside, on a sheet of the clerk's paper, because the clerk was waiting and he did not know how to write more, he put one word.

Background

The verse

Him I call indeed a Brahmana who fosters no desires for this world or for the next, has no inclinations, and is unshackled.Dhammapada 410, tr. F. Max Müller (1881)

In the commentary, the Elder Sāriputta spends the rains retreat at a monastery with five hundred monks, and the lay people there promise him all the requisites. When the season ends, not everything has come. As he leaves to see the Buddha, he asks the monks to send on whatever is brought for the young monks and novices, or else to send him word. The monks decide that craving must still be alive in him. The Buddha tells them it is not so: Sāriputta was thinking only that the givers should not lose the merit of their gift, and that the young ones should not lose what was rightly theirs. Harvey County, Kansas, 1931

From the 1910s the Santa Fe and other railroads recruited Mexican men to build and maintain their lines in Kansas, many from Michoacán, Jalisco and Guanajuato, housing them in converted boxcars or shacks beside the tracks. Colonies grew up at the division points, and at Newton the Santa Fe housed Mexican families along West First Street from about 1911, in the neighborhood people called the Ranchito. Harvey County was also the heart of Kansas Mennonite country; Russian Mennonites settled there in the 1870s with the Santa Fe's help and brought hard winter wheat with them. In 1931 Kansas grew the largest wheat crop in its history, 251 million bushels, and the farm price fell to about 33 cents a bushel.

Between 1929 and 1939, most likely somewhere between half a million and a million people of Mexican descent, forty to sixty percent of them American citizens, left or were pushed out of the United States in what is called the Mexican Repatriation, through deportation, relief cuts, pressure from employers and local officials, and cheap train fares to the border; 1931 was the peak year. Many Mexican readers in the United States followed the San Antonio daily La Prensa. Amado Nervo (1870–1919), one of the best-known Mexican poets of his time, wrote “En paz” in 1915. Parcels went by the Railway Express Agency, the express company sixty-nine railroads took over in 1929.
