The Cedar Moon

The light of the body is the eye: if therefore thine eye be single, thy whole body shall be full of light.
— Matthew 6:22

I heard it told like this, on the courthouse square in Ransom.

I. Red Cedar

The Llano River country, Texas · 1906

Mr. J. W. Stucky bought cedar posts for a yard in San Antonio, and he was known from the Pedernales to the San Saba as the man who never forgot a name. Before he went up any creek he would stop at the last store and get the names: who lived up there, and his wife's name, and the names of the children in order, and which one had been sick. Then he would drive his buggy up to the door and call the woman by her name, and ask after each child by its own name, and leave a bolt of calico and a sack of candy and a pocketknife for the man of the house, as if he were somebody's uncle come home. The women thought he must be some kin they had forgotten. By the time the man came in from the brakes Mr. Stucky had eaten supper and was sitting on the gallery like he owned it.

That was how he came to know Isom Pace, who chopped cedar on the breaks above the Llano and burned what was left for charcoal. After supper Mr. Stucky asked him, the way he asked every man, whether he had seen anything up in the brakes worth looking at.

Nothing much, Isom said. Some big old cedars in a draw up there. The heart of them is red.

How many?

A good many.

Mr. Stucky went up the draw with him in the morning, and they cut posts out of it for three weeks and freighted them down to San Antonio by the wagonload, and when they parted Mr. Stucky shook his hand and said, Isom, you come see me in town. And don't bring me anything but that red-hearted cedar. That's the only present I want. So once or twice a year Isom drove a wagon of red cedar down to the yard, and Mr. Stucky paid him better than he had ever been paid for anything.

The fall Isom was thirty, old Brother Lyle Dobbs died. He had ridden the circuit in that country forty years, through the Indian times and after, and preached under brush arbors to people who had no church, and he was buried at Ransom, on the camp-meeting ground east of town where he had preached every August. The Methodists built an open tabernacle over the ground in his memory, a long roof on cedar posts. Mr. Stucky came up from San Antonio for the dedication with a sack of cedar shavings out of his own mill and walked the length of the grave strewing them, so that the whole ground smelled like the inside of a cedar chest. Isom brought something too. He had sawed a round off the biggest log in the draw, red to the bark, and planed it smooth and oiled it, the size of a dinner plate. He climbed up a ladder and nailed it in the peak of the gable over the tabernacle door. It looked like a moon coming up. People said so.

That was all Isom ever did that anybody remembered.

II. The Light

On the road · 1942 to 1956

His grandson Delmer was born in a cedar camp on the Pedernales in the spring of 1942, and he had a light in him. It came from his navel, round and pale as the moon, about the size of a saucer, and in the dark it shone through his shirt and lit the side of whatever he was next to. His mother said the first night she could have read the Bible by it. The old people in the camp said it was because of the cedar moon. Isom had been dead ten years by then, but they remembered the gable.

His uncle Arlis was a preacher by then, Pentecostal, with a tent. Arlis believed the light came from God. That was the first thing to understand about him. He believed it the way he believed the sun came up, and he also saw, being a man who had been poor all his life, what a light like that would do in a tent. When Delmer was nine his mother let him go on the road with his uncle for the summer, and after that he did not come home except at Christmas.

They had a used Army tent that held three hundred, a generator, two hundred folding chairs and a truckload of sawdust, and a man named Fentress who had sold cemetery lots in Fort Worth and who handled the money in a King Edward cigar box. Arlis preached for an hour on the woman who touched the hem of His garment. Then they turned off the lights. The boy stood up on the platform in the dark in a white shirt, and the light came through the cloth, and the people made a sound Delmer never forgot as long as he lived, a sound like the air going out of the tent all at once.

Then Fentress said what it cost. A dollar to lay your hand on the light. Five dollars for a sick child. Ten for a prayer cloth, a square cut from a bedsheet that had been pressed to the boy's belly overnight, to be sent through the mail to anybody who wrote. Whoever touched the light, Arlis said, the Lord would remember them, in their body and in their crops and in their herds.

By 1956 the healing tents were thicker on the roads than they had ever been. And the drought was in its seventh year. The tanks were dry and cracked into plates. Ranchers were burning the spines off the prickly pear at night with kerosene burners so the cattle could eat it, and you could see the little flames moving on the hills for miles, and the cattle following the flames. Men who had lost the place, or were about to, stood in line in the sawdust with a dollar in their hand and laid their palm flat on a fourteen-year-old boy's stomach, and some of them cried. Fentress's cigar box was full most nights. The boy stood still and let them. He had been doing it since he was nine. He thought it was what he was for.

III. Ransom

The camp-meeting ground · August 1956

In the middle of August they came to Ransom, the county seat, on the river, and pitched the tent on the old camp-meeting ground east of town, between the square and the cemetery, next to the tabernacle with the gray wooden moon in its gable. Nobody had held a meeting under the tabernacle since the war.

On the Saturday morning, early, the whole town came down the road past the tent, walking and in pickups, in their good clothes, with flowers in coffee cans and cakes under dish towels and casseroles wrapped in newspaper to keep them hot. Arlis stood at the front of the tent and asked them where they were going.

To Mr. Hext's, a woman said. For Mrs. Tolliver. Mrs. Tolliver had taught the third grade in Ransom for forty-one years and had died on Thursday, and she was laid out at Hext Furniture and Funeral, up the hill from the tabernacle, and the visitation was all day.

Come in here first, Arlis said. There is a boy in this tent with a light in his body that God put there. Whoever touches it, the Lord will remember. You never saw anything like it.

A rancher named Corley Wade, who had sold the last of his cows in July, laughed, not unkindly.

Preacher, Mr. Hext has laid out every soul in this county for forty-seven years. There isn't one thing your boy can show him he hasn't seen.

They argued in the road. Arlis said a light from God was a light from God. The Ransom people said they had nothing against the boy and nothing against the Lord, but they were going to Mr. Hext's. Neither side gave an inch, and the cakes were getting warm. At last Fentress said, Well, then let's all of us go up the hill and see, and Arlis took Delmer by the arm.

IV. The Charm

Hext Furniture and Funeral · August to October

Wiley Hext was seventy-four. He had come to Ransom in 1909 to sell furniture, and in those days the furniture man also sold the coffins, and somebody had to lay the dead out in them, so he had learned that too. He ran the only ambulance in the county, a black Cadillac combination car that was a hearse by day and turned into an ambulance when the phone rang at night, with a cot in the back and a red light he clamped to the roof. He was a Church of Christ man, and a widower, and he did not talk much. When they came up the hill he was in the front parlor in his shirtsleeves, combing Mrs. Tolliver's white hair with a pocket comb and laying each curl where she had worn it.

Arlis brought the boy in at the door, and the light went out.

It went out between one step and the next. In the dim parlor, among the sprays of gladiolas, the boy stood there in his white shirt, and there was nothing in it but a boy. Somebody said afterward that he looked like a crow in a coal scuttle. Arlis took him back out on the porch, and in the shade of the porch the light came up again in his shirt, round and pale, plain as ever. They brought him back in to Mr. Hext, and it went out. The third time, Delmer went in by himself.

He stood by the casket in the dark of his own shirt and looked at the old man with the comb.

You know some way to put it out, he said.

I know one, Mr. Hext said.

Show me.

Mr. Hext laid another curl.

I can't show it to anybody that doesn't work here.

Out in the yard, by the tent truck, the boy told his uncle. Delmer had thought it through on the porch. A man who could put out a light like that could put out any light, any preacher's in Texas, and a boy who could put his own light out and bring it back would be the biggest thing on the sawdust trail. You go on to Brady and Coleman, he said. Leave me here. I'll learn it in a few weeks and catch up with you, and then nobody in Texas can touch us. Arlis prayed over it and let him. Fentress looked at the boy a long time and said nothing.

•••

He slept on a cot in the back of the furniture store, between the bedroom suites and the stacked kitchen chairs. He swept. He washed the Cadillac. He rode in it at night with Mr. Hext on the ambulance calls, to car wrecks on the San Saba highway, to ranch houses forty miles out where somebody's heart had stopped in the kitchen, and on those roads he saw the pear burners going on the hills in the dark, the little blue-and-yellow flames and the cattle crowding after them.

And he learned to wash the dead. Mr. Hext did it the old way, with a basin and a cloth, and he named the parts of the body aloud as he washed them, in a low plain voice, the way a man counts posts.

Hair of the head. Hair of the body. Nails. Teeth. Skin.

Delmer asked him the first night what that was.

That's what you say first, Mr. Hext said, before you can learn the other. And he went on. Flesh. Sinews. Bones. Marrow. Kidneys. Heart. Then he made the boy say it, every part, every body, over and over, the old rancher from Katemcy and the baby from the Mexican side of town across the river and the drowned man they brought up out of the Llano at Kingsland, and when the boy had all of it by heart Mr. Hext gave him a chapter of the Bible to learn as well, from the back of a funeral card he kept in his shirt pocket, and had him say that over the washing too.

While the sun, or the light, or the moon, or the stars, be not darkened, nor the clouds return after the rain: In the day when the keepers of the house shall tremble, and the strong men shall bow themselves, and the grinders cease because they are few, and those that look out of the windows be darkened,

— Ecclesiastes 12:2–3

The boy said it all, and washed, and said it again. The keepers of the house were the hands, Mr. Hext told him, and the strong men were the legs, and the grinders were the teeth, and the windows were the eyes. The boy looked at his own hands in the basin. In the evenings, after supper at the café, he sat on the loading dock behind the store with the light off in him and looked at the hills.

Arlis came back through Ransom at the end of September, with the tent folded on the truck, on his way to a meeting at Menard.

You learned it yet?

Not yet, Delmer said. I'm learning it.

V. The Bench

The courthouse square · November 1956

Arlis came again in November, and this time Fentress was driving. The drought had not broken, and the meetings had been thin all fall; people who had sold their cattle had no dollars left to put in a cigar box. They found the boy on the courthouse square in a pair of Mr. Hext's old khakis with the cuffs turned up, waiting with the Cadillac outside the drugstore while the old man got his prescription.

Well? Arlis said. Have you got it?

Delmer looked at his uncle, and at Fentress behind the wheel, and at the tent folded in the truck bed with the generator.

Y'all go on, he said. I'm not coming. I'm not ever going back out there.

Arlis argued with him and then prayed with him right there on the sidewalk, with his hand on the boy's head, and the boy let him. Then the truck went off down the San Saba road. Fentress lifted two fingers off the wheel as they passed the bench.

The old men on the bench under the pecan by the courthouse door had heard every word. They played dominoes there every morning there was no rain, which that year was every morning, and they knew the boy, the way they knew everybody who had come to town since 1910. When Mr. Hext came out of the drugstore one of them, Corley Wade's daddy, called out to him.

Wiley. Your boy just told his people he's done with the tent for good. Not ever, he said.

I heard him, Mr. Hext said.

Well, you know that's a lie. That's a fourteen-year-old boy. He'll be back in that tent by Christmas, first time he wants a dollar.

Mr. Hext put the little paper sack in his coat pocket and stood a minute on the curb, looking at the boy by the car. He did not argue with men on benches as a rule.

He's telling the truth, he said. His eye is single. And when they looked at him he said the rest of it, the way he said the parts of the body over a basin, plain, so they would have it right.

The light of the body is the eye: if therefore thine eye be single, thy whole body shall be full of light.

— Matthew 6:22

Then he got into the Cadillac, and the boy drove him home, because the old man's eyes were going at night by then.

•••

He ran Hext Funeral Home for thirty-eight years after the old man died. He never preached and he never went back on the road. People in Ransom said he had the gentlest hands in the county, and that he could come into a house where somebody had just died and the whole house would go quiet, the way a dog does when you put your hand on it.

Nobody ever saw the light again. The cedar moon is still in the gable of the tabernacle on the camp-meeting ground, gone silver, and the nail heads around its edge have rusted into it. On clear nights, when the real moon comes up over the cemetery hill behind it, the two of them look about the same size, and about the same color, and for a minute you can't say which one is giving the light.

Background

The verse

Him I call indeed a Brahmana who is bright like the moon, pure, serene, undisturbed, and in whom all gaiety is extinct.Dhammapada 413, tr. F. Max Müller (1881)

In the commentary, a frontier forester shows a merchant a stand of red sandalwood, and when a shrine is raised over the relics of the earlier Buddha Kassapa he sets a moon-shaped disk of sandalwood inside it. Reborn in a rich Brahmin family at Rājagaha, he has a light like the moon shining from his navel and is named Candābha, “Moonlight.” Brahmins carry him from town to town in a carriage and charge people a hundred or a thousand coins to stroke his body for luck. At Sāvatthī they quarrel with the Buddha's lay followers and bring the boy to the monastery to see whose power is greater, and in the Buddha's presence the light goes out three times; the boy, “like a crow in a basket of charcoal,” asks for the charm that does it, is told it can only be taught to one who has gone forth, ordains, and is given the meditation on the thirty-two parts of the body as “a preliminary.” He becomes an arahant within days and tells the Brahmins to go, since he will never return. The monks think he is lying; the Buddha says he speaks the truth. Read the full commentary story, and about the contemplation of the body's parts.

The Hill Country, 1956

The 1950s Texas drought ran from about 1949 to 1957, and by its end 244 of the state's 254 counties had been declared federal drought disaster areas. Ranchers fed prickly pear to their cattle, burning off the spines with kerosene pear burners. In the hills west of Austin, cedar choppers, Scotch-Irish families who had come by way of Appalachia and the Ozarks, cut Ashe juniper for fence posts and burned it for charcoal; their best years were the 1940s and 1950s, before chainsaws and steel posts put most of them out of work around 1960.

The postwar healing revival began in 1946 and peaked in 1956, when 49 evangelists were holding major campaigns. In small towns the undertaker often ran the only ambulance, in a combination car that served as hearse or ambulance as needed, a practice that lasted in the United States until the late 1970s. The drought broke in the spring of 1957, with rains that brought deadly floods.
