The Road Book

Indeed, the whole science and practice of the bicycle is “in your eye” and in your will; the rest is mere manipulation.
— Frances E. Willard, A Wheel Within a Wheel (1895)

The way it was told at the club, on Davis Street.

I. Five Hundred

Evanston, Illinois · summer 1895

That was the summer everybody in Evanston got on a wheel. The professors at the University rode, and the students, and the young ministers from the Biblical Institute rode in their black coats with their trousers clipped at the ankle. The Methodist ladies rode, in divided skirts their mothers would not have let in the house. Men who went in to the city on the Northwestern every morning came home at six and changed and were out again by seven, ringing their bells along Chicago Avenue under the elms, and the whole long town by the lake smelled of rubber cement and machine oil and cut grass, and every porch had a bicycle leaning on it, and every stable that had lost its horse had a bicycle in the stall.

Most of them had learned from Madame.

She had taken the old roller rink on Sherman Avenue in the spring of 1893 and painted Riding Academy over the door, and she kept a ledger of her pupils, name and date and the number of lessons it had taken, in a column as straight as a fence. By the end of June 1895 the ledger had five hundred names in it. Her own name was Odile Lacasse. She came from a parish on the river below Montreal, and in the eighties, when the wheels were high and only acrobats and young men rode them, she had raced for money, women against women and sometimes against men and once against a trotting horse, in halls in Chicago and Minneapolis and Cincinnati. She had ridden seven hundred and some miles in a single week on a board track in a hall on the lake front, round and round, from noon Monday to midnight Saturday, with a brass band playing and the crowd going home and coming back, and had never once gone out the door. She was near forty now. A fall in Cincinnati had left her with a hip that ached in wet weather, and she walked with a little hitch, but on a wheel there was nothing wrong with her at all.

Her pupils had made themselves into a club. They called it the Davis Street Wheel Club because they met in the evenings at the fountain at the foot of Davis Street, and by July there were forty of them, and on Saturdays they rode out together, north along the lake or west to the Ridge, in a long line with the captain in front, and in the evenings they sat on the rim of the fountain with their wheels leaning against it and talked about the roads.

The captain was Elmer Tuthill, who wrote fire insurance in the Rookery building and kept the club’s road book. It was the League’s book for the state, bound in red cloth, and he had written in the margins of it in pencil until there was hardly any margin left. Sheridan Road was macadam to the Ravines and then it was not. Ridge Avenue was good in dry weather and gumbo in wet. The road out past Gross Point was sand from the beach, and you walked it. The way to Niles Center was pebbles, and the way to Glenview had no pebbles, but it had a hill. The plank road to Wheeling had rotted, and there was a farm dog at the second bridge. They argued over it the way their grandfathers had argued over the meaning of baptism, and sometimes Tuthill would say a road was bad and Dr. Pruyn the dentist would say it was fair, and it would go on until the lamplighter came.

Madame was not there for any of this. At the end of June she had taken her wheel and a valise strapped over the back of it and gone up into Wisconsin to teach the summer people at the lake resorts, and she was gone all of July and August.

II. The Fountain

Davis Street · September 1895

She came home on the first Monday in September, riding in from the north at sundown, brown as a farmhand, with the dust of four counties on her shoes, and she got off at the fountain because there was a crowd at it and she knew every face in the crowd.

They were talking about the road to Lake Forest. Tuthill had the book open on his knee. Mrs. Bascom, who was the club’s secretary and the head of the Temperance Union in the Second Ward, had come back from it that afternoon and said it was a disgrace, that the stretch through Highland Park was nothing but pebbles the size of hens’ eggs, and that a lady could not enjoy herself for one minute on it, and Tuthill had written pebbles, H. P. in the margin and underlined it, and Dr. Pruyn was saying that he had been over it in July and it was not as bad as all that.

Madame stood holding her wheel and listened to them for a while. Nobody had seen her yet. Then somebody did, and they all got up and shook her hand, and somebody gave her the place on the rim of the fountain, and she sat down.

What were you all talking about, she said, when I came?

They told her. The road to Lake Forest. The pebbles in Highland Park. The sand at Gross Point, the farm dog at the bridge.

All summer?

Tuthill laughed and held up the road book, and the pencil in the margins was so thick it shone.

Mrs. Bascom said that Miss Willard herself agreed about pebbles. She had the new book in her bag, Miss Willard’s book about learning the wheel, which had come out that spring and which half of Evanston had read because Miss Willard was an Evanston woman, and she found the place and read it out to settle the matter.

Another thing I found is that we carry in the mind a picture of the road; and if it is humpy by reason of pebbles, even if we steer clear of them, we can by no means skim along as happily as when its smoothness facilitates the pleasing impression on the retina; indeed, the whole science and practice of the bicycle is “in your eye” and in your will; the rest is mere manipulation.

— Frances E. Willard, A Wheel Within a Wheel (1895)

There, said Mrs. Bascom. Pebbles.

Madame held out her hand for the book and read the passage again to herself, moving her lips, because she read English slowly. Then she gave it back.

Read the end again.

Mrs. Bascom read it again. In your eye and in your will. The rest is mere manipulation.

Bon, said Madame. She has it. She looked around at all of them on the rim of the fountain, forty people she had held up by the saddle on the floor of a roller rink, one at a time, until they could go. I rode seven hundred miles in a week once in a hall on the lake front. There was no road. There were boards, and the same corner every half a minute, and a man with a cornet. And I went seven hundred miles. She tapped the side of her eye with one finger. The road is in here. All summer you are carrying the pebbles in here. In Highland Park they are only on the road, they are only pebbles. In here they are with you the whole way home.

Nobody said anything. Tuthill closed the road book on his finger.

Saturday, said Madame, I will ride with you. We go to Gross Point. The sand.

III. The Nail

North of Evanston · the next Saturday

Twenty-six of them went. They rode out in a line in the cool of the morning, Madame at the head where Tuthill usually rode, and Tuthill behind her, with the road book in his coat. North of town the macadam gave out and the road turned to sand, deep and yellow and loose, the kind of road the book said you walked, and at the edge of it the line slowed and bunched up and people began putting a foot down.

Madame did not stop. She called back over her shoulder, not loudly, Look up. Look at the steeple. Not at the road. The steeple, and they looked up at the spire of the church at Gross Point a mile off across the fields, and pushed. The wheels squirmed and wandered and somehow went on, with the sand hissing under the tires. Mrs. Bascom said afterward that it was like riding through sugar, and that she had not once thought about the sand until she was on the far side of it.

On the far side Tuthill’s back tire went down with a sigh.

They all got off and stood around it. Madame laid the wheel on its side in the grass and ran her thumb around the tire until she found it, and pulled it out and held it up for them to see: a horseshoe nail, bent, black, an inch and a half long. Every farm road in Cook County was sown with them.

That, she said, is what hurts. Not the sand. Something small that gets in and stays in. She dropped the nail in Tuthill’s hand. Then she took the little tin of cement and the patches out of her own tool bag and held them out to him too.

Tuthill looked at the tin. Madame, you could do that in two minutes.

Yes, she said. You do it.

It took him twenty minutes, with the whole club giving advice, and his cuffs were black to the wrist when he was done, and the patch held all the way home.

IV. Let Go

Hinman Avenue · September 1895

Mrs. Abigail Crane was sixty-six years old, the widow of a professor at the Biblical Institute, and she was the only member of the Davis Street Wheel Club who could not ride. She had joined in June because her daughters were in it. All summer one of the men from the club had come by her house in the evenings to run beside her on the sidewalk with a hand on the back of her saddle, and she would pedal very straight to the corner, talking the whole time, and at the corner she would say, You have me, Mr. Tuthill? and Mr. Tuthill would say, I have you, Mrs. Crane, and the moment he let go she would feel it, and stop, and put her foot down.

On the last evening of September Madame came instead.

She held the saddle and ran, with her hitch, and Mrs. Crane pedaled and talked. She talked about her late husband, who had been against bicycles on principle. She talked about her daughters, and about Miss Willard, whom she had known when Miss Willard was a girl, and about the book, which she had read twice, and about the line in it that everybody in Evanston was saying that fall.

“Let go, but stand by”—this is the golden rule for parent and pastor, teacher and friend; the only rule that at once respects the individuality of another and yet adds one’s own, so far as may be, to another’s momentum in the struggle of life.

— Frances E. Willard, A Wheel Within a Wheel (1895)

Look up, said Madame. At the end of the street. Where the lamps are.

Mrs. Crane looked up the street. The lamps were just being lit, one after another, far up Hinman Avenue under the elms.

You have me? she said.

I am here, said Madame, and let go, and stopped running, and stood in the road.

Mrs. Crane went on. She went on past the corner, still talking, and past the next house and the next, pedaling very straight, with her eyes on the lamps, and her voice came back fainter and fainter down the avenue, telling Madame something about Miss Willard as a girl that nobody else would ever hear.

That was the last week the club talked about roads. In October the rains came, and somebody found the road book left on the rim of the fountain at the foot of Davis Street, swollen with water, its red cloth gone pink, the pencil in its margins running grey into the pages, and carried it in to Tuthill at the Rookery, and he thanked them, and put it in a drawer, and did not take it out again.

The verses

The best of ways is the eightfold; the best of truths the four words; the best of virtues passionlessness; the best of men he who has eyes to see.Dhammapada 273, tr. F. Max Müller (1881)
This is the way, there is no other that leads to the purifying of intelligence. Go on this way! Everything else is the deceit of Mara (the tempter).Dhammapada 274, tr. F. Max Müller (1881)
If you go on this way, you will make an end of pain! The way was preached by me, when I had understood the removal of the thorns (in the flesh).Dhammapada 275, tr. F. Max Müller (1881)
You yourself must make an effort. The Tathagatas (Buddhas) are only preachers. The thoughtful who enter the way are freed from the bondage of Mara.Dhammapada 276, tr. F. Max Müller (1881)

In the commentary, the Buddha comes back to Sāvatthī from a journey through the country and finds five hundred monks in the assembly hall talking about the roads they have walked: the road to this village is smooth, to that one rough, this one is covered with pebbles, that one has none. He asks what they are discussing, tells them that such roads are no concern of a monk, who should give himself to the Noble Path, and speaks these verses on the Noble Eightfold Path, which opens the Path chapter of the Dhammapada. At the end of the teaching all five hundred become arahants. Read the full commentary story.

Evanston, 1895

The “safety” bicycle, with two wheels of equal size and pneumatic tires, set off a bicycle boom in the 1890s that put millions of Americans, women among them, on wheels; the League of American Wheelmen, which reached 103,000 members in 1898, led the Good Roads movement, and its state divisions published road books for cyclists. Before the safety, racing was done on high-wheelers, and a few women raced professionally in multi-day indoor contests; the Canadian Louise Armaindo held a record of 843 miles in one such race in 1886. Evanston, on the lake just north of Chicago, was founded by Methodists, dry by law from 1858, and grew as a railroad commuter suburb around Northwestern University and the Garrett Biblical Institute, and the lakeshore Sheridan Road, named in 1889, was being laid out through the North Shore in those years.

Evanston was also the home of Frances E. Willard, president of the Woman’s Christian Temperance Union, who at fifty-three learned to ride a bicycle she named Gladys and described it in A Wheel Within a Wheel: How I Learned to Ride the Bicycle (Fleming H. Revell, 1895). She took lessons from a young Evanston schoolteacher and in a riding gallery in Chicago, and learned the rest in England with trainers who held the machine until she could tell them, “Let go, but stand by.” Madame Lacasse, Mrs. Crane and the Davis Street Wheel Club are fictional.
