New Clothes

When he is strong and the physical powers are full of vigor, he guards against quarrelsomeness.
— Analects 16.7

Wǒ shì zhèyàng tīngshuō de, in the parking lot of the 99 Ranch on Nogales Street.

I. The House on the Hill

Rowland Heights, California · 1994–1996

Lin Lǎoshī had learned three languages in one life and been allowed to teach in none of them that she dreamed in. She was born in Tainan in 1925, when the island belonged to Japan, and in the years when the Japanese wanted everyone on the island to become Japanese she learned to think in Taiwanese and answer in Japanese. After the war the Japanese left and the Mainlanders came, and she learned Mandarin at twenty from a radio, sitting on the floor of her father’s pharmacy with a dictionary, and became good enough at it to teach the old literature, the classical texts, at a girls’ high school in Tainan for forty years. In those years the girls were fined if they spoke Taiwanese at school. She collected the fines. She had never once in her life spoken to a classroom in the language she dreamed in, and she did not think about it much, the way you do not think about a tooth you lost a long time ago, except with your tongue.

In the summer of 1994 her niece called from Taipei. The niece’s husband made computer monitors, and they were rich now in the way people in Taiwan had become rich in those years, suddenly and a little afraid of it. Two years before, they had sent their sons to California. The older one, Kai-wen, had been sixteen. The younger one, Kai-li, had been twelve. They had gone partly for the schools and partly for the English and partly, though nobody said it on the telephone, because a boy who left before a certain age would not have to do his two years in the army, and because nobody in Taipei knew anymore what the Mainland was going to do. The boys lived in a house in the hills above Rowland Heights with a cousin, Peter, and a Mexican woman who came three days a week to clean, and a checking account. Their father flew over on China Airlines one weekend a month. Their mother flew over when she could. People had a word for this in Chinese that meant astronauts.

Kai-wen is not studying, the niece said on the telephone. He has a car. Please, Auntie. Just to cook. Just to be in the house.

So at sixty-nine Lin Lǎoshī flew to Los Angeles with two suitcases and a rice cooker, and the boys called her Lǎoshī, Teacher, because that was what everyone had called her for forty years, and because they did not know what else to call a great-aunt they had met twice.

The house was white stucco with a red tile roof, at the top of a street that curled up into the Puente Hills, and from the back patio you could see the whole valley go down to the freeway and the smog, and on a clear day the yellow roofs of the big Buddhist temple over in Hacienda Heights. Below the patio, on its own terrace, was a swimming pool nobody swam in. The Mexican woman’s son came on Thursdays to clean it. Lin Lǎoshī would stand at the kitchen window in the mornings and watch the light move on the bottom of it, the net of light that water makes, shaking and joining and coming apart, and it calmed her in a way she could not have explained, and did not try to.

She did not drive. Every morning she walked down the hill to the 99 Ranch Market at Gale and Nogales with a folding cart, a mile and a half down and a mile and a half back up, in a cardigan and a cotton sun hat, and every morning one of the younger boys walked with her, because their mother had said so on the telephone, and after a while because they wanted to. The neighborhood had a name in those years. People called it Little Taipei. In the plaza around the market there were bakeries and a bookstore and a travel agency and three restaurants where you could get oyster omelets and braised pork over rice the way they made it in the south, and the auntie who ran one of them was from Tainan and would not take Lin Lǎoshī’s money. Lin Lǎoshī paid her anyway, by bringing her poems. She copied one out by hand every week, on good paper, and the auntie taped them up on the wall by the register, where the customers read them while they waited and did not know what to make of them.

Kai-wen was eighteen now. He had a black BMW with a license plate that said a word in English she did not understand, and five friends, sons of a legislator and a plastics man and a man who owned half a shipping company, and when the six of them stood in the driveway together in their sunglasses she thought of the boys she had seen in the old paintings, the young lords of the Tang riding out to hunt. In Taipei the newspapers had a word for boys like them. They called them the princes.

II. The Eve

The house on the hill · February 18, 1996

On New Year’s Eve the parents did not come. They telephoned instead, from Taipei, one after another, at seven in the evening California time, which was eleven in the morning of New Year’s Day there already, so that the boys were talking to a day that had already happened. Kai-wen’s father said the trip was impossible this year, with the factory. He did not say the other thing. Everyone knew the other thing. Since the summer, the Mainland had been firing missiles into the sea off the island, testing, it said, and everyone in Taipei with money had a son somewhere, or a house somewhere, or a passport somewhere, and the flights to Los Angeles and Vancouver were full.

Stay where you are, the father said. Study. Listen to Auntie.

Lin Lǎoshī made the reunion dinner anyway, for three boys and herself. She made it the way her mother had made it: a whole fish, because the word for fish sounds like the word for more than enough, and you leave some of it uneaten so there will be more than enough next year too; long-stemmed mustard greens, uncut, for long life; a hot pot on the table, because the family sits around the fire. The boys ate a great deal and said very little. At the end of the meal she gave each of them a red envelope with a new twenty-dollar bill in it, which was what she could do, and the two younger ones bowed to her the old way, which nobody had taught them in America, and Kai-wen hugged her, which nobody did in Tainan.

Kai-wen’s red envelope from his father had come by Federal Express that afternoon. She did not see what was in it. She saw what he bought with it. He came home at eleven from a store in Beverly Hills with a black silk shirt printed all over in gold, gold scrolls and gold leaves and gold heads of Greek women, and a pair of trousers and a pair of shoes that cost more than she had earned in a month in 1975. It was the custom, new clothes for the New Year. He laid it all out on his bed under the plastic like a body laid out for viewing, and she stood in his doorway and looked at it.

Tomorrow, he said, we are going to Jason’s. Jason was the legislator’s son. His parents had rented a house for the winter on a ridge in Hacienda Heights, with a pool that went right to the edge of the hill so that the water looked as if it ran into the sky, and his parents were in Taipei.

All of you?

All six. We’re going to swim. We’re going to eat. Don’t worry, Lǎoshī.

She did not say anything. She had taught girls for forty years and boys for a year and a half, and in her experience, when a young person told you not to worry, it was the only true information in the sentence.

III. The Gods

The driveway · New Year’s morning

The first day of the Year of the Rat fell that year on the same Monday as the American holiday for the presidents, so there was no school, and the whole hill seemed to be awake early. Firecrackers went off down in the valley. Somebody’s grandfather on the next street was playing a cassette of the New Year songs too loud.

At nine o’clock the princes came for Kai-wen. They came up the hill one after another, a red sports car that sat low as a cat, a white car with a spoiler, a silver one, a black one like Kai-wen’s, and parked in a crescent in front of the house with their engines running. They got out. They were dressed, every one of them, in new clothes from head to foot, as the custom was, and the custom had never seen anything like it. Silk shirts in black and red and gold. Linen jackets the color of milk. A long coat on one boy, in February, in Southern California, because it was beautiful. Gold watches. Gold chains. Their hair cut the day before and shining. Kai-wen came out of the house in the black and gold shirt and they cheered him, and he spread his arms and turned around once on the step, laughing, so the gold caught the sun.

Lin Lǎoshī came out behind him with the folding cart, and Kai-li and Peter behind her, in their school sweatshirts. They had to wait at the top of the walk for the princes to finish admiring one another before they could get by.

She stood and looked at them for a long time. The boys beside her were embarrassed by her looking, because it was their brother, and because she looked at the princes with an attention she ordinarily gave only to a page.

Kai-li, she said. Peter. You were born too late, and in the wrong place, and you have never seen the gods come out of the temple in Tainan on Mazu’s birthday. The generals with their painted faces. The palanquins with the gold. The whole city in the street. You have never seen it.

No, Lǎoshī.

Then look at them, she said. There they go. That is what it looks like.

The princes got into their cars and went down the hill in a line, slowly at first, then not slowly, with their windows down and their music on, and turned onto Colima Road toward Hacienda Heights, and the sound of them went on in the valley for a long time after they could not be seen. Lin Lǎoshī tipped her sun hat against the light and started down the hill with the cart, and the two boys walked with her, one on each side, and nobody said anything for a while.

IV. Wendy

Colima Road, and the house on the ridge · the same day

Her name was Chen Hsiao-ling and at the Golden Bell KTV in the strip mall on Colima Road they called her Wendy, which she had chosen herself out of a book of English names, because it was a name from a children’s story about a girl who flew away. She was twenty-two. She came from Chiayi, where her mother sold betel nut from a glass booth by the highway. She had come over on a student visa to study English at a college in Pomona and studied, mornings, and worked, nights, singing with men in the private rooms of the Golden Bell, sitting beside them on the leather couches and pouring their cognac and laughing when they sang badly, which was always. The men tipped in cash. She sent eight hundred dollars a month to Chiayi. She had never once been out of the valley except to the airport.

She was sitting outside the bakery in the plaza on Colima Road on New Year’s morning with a coffee and a pineapple cake, in the red dress she had bought for the New Year, not for work, for herself, when the five cars came into the parking lot, and Jason Hsu got out of the red one, whom she knew from the Golden Bell. He tipped well and sang worst of anybody.

Wendy. Happy New Year. Come swim.

She said she had no swimsuit. He said there were swimsuits at the house, there were twenty swimsuits, his mother left them. She said she had to be at work at eight. He said he would pay her what she made at work. She looked at the six of them in their clothes, laughing in the sun with their sunglasses pushed up in their hair, and she thought they looked like the boys on the posters in the windows of the music stores, and she thought of her mother’s glass booth on the highway outside Chiayi, and she thought it was New Year and nobody had invited her anywhere. She got in the red car.

The house on the ridge was all glass on the side that faced the valley. The pool ran to the edge of the hill and seemed to pour off it into the air, and below it, a long way off, were the yellow roofs of the temple. There was food from a restaurant in Monterey Park in silver trays, and bottles of cognac, and a stereo in the living room that played into the yard. They swam. She swam. They took pictures of each other with a camera you threw away after. For an hour it was the best day she had had in America.

It went wrong the way those days go wrong, a little at a time and then all at once. Jason wanted her beside him. Eric Wang, the plastics man’s son, who had said almost nothing all morning, wanted her beside him, and said so, and Jason laughed at him for saying so. Then there was the business of who had brought her, and who was paying her, and whether paying her meant what Jason seemed to think it meant, which she said out loud it did not, and Jason laughed at that too. Then there was the cognac. Then there was a thing Eric said about Jason’s father, the legislator, and what everybody in Taipei knew about how he had gotten his money, and a thing Jason said about Eric’s mother, and then the bottle.

She saw it the way you see an accident from the sidewalk, every piece of it very clear and none of it in order. Jason swinging the cognac bottle by the neck. The bottle breaking on the edge of the pool. Eric with a knife, the kind that opens with a flip of the wrist, that half the boys in the valley carried that year because the other half did. Daniel Tsai going in on Eric’s side, and Victor Ho on Jason’s, for no reason that anyone could ever afterward explain, except that they were standing where they were standing. Kai-wen in his black and gold shirt in the middle of it, with his hands out, saying stop it, stop it, and the knife going across his palm. Victor going down on the concrete with his head. Jason sitting down suddenly at the edge of the pool with a surprised face and his hand pressed high on the inside of his thigh, and then sliding into the water.

The water went pink. It went pink from where he was and spread, the way ink goes into a glass, and then it went darker than pink, and the net of light on the bottom of the pool shook through it, red now, and the water went on pouring over the edge of the hill into the air, and it was red too, running down the outside of the wall into the ivy.

Somebody pulled Jason out. Somebody was screaming in the house into a telephone. Wendy stood on the patio in a borrowed swimsuit with her red dress in her hand and watched it all, very clearly, out of order, and then she put her dress on over the wet suit, and found her shoes, and walked out the front door and down the long driveway, and down the ridge road in the sun, toward the valley, as the first sirens came up the hill to meet her.

V. The Litters

Hacienda Heights · the afternoon

Lin Lǎoshī and the boys had eaten their New Year lunch at the auntie’s restaurant in the plaza: turnip cake, and noodles for long life, and the sweet rice cake that sticks, for the year to be higher than the last one. The auntie would not take any money because it was New Year, and Lin Lǎoshī took a poem out of her purse that she had copied the night before, a short one by Du Fu about spring, and the auntie taped it up by the register. Then, because the day was warm and the boys were full and restless, she let them talk her into walking home the long way, west along the foot of the hills toward the temple, so that they could look at its roofs, and then up.

They heard the sirens before they saw anything. Then a sheriff’s car went past them up the ridge road very fast, and an ambulance, and then a fire truck, which Peter said was what they sent with the paramedics, and then another ambulance. Kai-li stopped on the sidewalk.

That’s Jason’s street.

A young woman came down the hill toward them on the other side of the road, in a red dress with dark wet patches coming through it, walking very straight in high heels, carrying nothing. Her hair was wet. She looked at them as she passed and Lin Lǎoshī looked back at her, and saw her face, and crossed the road with her cart, which the boys had never seen her do against traffic, and stopped the young woman with a hand on her arm.

She said it in Taiwanese. The boys did not catch all of it. It was the New Year greeting, the one that wishes you peace, and then something else, low, that made the young woman put her hand over her mouth. Then Lin Lǎoshī took a red envelope out of her cardigan pocket, the one she had kept back from the night before for anybody who came to the door, and folded the young woman’s fingers around it, and let her go on down the hill.

They walked up the ridge road. There were people standing in the street by then, neighbors in house slippers, and the sheriff’s men had put a yellow tape across the long driveway. Lin Lǎoshī stood at the tape with the boys and watched them come down the driveway, one after another, on the narrow wheeled beds the paramedics push, with the rails up and men running alongside holding the bags of saline up in the air. Victor Ho, with his head wrapped and his eyes open. Daniel Tsai, sitting up, holding his own arm. Eric Wang, walking, in handcuffs, in a cream linen jacket that was not cream anymore. Kai-wen, on a bed, with his right hand wrapped in a towel that had been white, his face grey under the tan, the black and gold shirt torn open down the front, saying I’m fine, I’m fine to nobody. He saw his brother and looked away.

Jason Hsu came last. They were working on him on the bed as they pushed it, one man up on the side of it with both hands on his chest, and they did not stop at the ambulance, they lifted him in still working, and the doors shut, and it went down the ridge road with the siren going, and the people in the street stepped back onto the lawns to let it by.

Through the gate they could see the end of the pool where it ran off the edge of the hill. The water was the color of tea with milk in it, and of something darker, and it was still running over, steadily, into the air.

VI. Delight

The hospital in Whittier, and the kitchen · that night

Jason Hsu died at seven minutes after six in the evening, which was seven minutes after ten on the morning of the second day of the New Year in Taipei, while his mother was on the telephone to the hospital from the airport. Kai-wen had eleven stitches in his palm and two in his chin and would keep both scars all his life. Victor would not remember the day at all. Eric Wang was in the county jail, and his father was in the air.

They drove home from the hospital in Kai-wen’s black car, with Kai-li driving on his learner’s permit, which was not legal, and Lin Lǎoshī beside him in front, which was not legal either, because she had decided it, and Kai-wen in the back with his hand in his lap like something somebody had handed him. Nobody had told the parents in Taipei yet. That was going to be Lin Lǎoshī’s job, tonight.

In the kitchen she made rice porridge because it was what she knew how to make for people who could not eat. Kai-wen went to bed. Peter fell asleep at the table with his head on his arms. Kai-li sat across from her with the bowl in front of him, not eating, and after a long time he said it.

This morning, he said, you said look at them. You said there go the gods. I looked. They were. They really were, Lǎoshī. And now. He pushed the bowl away. And now Jason’s dead, and Eric’s in jail, and Kai-wen— All of it. All of it because of one girl.

Lin Lǎoshī got up from the table. She went into her room and came back with a book, an old bilingual Analects with the Chinese down one side of the page and the English of the nineteenth-century Scotsman down the other, which she had brought from Tainan to make the boys practice both languages at once and which they had never once opened. She sat down and turned the pages without looking for the place, the way you find a room in your own house in the dark.

Not because of the girl, she said. The girl walked home. You saw her. Read.

She put her finger on the Chinese, and he read the English across from it, out loud, slowly, because it was hard English, the English of a hundred years ago.

Confucius said, ‘There are three things which the superior man guards against. In youth, when the physical powers are not yet settled, he guards against lust. When he is strong and the physical powers are full of vigor, he guards against quarrelsomeness. When he is old, and the animal powers are decayed, he guards against covetousness.’

— Analects 16.7, tr. James Legge

It’s the same thing, she said. The wanting and the fighting. It’s one thing, seen from two sides. What you saw going down the hill this morning in the gold shirts was the wanting, and it was beautiful, I said so, I don’t take it back. What came down the driveway on the beds this afternoon was the same thing. It was the same thing all day. It just turned around.

Kai-li looked at the page for a long time. Then what are you supposed to do? Not want anything?

She turned some pages back, slowly, to another place, and put her finger on it, and he read that too.

The Master said, ‘While they have not got their aims, their anxiety is how to get them. When they have got them, their anxiety is lest they should lose them. When they are anxious lest such things should be lost, there is nothing to which they will not proceed.’

— Analects 17.15, tr. James Legge

Jason had her, she said. So he was afraid. Eric didn’t have her, so he was angry. Nobody in that house was afraid this morning, not of anything, and by lunch every one of them was afraid. That’s where it comes from. The fear comes in the same door as the delight. You don’t have to not want anything. Just watch the door.

She closed the book. The clock on the stove said a quarter to midnight.

A man who isn’t holding anything, she said, what is he going to be afraid of losing?

Then she got up and went to the telephone on the kitchen wall and dialed the long number to Taipei from memory, and stood with her back to the boy while it rang, and rang, in a house on the other side of the ocean where it was already tomorrow.

VII. The Shirt

The house on the hill · February and March, 1996

Jason Hsu went home to Taipei in the hold of a China Airlines plane, and his mother went with him. The legislator’s office said in the newspapers there that it was an accident in a swimming pool. Eric Wang’s father hired a lawyer from downtown who had been on television, and the parents of the boys who had been there flew in one after another and stayed at a hotel in the City of Industry and took their sons out to dinner and did not talk about it, and flew out again.

On the second morning Lin Lǎoshī took the black and gold shirt out of the bag the hospital had put it in. She filled the laundry sink with cold water, because blood sets in hot water, her mother had taught her that in the pharmacy in Tainan, where people came in bleeding all the time, and she put in a handful of salt and the shirt and left it. She changed the water four times. The blood came out a little at a time, brown, then rust, then pink, then nothing, and the gold scrolls came up again out of the black, and the gold Greek heads, each one, looking off to the side the way they had in the store. She hung it on the line at the side of the house where it would not be seen from the street. It was torn down the front where the paramedics had cut it, and it would never be worn again, and she washed it anyway, because it was a thing that had been made carefully by somebody, and she did not know what else to do with her hands.

In the second week of March the Mainland fired its missiles again, closer this time, into the sea off Keelung and off Kaohsiung, and the Americans sent their aircraft carriers, and the television in the living room showed the same maps all day with arrows on them. Kai-wen’s father telephoned every night. Stay where you are. Listen to Auntie. Kai-wen sat in front of the television with his hand still bandaged and did not go anywhere. He had not driven the black car since New Year. The princes did not come up the hill anymore. One evening he came into the kitchen while Lin Lǎoshī was washing rice and asked her, without looking at her, if she would show him the page.

She showed him. He read it in the English. Then he asked her to read it in the Chinese, and she did, and then, because it was evening and the boys were all in the kitchen and nobody was listening who could fine her for it, she said it over again the way she would have said it to her own mother, in Taiwanese, and the boys did not understand a word of it, and listened anyway.

On the twenty-third of March the island elected its own president for the first time in its history, and the missiles did not fall on anyone, and the carriers went away. That morning Lin Lǎoshī walked down the hill to the 99 Ranch with the folding cart, and Kai-wen walked beside her, in a grey sweatshirt with nothing printed on it. When they came back up she stood a while at the kitchen window, looking down at the pool nobody swam in, at the net of light on its bottom, joining and shaking and coming apart. The shirt was still on the line at the side of the house. The wind was out of the east that day, warm, off the desert, and the shirt filled with it and emptied and filled, and the gold caught the sun every time it turned.

Background

The verse

From lust comes grief, from lust comes fear; he who is free from lust knows neither grief nor fear.Dhammapada 214, tr. F. Max Müller (1881)

The commentary story is one of the shortest. On a festival day the Licchavi princes of Vesālī ride out to a pleasure garden in such finery that the Buddha, passing on his alms round, tells the monks that anyone who has never seen the gods of the Thirty-Three should look at them. On the way the princes take a courtesan along with them, fall to fighting over her, and “set flowing as it were a river of blood”; by afternoon they are being carried home on beds. When the monks remark on the change, the Buddha answers that grief and fear arise only from delight. Read the full commentary story, or about the Licchavis, an aristocratic republic that led the Vajjika League.

Little Taipei, 1996

In the 1980s and 1990s, the hillside suburbs of the eastern San Gabriel Valley drew so many wealthy immigrants from Taiwan that Rowland Heights was known as “Little Taipei,” anchored by the 99 Ranch Market that opened at Gale Avenue and Nogales Street in 1989. Next door in Hacienda Heights, the Fo Guang Shan order completed Hsi Lai Temple in 1988. Many families were “astronauts,” with a parent shuttling across the Pacific, and some sent children over alone as “parachute kids”; a 1990 UCLA study estimated there were 40,000 Taiwanese parachute kids in the United States. As the Taipei Times later reported, they lived in arrangements ranging from unsupervised to the care of a relative or paid guardian, and some sons were sent abroad partly to avoid military conscription.

Part of what drove parents was fear of the Mainland. During the Third Taiwan Strait Crisis, the People’s Liberation Army fired missiles into the waters near Taiwan in July 1995 and again in March 1996, when missiles splashed down off the ports of Keelung and Kaohsiung and the United States sent two carrier groups to the area. Flights from Taiwan to North America filled. On March 23, 1996, Lee Teng-hui won Taiwan’s first direct presidential election. Lin Lǎoshī’s schooling spans the island’s language politics: Japanese rule until 1945, then decades of Mandarin-only policy in schools, under which students who spoke Taiwanese were fined, punished or humiliated.
