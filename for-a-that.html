For A' That

The rank is but the guinea's stamp,
The Man's the gowd for a' that.
— Robert Burns

The way we tell it, in Parma.

WEST RIDGE PIPES & DRUMS

Annual Burns Supper

Sokol Hall, Ridge Road · Wednesday, January 25, 1978 · 6:30 p.m. · $9.50

Piping In · Selkirk Grace · Piping In of the Haggis · Address to a Haggis · The Immortal Memory · Toast to the Lassies · Reply · Auld Lang Syne

I. Piping In

Sokol Hall · 6:30 p.m.

There were fourteen of us that winter, if you count Murdo, and you had to count Murdo. Six pipers, four snares, two tenors, Danny Kovac on the bass, and Pipe Major Murdo MacAskill, who was seventy-one and had been born in a black house in Glendale on the Isle of Skye and had come to Cleveland by way of the Clydebank yards in 1949 and been a millwright at the GM plant on Chevrolet Boulevard until they gave him his pin. We practiced Tuesday nights in the basement of the Methodist church on Pearl Road with the Sunday-school chairs stacked against the wall. Most of us had never been to Scotland. Danny was Slovak. Lesia Hnatiuk was sixteen and Ukrainian Catholic, and practiced in her father's garage on Snow Road with the door up in all weathers because her mother would not have it in the house. The Szabo twins were Hungarian. Two of the snares were Methodists from Brook Park whose grandfather might have been Scotch, they said, or Welsh. We had learned the pipes from Murdo the way you learn anything from a man who does not talk much, by doing it wrong in front of him until he stopped wincing.

Murdo called three people in his life a piper. One was a man named Angus Lamont who had taught him in a tenement in Clydebank and was killed in the Blitz there in 1941. One was old Joe Szabo, the twins' grandfather, who had played at every funeral at the VA hospital for twenty years whether or not anybody had asked him. The third was Lesia. He had started calling her that in October, the first week the frost was on the garage floor, when he drove by on Snow Road at seven in the morning and heard her in there going over the same four bars of a strathspey with the door up and her breath showing. He came to practice that Tuesday and said, Piper, give us the tune, and it took her a second to understand he meant her, and then she went red to the hair and gave us the tune.

Don McCrimmon had been with us since May. He was forty-four and sold water softeners, the regional man for the whole west side, and knew the inside of more Parma basements than the gas company. He was a decent man and an awful piper. He had come to us the spring after Roots was on, when half the country was writing away for its grandparents. Don wrote to a genealogy society in Edinburgh and paid them sixty dollars and they wrote back that McCrimmon was MacCrimmon, and that the MacCrimmons had been the hereditary pipers to the chiefs of MacLeod of Dunvegan, on Skye, for generations, and had kept a school of piping at a place called Borreraig.

After that Don grew a beard. It came in red, which surprised everybody, Don included. Bev, his wife, ordered him a kilt in MacLeod tartan from a house in Edinburgh, because the MacCrimmons as far as she could find had no sett of their own, and a plaid to go over the shoulder, and a badge for the plaid with the MacLeod bull's head on it, and he wore the whole of it to practice, every Tuesday, in the church basement, under the Sunday-school pictures of Jesus with the lambs. He had a set of African blackwood pipes with silver mounts that cost more than Danny's car. He blew them like a man inflating a raft.

He never said anything about Lesia. But we saw him look at her when Murdo said Piper.

That night we piped the guests in out of a cold rain, the drones going and the rain coming down slantwise in the light over the Sokol hall door, eighty of them, Methodists and Slovaks and the Scottish society people from Lakewood in their good kilts, and Don's wife Bev in a tartan sash, and Lesia's father, who had come to see what all the noise in his garage had been for.

II. Piping In of the Haggis

7:40 p.m.

The haggis came from a butcher on Lorain Avenue who made it for the Scottish societies every January, and it was American, which meant there were no lungs in it. You couldn't have sheep lung in food in this country since 1971. Murdo said every year that an American haggis was a haggis in the way a wax apple was an apple, and every year he addressed it anyway. Danny carried it in on a silver tray behind Lesia, who piped it in, and Murdo stood up at the head table with a knife from the Sokol kitchen and gave it the whole address in a voice we had never heard him use, the voice, Bev said afterward, of a man in a pulpit in Skye. When he came to the cutting he cut, and it steamed, and everybody clapped. Then he sat down and ate a little of it, and said it was very good, and it was not.

Outside the rain had stopped sounding like rain.

III. The Immortal Memory

9:15 p.m.

Don gave the Immortal Memory. He had asked for it in November, and Murdo had said Fine, and Don had been working on it since Thanksgiving in his den on a yellow legal pad. It ran thirty-five minutes. It was about Burns, some of it. Mostly it was about Skye, which Don had never seen, and the MacCrimmons at Borreraig, and the clans, and the blood. Blood, he said, remembers. Some of us in this hall are here because we like the music. Some of us are here because we can't help it. Because the music's in us from three hundred years back. He looked at the head table when he said it. He finished with a verse from “Scots, Wha Hae,” and the Lakewood people stood up and cheered, and Danny's mother, who was from Bratislava, clapped politely, and Lesia looked at her plate.

Murdo stood and raised his glass to the Immortal Memory with the rest of us. He did not say anything about the speech. He looked, we thought, tired.

At a quarter to eleven somebody opened the side door to go out for a smoke and the door was pulled out of his hand.

IV. The Storm

Thursday, January 26 · after midnight

It came up out of the south and west, and by one o'clock the wind was over fifty, and some time before three the rain turned to snow, and the snow did not fall so much as go by. The windows of the Sokol hall went white and stayed white. You could not see the cars in the lot. You could not see the lot. The radio in the kitchen said to stay off the roads, and then that the Turnpike was closed, and then that the National Guard was being called out, and then the power went off for a long minute and came back with the lights a little browner than before. Eighty people at a Burns supper looked at each other in their kilts and good dresses and understood they would be sleeping on a gymnasium floor.

We pushed the tables to the walls. The Sokol ladies made coffee in the big urns. Somebody found the gym mats and somebody found the flags from the Slovak national days and people slept under the flags. Murdo sat on a folding chair by the coffee with his pipes across his knees and his eyes shut. He was not asleep. Murdo never slept that we knew of.

It was close to four o'clock when Don came and stood in front of him. He had been drinking a little, the toasts and then the bottle the Lakewood men had passed around after the power came back, but not so much that it did all the talking. He still had the plaid on, pinned with the bull's head. His beard was in a mat from lying on it.

Murdo, he said. Can I ask you something.

Murdo opened his eyes.

You call her a piper. He didn't point. He didn't have to. Lesia was asleep against the wall bars under a Slovak flag with her pipe case for a pillow. She's sixteen. She's been playing a year and a half. She's Ukrainian, Murdo. Her folks came over after the war. There isn't a drop of Scotch in her. His voice wasn't angry. That was the worst of it. It was the voice of a man who had been holding something in a long time. My people were pipers to MacLeod for three hundred years. On Skye. Your island. I've got it in a letter from Edinburgh. I've got the tartan, I've got the blood, I come every Tuesday. I'd just like to know what it is I don't have.

People were awake by then, around the urn and on the mats. We were all awake. Bev was sitting up with her coat around her shoulders.

Murdo looked at him for a long time. Then he said, quietly, so that we had to lean to hear him over the wind:

Donald. I don't call anybody a piper for his kilt. Or his name. Or whose grandson he is. My own grandfather crofted two miles from Borreraig and he couldn't play a note, and he was the best man in Glendale. He was quiet a moment. You did the Immortal Memory and you left out the best one.

And he said it to him sitting down, in the brown light, not in the pulpit voice. In his own.

Is there for honest Poverty
That hings his head, an' a' that;
The coward slave—we pass him by,
We dare be poor for a' that!
For a' that, an' a' that.
Our toils obscure an' a' that,
The rank is but the guinea's stamp,
The Man's the gowd for a' that.

A prince can mak a belted knight,
A marquis, duke, an' a' that;
But an honest man's abon his might,
Gude faith, he maunna fa' that!
For a' that, an' a' that,
Their dignities an' a' that;
The pith o' sense, an' pride o' worth,
Are higher rank than a' that.

— Robert Burns, “A Man's a Man for A' That” (1795)

The guinea's stamp, Murdo said, is the king's head they put on the coin. It's not the gold. That girl was in a garage at seven in the morning in October with the door up, playing the same four bars until they were right. Nobody was listening but the cat. That's the gold.

Don did not say anything. He stood there in his plaid. The wind hit the building on the lake side so hard that the flags on the wall bars lifted.

We have argued since, the fourteen of us, about what happened in that hall then, and nobody can say it well. Danny says it was like the moment the drones strike in together and the whole room goes into tune and you feel it in your teeth. One of the Szabo twins says it was nothing, it was four in the morning and we were tired. But Danny's mother, who had come from Bratislava with one suitcase in 1949 and had sat through thirty-five minutes on the blood of the clans, put her face in her hands, and Lesia's father got up off his mat and went and stood by his daughter without waking her, and one of the Lakewood men in a good kilt said Aye, and then aye again, softer, as if he had been told something about himself. We all of us thought of our names. Hnatiuk. Kovac. Szabo. McCrimmon. MacAskill. What was in them, and what wasn't.

V. Toast to the Lassies

Thursday · first light

At six the furnace quit.

The Sokol custodian went down to the boiler room and came back up and said it was the flue, the snow had drifted over the vent on the lot side of the building and it was backing up and he had shut it off before it put us all to sleep for good. Nobody could open the lot-side door. The wind had packed it shut.

It was Don who knew where the vent was. He had sold a softener to the Sokol in 1974 and been in that boiler room, and he knew how the pipe ran out through the block wall and where it came up on the outside, behind the dumpster, eighteen inches off the ground, under, by then, about six feet of snow. He went out the front door, which faced away from the wind, with a coal shovel from the boiler room and the custodian's flashlight, and went around the building on his hands and knees in the kilt.

He was gone forty minutes. Bev stood at the front door with it open a crack the whole time. When he came back his beard was a solid block of ice and his knees were red and raw and he could not open his hands. But the custodian went down and lit the boiler and in a quarter of an hour the radiators began to knock.

We sat Don down by the urn and Bev and Danny's mother held his hands in a dishpan of lukewarm water, and he didn't say anything, and after a while he stood up. Snow was still coming in under the double doors at the front in a little white tongue across the floor. He looked at it. Then he took the bull's head badge off his shoulder and put it in his sporran, and unwound the plaid, all eight yards of MacLeod, and knelt down and rolled it tight and packed it along the bottom of the doors, the way you'd lay a sandbag, and pressed it in with his ruined hands until no more snow came through.

Murdo watched him do it. He didn't say anything.

•••

Lesia woke up and wanted to know what she had missed, and nobody could tell her. So Murdo asked her for a tune instead, a slow one, and she got out her pipes and stood up in the middle of the gym floor among the mats and the Slovak flags and the eighty people in yesterday's clothes and struck in her drones and played “MacCrimmon Will Never Return,” because it was the slow air she knew best, she said afterward, and she hadn't thought about the name.

Don sat on the floor by the door with his back against the wall and his hands in his lap and his eyes shut. The ice in his beard was melting and running down into his collar. He was listening with his whole face, the way Murdo always told us to listen, and nobody said anything to him, then or later. Behind him the plaid lay along the bottom of the doors, and the snow outside drifted up against it and stopped there in a long ridge, and held.

Background

The verse

A man does not become a Brahmana by his platted hair, by his family, or by birth; in whom there is truth and righteousness, he is blessed, he is a Brahmana.Dhammapada 393, tr. F. Max Müller (1881)

A Brahmin with matted hair, born of a Brahmin family, noticed that the Buddha called his own monks "Brahmins," and came to ask that the Buddha call him one too, since he was one by birth. The Buddha answered that he did not call anyone a Brahmin for his matted hair, his clan or his birth, but only for the truth and the Dhamma in him, and spoke the verse. Many who heard it reached the first stage of awakening. The whole story is only a few lines long. Read the full commentary story.

Parma, 1978

Parma, south of Cleveland, boomed after the Second World War into one of the largest suburbs in Ohio, home to General Motors and to large Ukrainian, Slovak, Polish and Hungarian Catholic communities; Cleveland television hosts made a running joke of its white socks and pink flamingos. In January 1977 the ABC miniseries Roots ran eight nights in a row and set off a wave of family-history research across the country. Scottish heritage societies, clan associations and amateur pipe bands grew with it. A Burns supper on or near January 25, Robert Burns's birthday, follows an old order: piping in, the Selkirk Grace, the haggis piped in and addressed, the Immortal Memory, the toasts. Since 1971 American haggis has been made without sheep's lung, which U.S. food rules forbid.

The Great Blizzard of 1978 struck the Ohio Valley and Great Lakes from January 25 to 27. Rain turned to heavy snow before dawn on the 26th, with winds strong enough to bring zero visibility all day. It was the worst blizzard in Ohio history: 51 people died, more than 5,000 National Guard troops were called out, and the whole Ohio Turnpike closed for the first time. The MacCrimmons were pipers for generations to the chiefs of Clan MacLeod, based at Borreraig near Dunvegan on Skye, where they kept a piping college. “MacCrimmon Will Never Return” is one of the best-known laments for the pipes.
