Brother Fly

… the rules of pilgrims were to abide under a strange roof, to thirst for their fatherland, and to pass on their way in peace.
— Bonaventure, Life of St. Francis VII

J’ai ouï dire ceci, on the levee at New Orleans.

I. The Shells

Paris and La Rochelle · 1719

He was called Jacquot la Coquille. A woman who may have been his mother set him on a church porch near the Halles when he was six, with his arm bound up in a rag to look withered, and at twelve he was taken into the trade proper. Begging in Paris had its ranks and its masters, like the drapers. There were the ones who chewed soap and fell down foaming, and the ones with a paper saying their house had burned, and the ones who went as pilgrims of Saint James, with cockle shells sewn on hat and cape and a staff with a gourd, and a tale of the long road home from Compostela.

Jacquot was a pilgrim. He had the shells, the staff, a cape that was more patch than cape, and a beard to his breastbone. He had never been south of the Orléans gate. In twenty years he had not done one day's work, and he said so with some pride. He lived on what was given, he said, like the birds of the air and the friars.

Then the Company wanted people for the Mississippi. In November of 1718 it was cried in Paris that vagabonds might be taken up and sent, and that winter the archers the streets called the bandouliers of the Mississippi went through the city, paid, it was said, by the head. They lifted Jacquot off his church steps, shells and all. He was held with others until spring, walked to La Rochelle on a chain, and put into a ship.

II. The Sack

New Orleans · summer 1724

They were set ashore on the sand at Biloxi, where there was nothing, and a good many did not get up from it. Jacquot got up. He reached the new town on the river in time for the September storm of 1722, which blew down most of what was standing, and he stayed, having nowhere to go that was not farther.

By the summer of 1724 it was a town of straight streets ruled through mud, huts of cypress posts, a levee and a guardhouse. And he found that a beggar there had no one to beg from. There were soldiers who had not been paid, clerks of the Company, men and women shipped out as he had been, and Africans who were not let to own even themselves. A man with shells on his hat got a laugh the first day and after that nothing. He lived on the levee off what fell from the boats.

The Capuchins had come the year before, friars of the province of Champagne in brown habits with long pointed hoods, corded and sandaled and bearded. Their superior had hardly been able to find a room to put them in, and fewer than thirty people came to his Sunday Mass. The Company was bound to keep them and paid when it remembered. With them was an old lay brother, Frère Zacharie, who had carried the alms sack for his convent at home for forty years. Nobody told him to take it up in Louisiana. He took it up. Each morning he went along the huts and the levee asking, for the love of God, for whatever there was: a measure of rice, some corn, a fish, a candle end. Part went to the fathers' table and the larger part to the sick in the King's hospital. Between rounds he split the wood, carried the water and dug the graves, and he spoke very little.

Jacquot watched him for a month, the way one tradesman watches another. Then one morning he stood in the brother's path on the levee, with people about.

Brother. A word, between two of the same trade.

The old man stopped.

You ask at doors. I have asked at doors since I could stand. You have a beard and I have a beard. You have a cord and a patched gown; look at this cape. You own nothing. They chained me by the neck for owning nothing and sent me here for it, and you came over as a passenger. Now the whole town calls you frère, down to the commandant. Very well. I live on alms the same as you. Let them call me frère too. And you may begin.

Frère Zacharie set down the sack. He looked at the shells.

Where does Saint James lie?

In Spain.

You have been?

I have been to the Mississippi, which is farther.

The people standing by laughed, and the brother very nearly did.

Our father Francis had a name ready for you, he said. You want to be called brother. Here it is.

If he beheld any man wandering about in idleness, and fain to feed on the toil of others, he thought he ought to be called “brother fly,” for that, doing no good himself, and spoiling the good done by others, he made himself an hateful pest unto all.

— Bonaventure, Life of St. Francis V

There were flies enough on the levee that morning to make the point, on the fish in the sack and on both their faces.

And you? said Jacquot. With your sack? What are you but the same fly in a hood?

If the sack were the whole of it, I would be. No one is a friar for asking. Nor for the gown, nor the beard. You wear a pilgrim's shells. Francis said what a pilgrim's rules are, and there are three of them.

For he said that the rules of pilgrims were to abide under a strange roof, to thirst for their fatherland, and to pass on their way in peace.

— Bonaventure, Life of St. Francis VII

Which of the three have you kept?

Jacquot opened his mouth. He had a strange roof, if the sky counted. He thirsted for Paris, but he understood well enough that Paris was not the country meant. And he had not passed one day of his life in peace with anybody, least of all the people who fed him.

The brother picked up his sack and went on down the levee. He said nothing more to him that day, nor, so far as anyone heard, on any day after.

A sergeant's wife was standing by, and two boatmen, and a clerk from the Company's store. It is told that the clerk went to confession that week for the first time since France, and that one of the boatmen carried the sack as far as the hospital. And by evening the whole levee had a name for Jacquot. They called him Frère Mouche.

III. The Hat

New Orleans · the fever months and after

He wore it as a joke at first. He answered to it with a bow and held out his hand. It brought him no more than the shells had.

In the fever months the hospital filled. One morning Jacquot saw the old brother coming out of the shed backward, bent double, hauling a dead soldier by the armpits because there was nobody for the feet. Jacquot looked up and down the levee. Then he went over and took the feet. Nobody had asked him and nobody thanked him. The next morning he was there before the brother was, and after the burying he carried the water.

Nobody saw him cut the shells off. Some children found seven cockles in the mud below the levee and played at pilgrims with them for a week. He never again asked any man to call him anything. He slept under the eave of the hospital shed, which was not his, and ate what the brother's sack brought, after the sick had eaten.

It is said that when a new father came out from France and asked who the bearded man digging in the cemetery was, a child told him, That is Brother Fly, and that Frère Zacharie, who did not often correct anyone, said, Leave off the fly.

Jacquot wore the same hat to the end of his life. It had seven pale places on the felt, each in the shape of a shell, where for twenty years the sun had not got at it.

The verses

A man is not a mendicant (Bhikshu) simply because he asks others for alms; he who adopts the whole law is a Bhikshu, not he who only begs.Dhammapada 266, tr. F. Max Müller (1881)
He who is above good and evil, who is chaste, who with knowledge passes through the world, he indeed is called a Bhikshu.Dhammapada 267, tr. F. Max Müller (1881)

In the commentary, a Brahmin who has gone forth in another sect and lives by begging reasons that since the Buddha calls his own alms-gathering disciples bhikkhus (the word means one who asks for alms), he ought to be called a bhikkhu too, and goes and says so. The Buddha answers that he does not call a man a bhikkhu merely because he receives alms or keeps the outward forms, and speaks the two verses. That is the whole story. New Orleans, 1724

John Law's Company of the West, chartered in 1717, was obliged to people Louisiana and could not find volunteers. An ordinance proclaimed in Paris on November 10, 1718, allowed vagabonds to be arrested and transported, and police squads nicknamed the bandouliers du Mississippi swept up beggars, deserters, smugglers and people whose families or enemies wanted them gone; they were marched to the ports in chains. The abuses were notorious, and a royal decree of May 9, 1720, ended the forced shipments. Many of those landed on the Gulf Coast died of hunger and disease. New Orleans, founded in 1718, became the colony's capital in 1722, the year a September hurricane leveled most of it. The Historic New Orleans Collection tells the story in "The Vagabond City."

In 1722 lower Louisiana was assigned to the Capuchins of the province of Champagne, a reformed branch of the Franciscans who by rule live on alms. Their superior, Father Raphaël, arrived in 1723 and reported that he could hardly find a room for his friars and that fewer than thirty people came to Sunday Mass; the brick-and-timber parish church was begun in 1725 (see the Catholic Encyclopedia on New Orleans). False pilgrims with cockle shells were an old and recognized class of Paris beggar. Jacquot and Frère Zacharie are invented, and whether the Louisiana Capuchins sent a brother out with the sack, as their houses in France did, is not recorded. Francis's "brother fly" and his rules of pilgrims are in St. Bonaventure's life of the saint, which every Franciscan knew; it is quoted in E. Gurney Salter's English of 1904.
