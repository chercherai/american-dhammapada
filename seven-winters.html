Seven Winters

Now, methinks, I stand easy.
— John Bunyan, The Pilgrim's Progress, the Second Part

The old people gave it this way, on the south end of San Juan Island.

I. The Carrying

Howard County, Missouri, and Marysville, California · 1852–1858

Lavinia Tolliver was carrying when her husband bought her. That was in Howard County, Missouri, in the spring of 1852. Amos Tolliver had been two years in the California diggings with the man who owned him. He came home with his own price paid and nine hundred dollars over in a belt, and he laid the nine hundred down for his wife. The paper was signed on a Tuesday. She was some four months along, and that night she told Amos the child had waited to have a free mother, and it could wait a little longer and have free ground.

People have told it since as though she were boasting. She said afterward that she only knew it, the way you know rain is coming.

They went out from Independence with a train in May. It was the cholera year on the Platte, with graves at every camp, and she walked most of the way behind the wagon because the jolting was worse than walking. Her time came due on the Humboldt and went by. The child moved in her, strong, and stayed where it was.

They stopped at Marysville. Amos shod mules and Viney washed for miners. California was called free soil. That same spring its legislature had made a law under which a colored person brought in before statehood could be claimed and carried back, and under an older one no colored person could give evidence against a white man in any court, so that Amos could be robbed at noon in the street and have no witness. It was not the ground she had meant.

She carried a year past her time, and then two. A doctor examined her, said there was a living child and that he could not account for it, and charged five dollars. The women at church stopped asking. She was not sick. She was heavy and slept propped against the wall, and she worked every day. In the evenings Amos read to her from the one book besides the Bible that had come across the plains with them, a Pilgrim's Progress with the boards gone. Near the beginning the pilgrim falls in a bog on the road, and someone asks why the place is never fixed. She had Amos read the answer twice each time he came to it. This miry slough is such a place as cannot be mended.

That's so, she would say. It isn't there for mending. It's there to get over.

They were six winters in California. In the spring of 1858 a bill went through the Assembly to shut the state against any more colored people, and a young man named Archy Lee was tried over and over in the courts on the question of whether he belonged to himself. In April the colored people of San Francisco sent thirty-five of their number north on the steamer Commodore to the English colony at Victoria, whose governor had sent word that they would be received there like anyone. Hundreds followed. The Tollivers went in July.

II. Seven Days

San Juan Island · October 1859

Victoria that year was tents and mud and men going to the Fraser for gold. Viney carried through a seventh winter there. Seven miles over the strait lay an island that the Queen and the United States both claimed, with little on it but the Hudson's Bay Company's sheep. In the spring of 1859 Amos hired on with the Company farm there. He was given a cabin with half a roof at a sheep station above the bay, among Kanaka shepherds from the Sandwich Islands and their wives. The Lummi and Songhees people had fished the reefs off that shore longer than anybody could say. Neither country had asked them.

In June an American settler shot a Company pig in his potatoes. By August there were American soldiers dug in on the south end with cannon, and five British warships lay in the bay with their ports open. Amos went for his wife in October all the same, because she sent for him to.

A Company sloop put them off at low water, and she went over the side and waded up onto the stones. She stood there a minute with her skirt dripping. She looked at the flag on the hill and then at the flags on the ships.

Neither one has got it yet, she said.

This will do, she said, and the first pain took her before the boxes were ashore.

It went on seven days. The child lay crosswise. Mrs. Oakes, whose husband kept the Company's oxen, came up from the home farm and did not go back. Keoni's wife, a Songhees woman from the next cabin, stayed two nights; twice she turned the child with her hands from outside, and twice it turned back. The roof was half on, and it rained.

Mrs. Oakes told it for forty years. She said that Viney did not curse or beg, and that when the pains were at their height she said three things over, always the same three. There is One who went through worse than this to make an end of it. There are people walking after Him, and I am one. There is a country where this is not. Then she would get her breath and wait for the next.

On the sixth morning she called Amos to the bed.

Go over to Victoria, to Mr. Pembroke. Don't ask him for a thing. Tell him Viney Tolliver greets him.

III. The Chair

Yates Street, Victoria · the seventh day

Lot Pembroke kept a barber's chair on Yates Street. He had been a ship's cook for thirty years, out of Newport first and then New Bedford, and a Quaker woman had taught him his letters when he was a boy. He owned a razor case, a Bible and a Bunyan. He read from the Bunyan to whoever was under the lather, and on Thursday nights he led a class of colored people in the room behind the shop. The Tollivers had sat in that class for a year. He seldom said ten words together that were his own.

It should have been one day by canoe. Keoni and his brother paddled, and fog held them overnight on a rock in the middle of the strait. Amos came into the shop at noon on the seventh day, wet to the knees. Mr. Pembroke was shaving the mate of a lumber brig.

Viney Tolliver greets you, Amos said. He had meant to stop there, as he had been told. Seven days she's been at it, he said.

Mr. Pembroke lifted the razor off the mate's throat and held it in the air. He looked at the clock on his wall, which said a quarter past twelve.

Let Lavinia Tolliver be well, he said. Let her be well, and bring a well son.

Then he finished the mate's left cheek. He told Amos to eat something and go home.

Amos was back the next evening. Mrs. Oakes met him on the stones with her sleeves still rolled. It was a boy. He had come on the seventh day a little after noon, as near as she could judge it by the tide. He had turned of himself, she said, like a man rolling over in bed, and after that it was no more trouble than any.

IV. The Dipper

The sheep station · November 1859

Viney was on her feet in three days. She sent word by the Company sloop that Mr. Pembroke and the whole class were to come up, and she would feed them a week.

Fourteen came. They slept on fir boughs in the cabin and under the sloop's sail, and for seven days she fed them mutton, clams, potatoes, bread out of the Dutch oven, and salmon bought off the canoes at a bit apiece. That same week the general the President had sent came to terms with the governor, and most of the soldiers and all but one of the ships went away.

Everybody who was there told the next part the same way. The child was three weeks old, and he looked three weeks old. On the first day of the feast he got down off the bed. He took the tin dipper from its nail, went to the bucket, and strained creek water through a cloth to get the fir needles out. Then he carried it round to the company in both hands, beginning with Mr. Pembroke. He did that all seven days and never spoke. Mr. Pembroke took the dipper from him each time and said thank you, as he would to a grown man, and made no other remark. When the sloop had gone the child lay down again and was a baby for the usual time.

Mrs. Oakes said he had had seven years to get his legs under him.

They named him Josiah. All his life he was called Tarry.

V. Halfway In

Griffin Bay · August 1880

The two countries kept a camp apiece on the island for twelve years and never fired a shot. In 1872 the Emperor of Germany, who had been asked to judge, gave the island to the United States. The marines went home, and most of the Kanaka families crossed to the British side, where they could hold land. The Tollivers stayed. There had been a war by then, and the ground was not what it had been in 1852. Tarry was twenty. He had signed his share of the homestead over to his sisters and he owned a boat. He carried people and freight across the line to Victoria and back for what they could pay, and Sundays he spoke a little at the schoolhouse. Nobody on the south end had seen him hurried, or put out, or wanting anything that was not already in his hand.

Mr. Pembroke was past eighty. He came up that August for the last time, to a meeting on the prairie above the bay, and after the dinner the old hands sat in the lee of the wagons and got to talking about Tarry, who was down at the water.

Think of it, Mrs. Oakes said. Seven years shut up in the dark. Over the Platte in the cholera, and six years in California, and then seven days crosswise in the rain with the guns run out. What that boy came through before he drew a breath.

They all agreed it was a wonder he was right in his mind.

Mr. Pembroke had seemed to be asleep. He came through it, he said. He is not in any of it now.

The Bunyan was in his coat. He turned to the end of the second part, where the last of the pilgrims goes down into the river, stops when he is halfway over, and talks to his friends on the bank. He read it in the voice he had used over the lather.

This river has been a terror to many; yea, the thoughts of it also have often frightened me. Now, methinks, I stand easy, my foot is fixed upon that upon which the feet of the priests that bare the ark of the covenant stood, while Israel went over this Jordan. The waters, indeed, are to the palate bitter, and to the stomach cold; yet the thoughts of what I am going to, and of the conduct that waits for me on the other side, doth lie as a glowing coal at my heart. I see myself now at the end of my journey, my toilsome days are ended.

— John Bunyan, The Pilgrim's Progress, the Second Part

Most of us will be old before we can say that, Mr. Pembroke said. That one says it at twenty, on a week day.

Toward evening Tarry took him down to the boat. The tide was out and she lay off the stones in three feet of water. Tarry walked out into the bay to his thighs and held the gunwale steady while two men handed the old barber across. That water is cold in August. He stood in it with both hands on the boat, in no hurry at all, until Mr. Pembroke was seated and had found his hat.

In the commentary, Suppavāsā of the Koliya clan carries a child for seven years and then labors for seven days because the child lies awry. She bears the pain by holding three thoughts, of the Buddha, of his followers, and of the peace where such pain is not, and she sends her husband to greet the Buddha in her name. He says, "May Suppavāsā be healthy; in health and happiness may she bring forth a healthy son," and at that moment she does. She feeds the Buddha and his monks for seven days, and from the day of his birth the child strains their drinking water. He grows up to become a monk and a saint. From California to San Juan Island, 1858–1859

California entered the Union as a free state, but an 1852 law let people who had been brought there enslaved before statehood be seized and returned, and Black Californians could not testify against whites in court. In 1858, the year of the Archy Lee case, the legislature took up a bill to bar Black immigration altogether. That April the Black community of San Francisco sent a Pioneer Committee of thirty-five north on the steamer Commodore to Victoria, in the British Colony of Vancouver Island, where Governor James Douglas, himself of mixed Caribbean ancestry, had let it be known they would be welcome. They landed on April 25, and several hundred people followed that year, among them Mifflin Gibbs. The Legislative Assembly of British Columbia tells the story here.

San Juan Island lies seven miles across Haro Strait from Victoria, in waters fished by Coast Salish peoples. The treaty of 1846 left it unclear which country owned it. In December 1853 the Hudson's Bay Company landed sheep and a crew of European, Hawaiian and Native men there and founded Belle Vue Sheep Farm; by 1859 some 4,500 sheep grazed at stations across the island. On June 15, 1859, an American settler shot a Company pig, and by August 461 American soldiers with fourteen cannon faced five British warships in what is called the Pig War. No one was hurt. General Winfield Scott arrived in October and arranged a joint military occupation, which lasted until an arbitration under the German emperor awarded the islands to the United States in 1872. Both camps are now San Juan Island National Historical Park. The Tollivers, Keoni, Mrs. Oakes and Lot Pembroke are invented, and no Black family is recorded on the Company farm. The Pilgrim's Progress (1678; second part 1684) was among the most widely owned books in English-speaking Protestant homes of the time; the text here follows George Offor's edition of the 1850s.
