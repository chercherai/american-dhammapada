From Its Stem

As from its stem the cucumber, so may I be released from death, not reft of immortality.
— Rig Veda 7:59:12

Maile yasto suneko chhu, on North Hill.

I. The Lot

North Hill, Akron, Ohio · June 2016

The garden was on a double lot off Dayton Street where two houses had come down, and the county land bank leased it to the neighborhood for a dollar a year. Rupa Niroula kept the lease in a binder. She was twenty-four and a program assistant with the county Extension office, which came to a county car on Tuesdays and Thursdays, a soil probe, a box of sample bags, and forty-two raised beds full of other people's grandparents.

She had been born in Beldangi-II, in a bamboo hut in Sector C, and had landed in Akron at seventeen. The first thing she did with the lot was push the probe into it in ten places and mail the dirt to the lab. The ground along the back fence came back at four hundred and ten parts per million of lead, a hundred years of house paint, so nobody grew in the ground. They grew in boxes of trucked compost, and every March she tested the boxes too and filed the report behind the lease.

On Wednesdays, in the tool shed, she taught the hundred questions. A refugee who was old or disabled could draw a check from Social Security for seven years from the day he landed, and after that only as a citizen, and a citizen had to be able to say in English who was the Father of Our Country and what was the supreme law of the land. Most of her class had landed in 2009. They sat on upturned buckets with the government's letters folded in their shirt pockets and said the Constitution after her, all together, and by the next Wednesday it had run out of them like water out of a basket. They had grown oranges and cardamom in Chirang and Sarbhang. They had been put across the border in 1992 and had sat eighteen and nineteen and twenty years in the camps in Jhapa, where there was nothing to hold on to, and they had held on. Constitution they could not hold.

The oldest gardener was a woman everybody called Boju, grandmother. She had bed number one, by the gate. She said she was ninety-one. Her papers gave her birthday as the first of January, like everybody else's, and no one argued with her. In Beldangi she had been the sudeni, the woman they sent for. She had caught better than nine hundred babies in that camp, Rupa among them, and when the cord was tied and cut she had said the same two words over every one. Dirghayu hos. Live long.

She had never come to the class. She grew cucumbers up strings, the long pale kind from home, and on each vine she let one alone. It hung there while the rest were picked, and swelled, and went yellow and then a netted brown like the skin of a russet potato. The first summer Rupa had asked whether she wanted that one picked before it spoiled.

That one's seed, the old woman said. It'll say when.

II. Forty-Eight Years

Schiller Avenue · the same month

Everybody on the Hill knew about Hari Prasad Timsina and told it one way. Two boys from one village in Chirang had gone off at fourteen behind a wandering baba, and for forty-eight years the pair of them kept the hard vows: one meal a day, no roof in the dry months, no woman, no money in the hand. They kept them in Bhutan. After 1992 they kept them under the pipal by the Shiva temple in Beldangi, and people coming back from the ration line touched their feet. In the forty-ninth year Hari Prasad watched the huts around him empty, family after family gone on the bus to the airport, and he thought, There will be nobody of mine anywhere. He walked down to Birtamod, to a sawmill owner with three daughters and bad luck. He took water in his right palm and poured the merit of forty-eight years out of his hand into the sawmill owner's. The sawmill owner gave him a lakh of rupees and a buffalo in milk. With that he cut his hair and married Dhan Maya, who was half his age. Their son was born the next year, and in 2013 the three of them flew to Akron on the same papers as everybody else.

Now he was sixty-eight and the boy was five. Dhan Maya worked nights in a commercial laundry. Hari Prasad walked the boy to kindergarten and back, and old women on Cuyahoga Falls Avenue who had touched his feet in the camp studied the sidewalk until he had gone by. He had bed thirty, against the back fence. He grew mustard greens there and spoke to no one. They called the boy Sano, the little one. His father had not dared put a heavier name on him.

The other of the two had never left off. He had walked up to Kedarnath and down to Rameswaram and back to the camp, and in June of 2016, with the camps nearly empty, he came off a plane at Akron-Canton in an orange cloth and rubber sandals, carrying the white plastic bag the agency gives every refugee for his papers. They put him with a nephew on Schiller Avenue. People called him the Baba. Hari Prasad knew within the hour that he had come, and did not go for four days. On the fifth he took his wife and the boy and went.

He gave the boy to Dhan Maya and got down and put his forehead on the Baba's feet.

Ayushman bhava, the Baba said. May you live long.

He took the boy back, and Dhan Maya bowed. Ayushman bhava.

Then they stood Sano on the linoleum, and his father put a hand on the back of his head, the way it is done with small children, and the boy bowed. The Baba looked down at the part in his hair and said nothing.

You said it to me, Hari Prasad said. You said it to her.

Something is waiting for this one.

How long has he got?

Seven days.

Dhan Maya had the boy up off the floor before the second word was out.

Then turn it. You kept yours. I know what you're carrying.

I can read it, brother. I never learned to turn anything.

Who can?

The old woman in the garden, the one who caught the babies. Go and ask her.

She put her head on my feet in Beldangi. How do I stand in front of her with my hair cut?

If you love the boy, the Baba said, never mind your hair.

III. Bed Number One

Dayton Street · the next morning

Rupa was restringing bean poles when they came through the gate. Boju sat on her bucket by bed number one with a dishpan of seed garlic in her lap. Hari Prasad came down the wood-chip path ahead of his wife with his eyes on his own feet, and when he reached the bucket he got down in the chips.

Dirghayu hos, said the old woman.

Dhan Maya bowed. Dirghayu hos.

They set the boy down. He bowed without being pushed. Boju looked at him for some time, and then she went back to her garlic.

Hari Prasad asked her what he had asked the Baba, and she gave him the Baba's answer, down to the number.

Is there no way to turn it, Boju?

There might be.

What way?

She told him in the voice she used for how deep to set an onion. Put up a tent in front of your door. Put one seat in the middle of it and chairs around that in a ring, eight, or sixteen if you can borrow sixteen. Have the Tryambakam said in that tent seven days and seven nights, and don't let it stop once, for food or for rain or for sleep. Said like that, it might turn the thing off him.

A tent I can put up. Who is going to say it? I gave mine away, and I can't pay sixteen pandits for a week.

You see to the tent. I'll send my people.

She looked across the beds at Rupa. Rupa had a degree in public health from Kent State and a county badge on a lanyard, and she found she already had her phone out and was counting folding chairs.

The tent was the blue-and-white twenty-by-twenty the community association put up for weddings. The chairs were the Extension office's, sixteen of them, signed out on the form for a garden program. The seat in the middle was a pirka, a low wooden stool somebody's mother had carried from Jhapa in a suitcase. Seven days is a hundred and sixty-eight hours. Rupa ruled a sheet into fifty-six watches of three hours, eight lines to a watch, because she knew no other way to keep old people in one place, and carried it round the garden on a clipboard. Every line was full before she got to the back fence. The names on it were the Wednesday class.

IV. The Tent

Blaine Avenue · seven days

They began on a Thursday at six in the morning, in front of the duplex on Blaine Avenue where the Timsinas had the downstairs.

Om tryambakam yajamahe, sugandhim pushti-vardhanam; urvarukam iva bandhanan, mrityor mukshiya mamritat.

Eight said it and eight sat quiet between them, every other chair. A quarter of an hour before a watch ended the quiet eight took it up underneath, so that for a while sixteen were saying it, and then the first eight let go and went in to eat. It never had a seam in it. The boy sat on the pirka in the middle with a box of crayons. He slept there against a bolster, and ate there, and was carried in to the toilet and back by his father, who did not sit in the ring. Hari Prasad made the tea. At night he lay on the porch boards with one arm through the railing toward the tent.

Rupa came before work and after. She watched Mrs. Khatiwada, who in four years had not once got past George Washington, say the whole of it nine hundred times before noon without looking at anything. None of them looked at anything. They had it the way they had their own names.

Across the street lived a Mrs. DiPaolo, who was eighty-six and had been on Blaine since Truman. The first day she watched from her porch. The second day she crossed over and asked Rupa what they were saying. Rupa knew it in two languages, and neither one was any use to Mrs. DiPaolo. That night she found it in an old translation at the library, printed it at the office, and taped it to the tent pole in a plastic sleeve.

Tryambaka we worship, sweet augmenter of prosperity. As from its stem the cucumber, so may I be released from death, not reft of immortality.

— Rig Veda 7:59:12

Cucumber, Mrs. DiPaolo said. She read it again. When it's ready it comes off in your hand. When it isn't, you got to twist. She looked at the boy on his stool. Then she went home and ran a hundred feet of extension cord across Blaine Avenue from her porch outlet, with a box fan on the end of it, and all that week the cars slowed down and eased over the cord.

It rained hard on Sunday afternoon. The tent roof bellied like a basin, and two grandsons pushed the water off from underneath with a broom while the saying went on. On Monday at two in the morning a police cruiser stopped at the curb, on a call from the next street. The officer was young. There was no permit for what they were doing, because there was no form for it, and Rupa showed him the sheet in the plastic sleeve. He read it by flashlight. He asked how long. Until Thursday. He asked whether they could keep it down, and she told him the truth, that this was as far down as it went. He stood a while at the edge of the tent with his thumbs in his vest. The boy was asleep with his mouth open. Around him sat eight people in their seventies and eighties in shawls and knit caps, and the thing went on like water over a sill. The officer wrote something in his book and drove off. The cruiser came down Blaine every night after that, about two, slowly, and did not stop.

V. The Seventh Night

Blaine Avenue · Wednesday

Boju had not come all week. On Wednesday at dusk she came down Blaine Avenue on her granddaughter's arm, in a white cotton sari and green rubber garden clogs, and took the chair that faced the boy.

The Hill came after her. People stood in the yards on both sides and then in the street, and Mrs. DiPaolo's porch was full of strangers. Those were the living. The ones who sat in the ring that night say it was fuller than that. They say the gods came, the great ones with their names and the small ones of springs and crossroads that only one village in Chirang had ever fed. When the great ones had taken their places there was no room left on Blaine Avenue, and the small ones had to give ground, back to Cuyahoga Falls Avenue, back out North Main to the High Level Bridge and over the gorge into Cuyahoga Falls.

Among the small ones, they say, was a yaksha who had served Kubera twelve years and had his wages in writing: Seven days from now you may take this boy. He had come early. He had been standing by the porch steps since Thursday with his paper in his hand. When the crowd came in he was moved back with the rest, a step, then a yard, then a mile, still holding the paper up over the heads so that someone would see it, and no one made way.

Boju said it all night and did not change with the watches. Her voice was not strong. Rupa sat on the curb and could not always find it under the others, and then toward four o'clock she could hear nothing else.

At six on Thursday the ring stopped. There was the fan, and there were birds. They woke the boy and stood him on the grass, and he bowed.

Dirghayu hos, Boju said.

Dhan Maya sat down in the street.

How long, Boju? said Hari Prasad. How long has he got now?

A hundred and twenty years.

So they named him over again in the tent that morning. He had been Sano. After that he was Ayubardhan, the one whose life was added to, and at school they shortened it to Ayu.

VI. Four Things

Dayton Street · September

One Wednesday in September the class never got to the hundred questions. The old people wanted to talk about the boy.

Seven days he had, and now it's a hundred and twenty years, said Mrs. Khatiwada. There's a reason behind a thing like that. A life doesn't get longer of itself.

Boju had come into the shed for once, out of the sun, and was sitting on a fertilizer pail by the door.

What are you all on about?

They told her.

It isn't only the years, she said. Then she gave them a line of Sanskrit, and every head in the shed came up, because each of them had it from a grandfather. Rupa had it from hers. She had never seen it in English until she looked that night.

He who habitually salutes and constantly pays reverence to the aged obtains an increase of four (things), (viz.) length of life, knowledge, fame, (and) strength.

— Laws of Manu 2:121

Manu said a good deal I wouldn't repeat to a girl, Boju said. That one he had right.

Four things. Rupa thought of the lab report in her binder, acidity and phosphorus and potassium and organic matter, four lines that had gone up every spring in boxes of made dirt set on poisoned ground. She kept it to herself.

Eleven of the Wednesday class took the oath the next year, some by a doctor's paper and some the long way. Mrs. Khatiwada got the Constitution out whole in front of the officer on her second try.

The boy is in the ninth grade at North now. On Wednesdays he interprets at the Social Security office for anybody's grandmother. He cannot get from the bus stop to his own door without stopping, because wherever he walks on the Hill there are old people looking out for him, five hundred of them if you counted, and he gets down in front of each one, in wood chips or on the sidewalk on North Main, and each one says it.

That afternoon in September, when the shed had emptied, Boju went back to bed number one. She put her hand under the brown cucumber on the first vine, only to feel the weight of it, and it came away from its stem into her palm.

Background

The verse

He who always greets and constantly reveres the aged, four things will increase to him, viz. life, beauty, happiness, power.Dhammapada 109, tr. F. Max Müller (1881)

In the commentary, two Brahmin ascetics keep their austerities for forty-eight years. One of them returns to the world, sells the merit of his practice for cattle and money, marries and has a son. When the family visits the old companion, the ascetic says "Live long!" to the father and the mother and is silent for the boy, who has seven days to live. He sends them to the Buddha, who gives the same blessing and the same silence, then tells the father to raise a pavilion at his door with a seat in the middle and eight or sixteen seats around it. Monks recite the protective texts there for seven days and nights without a break, and on the last night the Buddha comes and recites until dawn. So many gods crowd in that an ogre, who had served twelve years for the right to take the boy, is pushed back and cannot reach him. The boy is promised a hundred and twenty years and renamed Āyuvaḍḍhana, "life increased." North Hill, Akron, 2016

In the early 1990s Bhutan forced out more than 100,000 of its Nepali-speaking southerners, the Lhotshampa. Most of them spent the next two decades in camps in southeastern Nepal, among them Beldangi, Sanischare, Timai, Goldhap and Khudunabari. Resettlement abroad began in 2008, and by early 2017 more than 90,000 of the Bhutanese refugees had come to the United States. Several thousand settled in Akron, most of them in North Hill, an old Italian neighborhood; public radio's account of the first ten years describes the elders' isolation, the language barrier and the citizenship classes. The people, the garden and the week in the tent are invented.

Under the 1996 welfare law as amended in 1997, elderly or disabled refugees can receive Supplemental Security Income for seven years after arrival and after that only as citizens, which for people who arrived in 2008 and 2009 made the English civics test, drawn from a list of one hundred questions, a matter of rent. The four hundred parts per million in Rupa's soil report was the federal hazard level for lead in bare soil where children play. The verse the elders chant is the Mahamrityunjaya mantra, Rig Veda 7.59.12, said across the Hindu world for long life; it is given here in Ralph T. H. Griffith's translation of 1896. Boju's line is Manu 2.121 in Georg Bühler's translation of 1886. Its Sanskrit and the Pāli of Dhammapada 109 share their first line and two of their four increases, life and strength.
