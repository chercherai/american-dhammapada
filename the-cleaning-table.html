The Cleaning Table

The rewards or punishments appointed for men’s souls after the Particular Judgment are Heaven, Purgatory, and Hell.
— Baltimore Catechism No. 1, Q. 412

The way I heard it, on Southwest Forty-Seventh Terrace.

I. Twelve Years

Cape Coral, Florida · 1999–2011

Father Leo Brandl came down to Cape Coral in 1999, when the Diocese of St. Cloud retired him, because his lungs could not take another January in Stearns County. He was seventy. He had been a priest forty-three years in six parishes with onion domes and bingo halls and a hundred Schmitts and Theisens apiece, and he arrived in Florida with two suitcases, a set of golf clubs he did not use, and the name of one family he knew.

Roger and Mary Jo Kessler had come down from St. Cloud three years before. Roger had a jewelry store in a strip mall on Del Prado Boulevard, between a Subway and a place that did nails, and they had a ranch house with a pool cage on a canal off the Caloosahatchee, one of the four hundred miles of canals the developers had dug out of the mangroves in the fifties to sell lots to people from Ohio. Mary Jo had been in Father Leo's eighth-grade religion class in Albany in 1967. The first night he was in town she had him to supper, and he came the next night too, and after that, for twelve years, he ate his supper at their table every night of the week except Sundays, when he said the nine o'clock at the parish and ate with the pastor.

He had his chair. He had his napkin ring, a wooden one Kyle made in shop class. Mary Jo kept his pills in a cup on the windowsill and his decaf in its own can. He said grace, a short one, the one from home, and ate whatever she put down, and told the same stories about Albany and Richmond and Melrose until the Kessler kids could tell them back to him. Kyle, the youngest, grew up at that table and went to work behind the counter at the store, and Roger taught him the bench. Nobody in that house in twelve years ever heard Father Leo say a sharp word about a living person.

In March he and Roger went to spring training. They had gone every year since 1999, out Daniels Parkway to Hammond Stadium to sit in the sun behind the Twins dugout with men from Edina and Fargo and Sioux Falls in the same windbreakers, and watch the regulars play three innings and the kids from the minor-league camps play the rest. Father Leo kept score in pencil. On the evenings after, the two of them sat out on the dock while Roger cleaned whatever he had caught that day, with the radio on the cleaning table giving them the end of whatever game there was.

And every evening, at the hour when Roger cleaned fish, the heron came.

He was a great blue, four feet tall, gray as a filing cabinet, with a black plume going back from his eye. He had come first in 2003, standing at the end of the dock in the evening like somebody waiting to be served, and Roger had thrown him a sheepshead head, and that was that. Kyle named him Herb, after Herb Carneal, who had done the Twins on the radio since before Kyle was born, because he showed up every night at seven. Herb would walk right up the dock to the cleaning table, one long careful step and then another, and stand there an arm's length from Roger with his neck pulled down into his shoulders, and wait, and Roger would toss him the heads and the frames and the guts, and he would take them out of the air. He went up and down the canal all day, to the other docks, but at seven he came to the Kesslers'. Father Leo said Herb was the only parishioner he had ever had who was never once late.

II. The Pearl

Friday, March 25, 2011

It was the third Friday in Lent. Roger had been out at the mouth of the river since noon and come home with six sheepshead, and Mary Jo wanted them for supper before Stations, so he was at the cleaning table at a quarter to six with his hands red to the wrists. Father Leo was in his lawn chair. Herb was at the end of the dock. The radio on the table had the scores from the afternoon; the Twins had lost again, the way they were going to lose all that year.

A boat came down the canal, slow, a white center-console with two big outboards, and swung in to the dock. It was Gary Lund's son-in-law. Gary Lund owned four car dealerships on Pine Island Road and was on television every night of the week in a gold paper crown, as the Car King of Cape Coral, and he was the biggest customer the store had, and the only one Roger had ever been afraid of. The son-in-law did not get off the boat. He held out a little blue velvet bag.

Gary says clean it, drill it, put it on a chain. He wants it Sunday for Cheryl. It's their fortieth.

Roger knew what it was before he had it open. It was the pearl from Gary's grandmother, which he had been allowed to see once, in the store, and had not been allowed to touch. It was a natural pearl, not a cultured one, fished up somewhere in the Persian Gulf before the First World War, the size of a chickpea, white with a rose light in it, and Roger had looked it up afterward and knew that it was worth more than the house behind him.

He had blood on his hands. He tipped it out of the bag anyway, onto his palm, because he could not help it, and looked at it, and set it down on a square of clean paper towel on the corner of the table, away from the fish. The boat backed off and went on down the canal. Roger went up to the house to wash his hands and get his loupe.

Herb had watched the whole thing from the end of the dock. As soon as Roger was gone he came up the boards, one long step and then another, to the corner of the table, and put his head down and looked at the pearl a moment with one yellow eye. It had blood on it, and fish slime, and it smelled like the table, and it was about the size of the things that came off the table to him every night. He took it, and tipped his bill up, and it went down his long neck in a lump.

Father Leo saw it. He was ten feet away in his lawn chair with his hands on his knees.

Roger came back down with clean hands and the loupe in his pocket, and the paper towel was there, and the pearl was not.

He looked under the table. He looked in the fish bucket and in the gut bucket and between the boards of the dock with a flashlight. He went up to the house, where Mary Jo was cutting potatoes and Kyle was eating a slice of cheese, and asked them, and they said no, Dad, no, honey, what pearl. He came back down and looked in the water, which was black. He looked at Father Leo.

Then he went up again and got Mary Jo by the elbow and took her out on the lanai and said it in a whisper. He took it. Leo took it. There was nobody else on the dock.

Roger Kessler. Don't you ever say that. Twelve years that man has sat at our table, and I have never once seen him so much as take the last roll.

Then where is it?

I don't know where it is. I know where it isn't.

He went back down. Father. Did you pick up that pearl? Maybe you just wanted to look at it.

No, Roger. I didn't take it.

There was nobody else here. It was you and me and it was on the table. Give it back and we'll forget it.

I don't have it.

Mary Jo came down the lawn with her car keys. They were late for Stations and Kyle was already in the car. She looked at her husband's face.

Roger, whatever you're thinking, stop thinking it. If we have to sell the store, we'll sell the store. I'd rather go back to St. Cloud and clean houses.

If we sold the store, said Roger, and this house, and the boat, and Kyle's truck, we couldn't pay for that pearl.

She stood there a moment longer. Then she went, because the car was running and she did not believe it of him either. She did not believe it of anybody she knew.

III. Stations

The dock · dusk

He asked again, and again. It got dark. The lights came on under the pool cages up and down the canal, and the dock light came on over the table on its timer, and the bugs began. Father Leo sat in the lawn chair and said no, Roger, I don't have it, and that was all he said.

Roger was a man who had never in his life hit anybody, not his kids, not a dog. He was sixty-one years old and on blood-pressure pills and sang bass in the choir. Afterward he could not have said how it began, only that he had been talking and then he had the dock line off the cleat in his hands, and he had tied the old man to the piling by the chest with it, the way you would tie a boat, and was standing over him with the fish club from the table, the short heavy billy he used to quiet the fish. And then he was not talking.

He hit him on the head. He hit him again. The blood came out of Father Leo's hair and ran down into his eyes, and then it came out of his ears, and out of his nose, and his eyes were open very wide and looked as if they were pushing out of his face, and he did not say anything at all. He went down sideways against the piling as far as the rope would let him, and lay with his cheek on the boards.

There was blood on the boards. Herb came up the dock, one long step and then another, out of the dark at the end into the light, with his neck down, the way he came up every night for the fish, and put the tip of his bill in the blood by the old man's face and began to drink.

Roger kicked him. He was not kicking the bird; he was kicking at everything. It was one kick with a boat shoe, and it caught Herb high on the long neck, and the bird went over on the boards and lay on his back with his yellow legs folded up and his wings half open, and did not move again.

Father Leo turned his head a little on the boards and looked at him.

Roger, he said. His voice was all right. Loosen that rope a little. Go look at Herb. See if he's dead.

You'll be dead like him.

Herb took it. He swallowed it off the paper while you were washing up. I watched him do it. He shut his eyes. If he'd still been alive, I'd have let you go on till I was dead before I told you.

Roger stood there with the club. Then he dropped it, and took the fillet knife off the table, and knelt by the bird and opened his throat down the long neck, the way he would have opened a fish, and the pearl was the first thing that came out into his hand, white, with the rose light in it, under the dock light, with Herb's blood on it.

He began to shake. He shook so that he dropped the knife and it went between the boards into the canal. He got the rope off the old man with his hands shaking and could not untie it and cut it finally on the scaler, and then he was on his knees on the boards with his face down on Father Leo's shoes.

Father. Forgive me. I didn't know. I didn't know what I was doing.

Father Leo had got himself sitting up against the piling. He put his hand on the back of Roger's head.

It's not your fault, Roger, he said. And it's not mine. It's the way this world goes around. That's all. I forgive you. You know I do.

Then come up to the house. Please. Mary Jo will be back. Come in and sit in your chair.

The old man looked at the house, at the lights in the kitchen and the table set for four under the lamp, his napkin in its wooden ring.

No, he said. I've been going in under people's roofs since 1956. That's how this happened. I'm not going in under anybody's roof again. From now on, whatever anybody hands me, I'll take it at the door. I can still walk.

He would not go to the hospital. He let Roger wet a dish towel in the cooler and hold it to his head, and then he took it and held it himself, and walked home, the two streets to his condo, slowly, in the dark, with the towel on his head and his other hand on the fences.

IV. At the Door

Southwest Forty-Seventh Terrace · the last week of March

Mary Jo found Roger on the dock when she came home from Stations, sitting next to the dead heron with the pearl in his open hand. She went straight down the street to Father Leo's and banged on the door until he opened it. He had washed. There was a cut in his scalp and a bruise coming up the side of his face like weather. He said he had bumped it. He said he was fine. He would not go to the hospital for her either.

The next evening at six he did not come to supper. He came down the street instead, slowly, and stood at the Kesslers' front door and rang, and when Mary Jo opened it he would not come in. She brought him a plate, and he ate a few bites of it standing on the step with the plate in his hand, and gave it back to her, and thanked her, and went on to the next house.

He did that every night that week. He went down Southwest Forty-Seventh Terrace in the evening the way the mailman went down it in the morning, and rang at the doors of people he had married and buried and heard the confessions of, Minnesotans and Michiganders and a family from Puerto Rico, and stood on their welcome mats and would not come in. They thought he was getting confused. They brought him whatever was on their stove: a piece of meatloaf, a spoonful of hotdish on a paper plate, half a sandwich, a cup of soup, rice and beans. He took a bite or two at each door, and said thank you, and went on. By the end of the street he had had his supper, a little from every house. He said he had never eaten better in his life. He said it was a good thing he could still walk.

On Thursday night he went to bed and did not get up. Mary Jo found him in the morning when he did not answer his phone. The medical examiner said it was bleeding inside the skull, slow, the kind that can let a man walk around and talk for days before it takes him, and that it came from blows to the head, and asked who had struck him. Roger told them. He told the detective the whole thing at the kitchen table, from the boat to the knife, and signed it.

Kyle drilled the pearl. It was on its chain on Sunday, and Gary Lund's wife wore it at the Westin for their fortieth, and Gary never said a word to Roger about any of it, before or after.

V. Heaven, Purgatory, and Hell

Cape Coral · 2011–2019

Roger pleaded to manslaughter in the fall and went to the state prison up at Raiford. Mary Jo drove up to see him the first Saturday of every month, five hours each way. He wrote her every week in the beginning, and then less, and the letters were mostly about the food and the noise and a man from Ohio in the next bunk. In 2017 his heart stopped in the yard. He was sixty-seven. She buried him in St. Cloud next to his parents.

Kyle's girlfriend had a baby that December, a boy, and they named him Leo, and when Kyle's girlfriend went back to Fort Myers in 2013, the boy stayed with his grandmother on the canal. He was a tall, thin, silent child who ate raw shrimp out of the bait bucket when nobody was looking. From the time he could walk he went down to the end of the dock in the evenings and stood on one leg at the edge of the boards, watching the water, without moving, for as long as anyone would let him. The first time Mary Jo saw him do it she sat down on the lanai step and could not get up for a while.

Look at that, she said to Kyle. Herb's back. It was a family joke after that, and she was the only one who did not quite say it as a joke.

Mary Jo died in the spring of 2019, in the hospice in Fort Myers, of the cancer she had not told anybody about until it was too late to matter. Half the parish came to the funeral, and the women from the Altar Society did the lunch, and people stood up one after another and said that she had been the kindest person they knew, and that wherever she was now she had earned it.

That May Leo was in the second grade and in Mrs. Theisen's First Communion class on Wednesday nights in the parish hall. Arlene Theisen was eighty-one. She had taught religion in Melrose for fifty years before she retired to Florida and started over, and she still taught from her mother's little catechism, printed in 1912 and held together with Scotch tape, though the diocese had sent her new books with pictures. The children had been asked to bring a question.

Leo raised his hand. Where did my grandma go? And where did my grandpa go? And where did Father Leo go, that I'm named after? And where did Herb go?

Some of the children laughed at Herb. Mrs. Theisen did not. She knew who Herb was. Everybody in that parish over the age of thirty knew. She looked at the boy for a while, and then she opened the little blue book to near the end, where it fell open by itself, and read it to the class the way she had read it to fifty years of children in Melrose, question and answer, so that they could say it back.

Q. What are the rewards or punishments appointed for men's souls after the Particular Judgment?
A. The rewards or punishments appointed for men's souls after the Particular Judgment are Heaven, Purgatory, and Hell.

Q. What is Hell?
A. Hell is a state to which the wicked are condemned, and in which they are deprived of the sight of God for all eternity, and are in dreadful torments.

Q. What is Purgatory?
A. Purgatory is a state in which those suffer for a time who die guilty of venial sins, or without having satisfied for the punishment due to their sins.

Q. What is Heaven?
A. Heaven is the state of everlasting life in which we see God face to face, are made like unto Him in glory, and enjoy eternal happiness.

— Baltimore Catechism No. 1, Q. 412–414, 420

She closed the book. That's three places, she said. Your grandpa did a terrible thing, and he told the truth about it, and he was sorry, and it's not for me to say where that puts a man. Your grandma, I'd say she went the short way. And some people, Leo, there is nothing in them left to burn off when they go, nothing at all, and they go straight in and see Him face to face. I'd say Father Leo was one of those.

And Herb?

Mrs. Theisen took off her glasses and folded them.

The book doesn't say about herons, she said. Your grandma had her own opinion about that, and I wouldn't go against her. She put her glasses back on. Now. Who knows what a sacrament is?

Kyle sold the house that summer. The new people tore out the old dock and put in a boat lift and a composite deck with lights set into the edges, and the cleaning table went out to the curb on bulk-trash day with the brown stain still on the corner of it, soaked into the wood where it would never come out. Leo and his father lived in an apartment off Santa Barbara Boulevard after that, with no water near it. On Sundays, after the nine o'clock, Kyle drove him down to the public pier at the Yacht Club, where there are always fishermen cleaning their catch at the end of the day and always a heron or two standing by them, waiting, and the boy would walk out to the end and stand beside the herons, on one leg, with his hands in his pockets, and they did not move away from him.

Background

The verse

Some people are born again; evil-doers go to hell; righteous people go to heaven; those who are free from all worldly desires attain Nirvana.Dhammapada 126, tr. F. Max Müller (1881)

In the commentary, the Elder Tissa has taken his meals for twelve years at the house of a jeweler, who with his wife has looked after him like a parent. One day, while the jeweler is cutting meat with the elder sitting by, King Pasenadi sends a gem to be cleaned and pierced. The jeweler takes it with bloody hands, sets it down and goes in to wash, and the household's pet heron, smelling the blood, swallows it before the elder's eyes. The jeweler asks his wife and sons, then accuses the elder. His wife pleads that in all those years she has never seen a flaw in him, and that they would do better to become slaves; the jeweler answers that all of them sold as slaves would not pay for the stone. He binds the elder's head with a rope and beats him until blood runs from his head, ears and nose. The heron comes to drink the blood, and the jeweler kills it with a kick. Only then does the elder tell him to look in the bird, saying he would have died rather than speak while it lived. The jeweler finds the gem, falls at his feet and is forgiven ("it was neither your fault nor mine"), but the elder vows never again to go under anyone's roof and to take alms only at the door, says a short verse about food cooked "little by little, house by house," and soon dies of the beating. The heron is reborn in the jeweler's wife's womb, the jeweler in hell, his wife in heaven, and the elder passes into final Nibbāna. Read the full commentary story, or about King Pasenadi.

Cape Coral, 2011

Cape Coral was begun in 1957 by two brothers from Baltimore, Leonard and Jack Rosen, whose Gulf American Land Corporation dredged the mangrove flats across the Caloosahatchee from Fort Myers into building lots; the city has more than 400 miles of canals. Since Hammond Stadium opened in Fort Myers in 1991, the Minnesota Twins have held spring training there, and every February and March brings Minnesotans south to watch. The 2011 Twins went from first place the year before to last in the American League Central, at 63 wins and 99 losses. The great blue heron, the largest heron in North America and up to four and a half feet tall, lives mostly on fish and is at home wherever there is fish-bearing water, however built up. The Kesslers, the Lunds, Father Leo and the street are invented.

The Baltimore Catechism, issued in 1885 after the Third Plenary Council of Baltimore, was the standard Catholic school text in the United States from then until the late 1960s, and generations of children learned its questions and answers by heart; the passage here is from No. 1, the edition for First Communion classes. In Lent, Catholics abstain from meat on Fridays, and the Stations of the Cross are prayed especially on Lenten Fridays. A subdural hematoma, bleeding between the brain and its covering after a blow, can take days to show itself, with headache and growing confusion, and is especially common in the elderly.
