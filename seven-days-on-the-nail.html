Seven Days on the Nail

Your gold and your silver have become covered with rust, and the rust on them will give evidence against you, and will eat your flesh like fire.
— James 5:3 (Weymouth)

Näin minä sen kuulin, in the East End of Negaunee.

I. The Sarka

Negaunee and Ishpeming, Michigan · spring 1928

Toivo Rautio’s mother sent him eight yards of sarka from Ostrobothnia, folded in brown paper and tied with string and smelling, when he opened it on his bunk, of the shed where she kept the loom. It was the coarse gray homespun that farmers wore in the old country to the fields and to church and to their graves. It was as stiff as a horse blanket. He was twenty-three and had been four years in Michigan, tramming ore cars underground on the night shift, and he had not had a new coat since the one he came over in.

On Sunday he took it over to his sister in Ishpeming, three miles west.

Hilja was married to a timberman and had two children and a loom that took up half the front room, a big four-post loom that her husband had built her out of maple, on which she wove rag rugs for the company store at a dollar and a quarter apiece. She unfolded the sarka on the kitchen table and rubbed it between her fingers and looked at her brother, who was the youngest of seven and the only one of them besides herself who had come over, and she thought: this is not good enough for him.

She did not say so. She said, Leave it with me a while.

When he had gone back to Negaunee she took the shears to it. She cut the sarka into strips the way she cut old shirts for rugs, and soaked the strips in a tub, and beat them on the back step with a wooden paddle until the weave gave up and the wool came loose, and picked the strips apart thread by thread at the kitchen table in the evenings after the children were asleep. She washed the wool twice and carded it on her mother’s cards, which she had brought over in her trunk, and spun it on the wheel, finer than her mother had spun it, as fine as she could make it go without breaking, until her fingertips were raw. Then she put it on the loom and wove it, a close, soft twill, dark gray with a thread of black, and because she had spun it so fine there was more of it than there had been. It came off the loom nine yards long.

It took her until the end of July. She kept back the last of the yarn on a bobbin in her sewing box.

•••

Toivo came for his cloth at the beginning of August with two men from his boarding house, old Kalle Niemi and Jussi Pelto, who had been tailors in Vaasa before the mines and still sewed in the evenings for the other boarders at fifty cents an evening, and who had brought their shears and their chalk and their thread in a cigar box.

Hilja took the cloth down from the shelf and put it in his hands. He unfolded it on the table, and the tailors leaned over it, and Kalle whistled.

This isn’t mine, Toivo said. Mine was sarka. Eight yards, coarse. This is fine cloth, this is nine yards at least. This is yours. Give me mine.

It’s yours. Take it.

I don’t want what’s yours. Give me what I brought.

She held it out to him again and he put his hands behind his back like a boy. So she told him. She told him about the shears and the tub and the paddle and the cards and the wheel and her fingertips, and showed him her fingertips, and put the cloth in his hands a third time.

This one is yours, she said. Take it.

He took it.

II. The Coat

Mrs. Lehto’s boarding house, the East End · August

Mrs. Lehto kept fourteen men in a long frame house on the East End of Negaunee, two to a room and some of them three, the day shift sleeping in the beds the night shift had just got out of. There was a sauna in the yard that she heated on Saturdays, with an iron kettle built into the stove for the wash water. The Finns called a house like hers a poikatalo, a boys’ house, though some of the boys were fifty.

The tailors cut the coat on her kitchen table, after supper, with the oilcloth rolled back. They made it the way coats were made for Sunday in Vaasa, long, with a high notch and three buttons, and they argued about the shoulders for two nights. Hilja came over from Ishpeming every evening that week, her husband driving her in the Ford when he was on days. She brought coffee and a braid of pulla with cardamom in it, and once a pan of rice pudding, and she fed the tailors and the boarders who sat around the kitchen watching, and on the last night, when the coat was done, she brought a whole cake with lingonberries on it and set out a dollar each for Kalle and Jussi on top of what Toivo owed them.

Toivo put the coat on. The men stood back. He went to the piece of mirror over the washstand and looked at himself, and turned, and looked again, and ran his hand down the front of it, and could not stop. It was the finest thing he had ever had on his back. He looked, Jussi said, like a schoolmaster. He looked, Kalle said, like a man who owned a mine.

Tomorrow, he said, I’ll wear it to the dance at the hall.

He took it off and brushed it with his hand and hung it on the nail at the head of his bunk, where he could see it from his pillow.

He had eaten three pieces of the cake, and salt herring before that at supper, and some time after midnight the pain came in his belly, low on the right side. He did not wake anybody. The man in the other bunk was on the night shift. He lay on his side and looked at the coat on the nail in the light from the street, and drew his knees up, and in the morning when the night shift came in he was dead.

III. The Seam

The East End · the end of August

They buried him from the Finnish church with the boarders for pallbearers, and when it was over and the boarders had come back to the house Hilja came with them and went down on her knees on Mrs. Lehto’s kitchen floor, among the boots, and held on to Kalle Niemi’s ankles and wept, and rolled her head against the floor, and could not be got up for a long time. Mrs. Lehto put a pillow under her. The men stood around with their caps in their hands and looked at the stove.

Afterward, when Hilja’s husband had taken her home, the boarders sat around the table with the coffee, and the question of the coat came up, because it was hanging on the nail in the next room and every one of them had been thinking about it.

There was a custom in the boarding houses about a dead man’s clothes. If somebody had sat up with him at the end, the clothes were his. If nobody had, they belonged to the house, and the men drew lots. Nobody had sat up with Toivo. Hilja had said, on the way to the cemetery, that the boys should have it; he would want it worn. So the coat was the house’s. Only first, because it was the custom also, it would go in the sauna kettle on Saturday and be boiled, as every dead man’s clothes were, for the lice.

Boil that? said Kalle. You’ll ruin it. You’ll shrink it to fit a dog.

Then you can have it. You’re the size of a dog.

They laughed for the first time that day, and it was then, the way they all told it afterward, that the voice came out of the next room.

It was the smallest voice any of them had ever heard. It was thinner than a mosquito. It was in Finnish, in Toivo’s Ostrobothnian, and what it said was Thieves! Thieves! That’s mine, you’re robbing me, that’s mine, over and over, and the boarders looked at each other across the table and nobody moved.

Old Matti Hautala got up. He had been sitting by the stove, where he always sat. He went into the next room and stood in front of the coat on its nail and looked at it for a long time, close, the way he had once looked at iron in the fire to see what color it had come to. Then he came back.

Nobody touches that coat, he said. It stays on the nail seven days. Nobody boils it, nobody tries it on, nobody draws for it. Seven days.

Matti was seventy-one. He had been a blacksmith at the Jackson mine for thirty-one years, sharpening drill steel, until his eyes went, and he had been a red since the strike of 1913 and did not go to church, and he kept the sauna for Mrs. Lehto in exchange for his board. He did not talk much. When he did, the men did what he said, the Lutherans and the reds both, and nobody could have told you why.

Some of them said afterward that they had seen it, that if you went close to the coat on the nail you could see a louse running up and down along the inside of the seam of the left sleeve, this way and that way, and hear it. Others said they never saw a thing. Jussi Pelto, who had made the sleeve, said to the end of his life that he did not know what he had seen, and did not want to talk about it.

IV. The Moving

From the East End to Pine Street · the first week of September

The ground under the East End had been mined for eighty years, and it was going. There were cracks in the backyards you could put your arm in. The Cleveland-Cliffs Iron Company had bought the houses one after another all summer, and in August it asked the city to close the streets, and now the houses were going, the ones that could be moved, up the hill to new lots on the north side of town. Two of the Lutheran churches were going, one of them parsonage and all. Mrs. Lehto had sold to the company in July, and the movers had her house on the list for the first week in September.

So the seven days were the days of the moving, and the boarders packed. They packed the beds and the stove and the washstand and the piece of mirror and Mrs. Lehto’s dishes in barrels of straw, and the coat hung on its nail.

The movers came on the fourth day with timbers and jacks and a team of horses and a capstan, and they jacked the house up off its stone foundation and set it on cribbing and then on rollers, and it went up the hill a few yards an hour, creaking, with the horses walking round and round the capstan and the cable singing, and men running from the back of the house to the front with the rollers. Matti walked beside it the whole way. When it tipped going over the curb at Case Street the boarders could hear things fall inside, and Matti went in by the back door, which was a step up to his waist now, and came out again and said the coat was still on its nail. It swung. That was all.

Behind them, where the house had been, there was a hole full of old stones and broken crockery and a rhubarb plant, and the ground all around was the color of rust. On the East End all the dirt was that color. It came up out of the ground on everything, on the boots and the wash and the dogs and the children. You could not keep a white shirt white.

They set the house down on its new foundation on Pine Street on the evening of the seventh day.

V. The Spike

Pine Street · the eighth day

In the morning Matti went in and took the coat down off the nail and brought it out to the kitchen and laid it across the table.

Now, he said. Boil it, draw for it, do what you want.

They boiled it. It came out of the kettle a little smaller and none the worse, and they hung it to dry on the line in the new yard, and that evening they drew for it with burnt matches, and Arvo Salo, who was seventeen and had been in the country since June, drew the short one, and put it on, and it fit him.

Then they asked Matti. They sat around Mrs. Lehto’s table in the new kitchen, which was the same kitchen a quarter mile north and slightly crooked, so that the coffee in the cups did not sit level, and they asked him why seven days, and why the eighth.

Matti looked at his hands.

Toivo was in the coat, he said. You heard him. He went to sleep looking at it and he woke up in the seam. If you had boiled it the first day, he’d have gone out of the world cursing every one of you for a thief, and a man who goes out cursing goes down. So I let him run out his time. He’s gone up now. Now you can have it.

Nobody laughed. Arvo, who was wearing it, sat very still.

Matti got up and went out the back door and came back with something from the yard, where the movers had left a pile of rubbish: an old railroad spike, bent, crusted over with rust so thick it had swollen the iron and split it. He put it on the oilcloth among the cups.

You know where rust comes from? he said. Not from the rain. The rain only helps. It comes out of the iron. Nobody puts it on. It comes up out of the iron and eats the iron, until there’s no iron left, only the rust. He pushed the spike a little with one finger. Look at this town. They dug it out of the ground and the ground eats it. Look at that boy, God rest him. A coat, his sister’s coat. It came out of him, the wanting, and it ate him.

Then he said it the way he had learned it at confirmation school in Lapua sixty years before, from a pastor he hated, and had never forgotten, because it was the one place in the Bible the reds all knew by heart.

Come, you rich men, weep aloud and howl for your sorrows which will soon be upon you. Your treasures have rotted, and your piles of clothing are moth-eaten; your gold and your silver have become covered with rust, and the rust on them will give evidence against you, and will eat your flesh like fire.

— James 5:1–3 (Weymouth)

He wasn’t rich, said Arvo.

No, said Matti. He had one coat.

•••

Arvo wore it to the dance at the hall that Saturday, and to church, and to his wedding in 1934, and was buried in Hilja’s cloth in 1971, though by then it had been turned twice and was not the same coat. Hilja saw him in it, the first Sunday, across the church. She went over after the service and took the cuff of the left sleeve between her finger and thumb, and rubbed it, and felt the twill she had made, and let it go.

The spike stayed on the windowsill of Mrs. Lehto’s kitchen on Pine Street as long as she lived, and dropped a little ring of red on the white paint, which she scrubbed every spring and which every spring came back.

Background

The verse

As the impurity which springs from the iron, when it springs from it, destroys it; thus do a transgressor’s own works lead him to the evil path.Dhammapada 240, tr. F. Max Müller (1881)

In the commentary, the monk Tissa receives a length of coarse cloth and gives it to his sister, who secretly cuts it up, beats it to fiber, spins it fine and has it woven into finer, longer cloth. He refuses it until she tells him what she has done. Robe-makers sew it while she feeds them, and he folds the finished robe on the rack, meaning to wear it the next day, but dies in the night of indigestion and is reborn as a louse in the robe. When the monks, since no one nursed him, move to divide it, the louse runs up and down screaming that they are plundering his property. The Buddha has the robe set aside for seven days; the louse dies and is reborn in the Tusita heaven, and on the eighth day the monks divide it. Had they taken it while he still clung to it, the Buddha explains, he would have died hating them and gone to hell; craving eats the one it arises in, as rust eats the iron it comes from. The Marquette Iron Range, 1928

Iron ore was found near Negaunee in 1844, and the Jackson Mine there opened the Lake Superior iron country. By the 1920s the ground under the old town had been worked for generations, and roughly half of Negaunee was eventually abandoned for fear of cave-ins. In August 1928, the Mining Journal reported that the Cleveland-Cliffs Iron Company had bought most of the homes in the East End and was offering settlements; the city was asked to vacate and close several of its streets, and houses and churches, among them Immanuel and Bethany Lutheran, were moved north to new lots. Two years earlier, in November 1926, the Barnes-Hecker mine four miles west of Ishpeming had caved in and flooded, killing 51 men.

Finns came to the Upper Peninsula by the tens of thousands from the 1870s on, and many of the single men lived in boarding houses with a sauna in the yard. The Finns were divided between church people and “reds,” socialists and union men; the Finnish socialist paper Työmies, which had broken with its Lutheran backers, played a major role in the Copper Country strike of 1913–14 in the Keweenaw, to the north. Sarka is the coarse woolen homespun of the Finnish countryside. The verse comes from Richard Francis Weymouth’s New Testament in Modern Speech, first published in 1903.
