I. The Names

Camp Upton, Yaphank, Long Island · September 1917

First Sergeant Andrew Fedorko had the names in four days.

The company came down from the city on the Long Island Rail Road with tags on their coats, two hundred and fifty of them, and stood in the sand between the stumps while the carpenters were still nailing the roof on their barracks. There were bookkeepers and iceman's helpers, a pastry cook from the Hotel Astor, a Chinese laundryman's son from Mott Street, a boy who had sung in the choir at the Eldridge Street synagogue, two brothers who drove a moving van in Hell's Kitchen and fought each other every night until the Top separated them by a whole floor. Italians from Mulberry Bend and Italians from Bensonhurst who did not think of each other as the same thing. Irish from Chelsea. Swedes from Bay Ridge. Poles, Armenians, a Norwegian sailor who had jumped ship in 1912, an Albanian, three Greeks who ran a lunchroom together on Eighth Avenue and had been drafted on the same morning.

The other sergeants called the roll the way you push a wheelbarrow over roots. They stopped at every third name, swore, spelled it, guessed. Fedorko did not stop. He said Kowalczyk and Esposito and Der Bedrossian and Lindqvist and Moskowitz as if he had grown up next door to every one of them, and in a way he had.

He was thirty-eight that fall. He had come over from Šariš at four in the steerage of a ship he did not remember and grown up in a patch outside Shenandoah, in the hard coal, in a company house with a cow in the back and the Greek Catholic church on Chestnut Street at the end of the world. At nine he went into the breaker. He sat on a board over the chute all day picking slate out of the running coal, with Lithuanian boys on one side of him and Polish boys on the other and an Irish boss behind with a stick, and the names of everything in four languages went into him through the dust. At twenty he walked to Pottsville and enlisted. He had been in the Philippines, and in China for a summer, and on the Mexican border the year before. He had eighteen years in. The Army had sent him to Yaphank with a handful of other old Regulars to make soldiers out of New York, and he had a voice like a breaker whistle and a way of saying a man's name that made the man stand straighter.

The captain was a lawyer named Halpern, from Henry Street, who had gone through the officers' camp at Plattsburg and come out with two silver bars and a cough. He was thirty-four. He did not raise his voice, and he did not say much of anything. On the corner of his desk in the orderly room he kept a new black Bible, the translation the Jewish Publication Society had brought out that very year, and when the company clerk dusted the desk he dusted around it.

There was one name Fedorko did not need to learn, because nobody used it.

Jonas Kazlauskas was a cabinetmaker's helper from Williamsburg, twenty-two, short and fair and quiet, with a cowlick he wet down every morning and every morning lost. On his first day a corporal had looked at the tag on his coat and said, What is that, Cassidy? and Cassidy he became. He answered to it. He answered to Hey You and Blondie and the Swede, though he was not a Swede. He did his drill and cleaned his rifle and wrote his mother on Sundays at the Y.M.C.A. hut, in Lithuanian, and nobody in the company had ever heard him laugh out loud.

When the Top called the roll in the dark at a quarter to six, he said Kazlauskas like the rest, correctly, with the weight in the right place. But the roll was the roll. Kazlauskas waited for something else, without knowing exactly what it was.

II. Russian Christmas

The company street · Saturday, January 5, 1918

The winter came down on Long Island early and stayed. By Christmas the sand was frozen like cement and the wind off the Sound came through the board walls of the barracks as if there were no walls. In the city there was no coal. People stood in line with baby carriages to buy it by the pail. At Upton the stoves were red all night and the men slept in their overcoats anyway.

Fedorko had not taken a furlough in four years. On the first of January the captain called him into the orderly room and said, Your people keep Christmas on the seventh.

Yes, sir. The old calendar.

Go home for it.

The company, sir.

Go home, Sergeant.

That was a long speech, for Halpern.

So on the Saturday morning before the Holy Supper, Fedorko came out of the barracks in his overcoat with a barracks bag over his shoulder, to walk to the station and take the train to the city, and the ferry over to Jersey City, and the train from there into the coal country. In his bag he had a pound of honey from the canteen for his mother, a wool scarf for his brother's girl, and a German helmet spike a sergeant in the 305th had swapped him for a pocketknife, for his nephew.

He had meant to slip out. He did not get the chance. Somebody in the kitchen had told somebody, and by the time he came down the stairs the company was out on the frozen street in their overcoats, without being told, in no kind of order, to see him off.

He went down the line of them shaking hands. He did it the way he called the roll, every name, every man, with the weight in the right place. Esposito. Go easy on the sugar. Lindqvist. Shave. Moskowitz, if you write that girl one more poem she'll marry the iceman. They laughed and stamped their feet and their breath went up white. The Hell's Kitchen brothers stood together for once. The choirboy from Eldridge Street gave him a cake his mother had sent, wrapped in a newspaper, for the train.

Kazlauskas was at the back by the woodpile. He had been there first. He was in the second row, then the third, as men pushed in front of him, and by the time Fedorko reached his part of the street there were six big men between them, and Fedorko said Keogh and Russo and Der Bedrossian and then he was past, still shaking hands, still saying names, on toward the end of the street.

Kazlauskas thought, He said everybody's but mine.

He moved along the back of the crowd to get ahead of it, to the corner where the street met the road to the station, so that the Top would have to pass him there. He stood with his hands in his pockets. The Top came on, finishing up with the last of them, and turned the corner fast, because the train would not wait, swinging his barracks bag from one shoulder to the other as he turned, and the long end of his wool muffler, stiff with frost, flew out and caught Kazlauskas across the ear, not hard, the way a wet towel slaps, and the Top kept going down the road toward the station with his barracks bag on his shoulder and did not look back.

Kazlauskas stood there with his ear stinging.

He waited until the Top was a small brown figure going down past the base hospital. Then he went to the orderly room and knocked and stood at attention in front of the captain's desk.

Sir, I want to report the first sergeant.

The captain looked up.

He hit me, sir. Back there on the street, in front of everybody. On the side of my head. My ear's still ringing. He says, I'm the Top, and he hits me, and he walks off to the train without a word.

Halpern looked at him for a while. Then he said to the clerk, Abramowitz. Take the Ford. Go to the station and ask the first sergeant to come back.

III. The Keys

Barracks and mess hall · eleven o'clock

Corporal Sol Abramowitz, the company clerk, drove the Ford down to the station and found the Top on the platform with the cake under his arm, stamping his feet. He told him. The Top did not say anything. He picked up his bag and got in the Ford, and they drove back past the train coming in.

By then Sergeant Pat Keogh had heard it.

Keogh was the supply sergeant, a structural ironworker from Chelsea who had walked the beams of the Woolworth Building, six foot three, and he loved the Top the way a younger brother does who would never say so. He found Abramowitz in the orderly room. Abramowitz had the keys. He always had the keys. He knew where everything in the company was, every blanket and spoon and every man's next of kin, and he could tell you without looking what the Top had said to the colonel on the eleventh of October.

Cassidy says the Top hit him, said Keogh.

I know.

The Top never hit a man in his life that didn't hit him first.

I know.

The captain don't know the Top like we do. Keogh put out his hand for the keys. Get everybody in the mess hall. He's going to give it to that kid with both barrels, and I want them all there when he does.

So they went through the barracks, the two of them, upstairs and down, and the barracks next door where the company's overflow slept. Abramowitz unlocked the supply room and the drying room and the latrine door, which stuck. Keogh banged on the bunks with his fist. Mess hall! Everybody! Come on, come on, come on! The Top's back, and he's going to let Cassidy have it! You want to see something, come on! They pulled men out of the card games and out of the washroom with lather on their faces and out of their blankets, men who had been on guard all night, and they brought them all down the frozen street into the mess hall, two hundred and forty-some men in overcoats, and stood them in among the tables, and more sat on the tables, and the cooks came out of the kitchen with their arms folded.

The captain came in and sat at the end of the officers' table under the flag. Kazlauskas stood near him, white. The lieutenants stood. Then the door opened and the Top came in and took off his campaign hat and stood where the captain pointed.

It was so quiet you could hear the coffee tick in the urn.

Sergeant, the captain said. Private Kazlauskas says you struck him on the street this morning, on the head, and went on without a word. What do you say?

Keogh leaned forward on his table, waiting for it.

IV. Nine Things

The mess hall · noon

The Top looked at the floor a while. He did not look at Kazlauskas.

Sir, he said. A man who doesn't keep his own body in his eye, he might knock against another man and go off and never know it. Never say he was sorry. That could be.

He stopped. Then he went on, slowly, the way he talked on the rifle range when a man was afraid of his piece.

Sir, this ground here. They dig latrines in it. They bury the garbage in it. They plant the flagpole in it and they bury the dead in it. The ground doesn't care. It doesn't say, this is clean, I like it, this is dirty, I don't. I try to be like that.

Water. The wash rack. Same water washes the officers' cups and the grease trap. It doesn't mind.

The stove. You put in coal, you put in letters, you put in rags with lice in them. It burns them. It doesn't ask which.

The wind off the Sound. It blows on the colonel's quarters and it blows on the latrines. It doesn't pick.

The kitchen rag. He nodded at the cooks. Russo wipes the captain's table with it, he wipes up what the dog did. The rag doesn't get proud. Doesn't get ashamed. I try to be like the rag.

Somebody near the kitchen door made a small sound.

When I was a kid, the Top said, in the breaker, we used to go up into the English town on Saturday, the Welsh town. Up on the hill. We were Hunkies. You know what that means, some of you. A Hunky kid goes up that hill with his cap in his hand, and he walks small, and he don't ask for nothing, and he don't look at nobody, and he goes home. I try to walk like that kid. All the time. Even now. With these. He touched the diamond on his sleeve. Especially now.

At home we had a cow that the old man sawed the horns off. She'd walk up and down the patch, in and out of every yard, and the little kids would hang on her and she never hooked one of them. I try to be like that cow.

He was quiet a little. Then he said:

Sir, you know how some fellows, they're particular. They take a bath every day, they put on the hair oil, they want their shirt just so. You hang a dead dog around a fellow like that's neck, that's been in the road three days, he'll go crazy trying to get it off. That's how I feel about this, mostly. He touched his own chest with two fingers. This body. It's a dead dog they tied on me. I carry it because I got to.

And I carry it careful. Like the old man's dinner pail, when it rusted out. It had holes in the bottom. The lard came out the holes. He carried it down the shaft anyway because he didn't have another, and he carried it careful, both hands, so as not to lose any more than he had to. That's me, sir. A pail with holes in it. Carrying it careful. A man like that, sir, how is he going to go and hit somebody?

That was all he said.

The men who were there told it afterward in different ways, and in some of the ways there were more words, and in some fewer. But all of them told it the same about the floor. While he spoke, the floor of the mess hall shook. It shuddered up from under, from the sand, the way it had in the fall when the engineers were blowing stumps. It shook nine times, once at the end of each thing he said, and nine times the coffee in the big urn rang like a bell, and nine times the stovepipe ticked in its collar, and one of the cooks put his hand flat on the wall. There was no blasting that day. It was a Saturday, and the ground was frozen two feet down, and the engineers were all in Patchogue at the pictures.

When he got to the Hunky kid with his cap in his hand, the pastry cook from the Astor started crying and could not stop. When he got to the rag, two of the Italians did, and Lindqvist, who had never been seen to have a feeling of any kind. At the pail with holes in it a good part of the company was crying, the way men cry who have not done it in a long time, with their shoulders, looking at the floor. Keogh was not crying. Keogh sat on his table with his big hands open on his knees and his mouth a little open, and he had the look on his face that he had told Abramowitz once a man gets on a beam forty floors up when he looks down for the first time and finds out he isn't afraid.

V. The Floor

The mess hall · afternoon

Jonas Kazlauskas stood by the officers' table. All the time the Top was talking, he felt it come up through him from his feet, the way the shudder came up through the floor. It came up into his knees and his stomach and his chest and into his face, where the ear had stopped stinging an hour ago, and when the Top said a pail with holes in it it reached the top of his head, and he went down on the floor in front of the captain's table on both knees.

He never hit me, he said. Sir. He never touched me. It was his muffler. It was the end of his muffler in the wind. He didn't say my name. That's all. Everybody's but mine. I made it up. I'm a liar, sir. Put me in the guardhouse.

Nobody moved.

The captain looked down at him a long moment. Then he looked at the Top and said, quietly, in the voice he used for everything:

Sergeant. Let him off. Before his head comes apart.

And the Top came across the mess hall floor between the tables. Men moved their feet out of his way. He came to where Kazlauskas was kneeling, and he did not stand over him. He went down on his heels in front of him, the way men squat at the mouth of a mine waiting for the cage, so that their faces were on a level. He put his two hands together in front of him with the palms flat, the way the old women did at the Greek Catholic church on Chestnut Street when the priest came out with the cup, and held them out toward the boy.

Kazlauskas, he said, with the weight in the right place. Jonas. It's nothing. I let you off. Now you let me off. If I did anything to you, any time, that I don't know about, you let me off for it. All right?

Kazlauskas looked at the two flat hands in front of his face. He did not know what to do with them. After a while he put his own hands around them, and they stayed like that, the two of them on the mess hall floor, and the coffee urn ticked.

Afterward, at the tables, the men could not leave it alone. Did you see that? The kid lies on him in front of the whole company and he gets down on the floor and asks the kid's pardon. I'd have broke his neck. My old man would've broke his neck and then asked the priest. There's nothing in him. You can't get a hook into him. There's no place to hang it.

Halpern had not left. He stood up at the end of the officers' table, and the talk stopped.

What are you all talking about?

They told him.

He nodded slowly, as if it were a case he had already read.

He can't hold it, the captain said. Not men like him. There's nothing in him to hold it with. He's like the ground out there. Like the sill of that door, that every one of you walks over twenty times a day. Like the lake down at Yaphank where the river comes up out of the sand, that you can see the bottom of in January.

He picked up the black Bible from the table, where he had carried it in, and he did not open it. He knew it.

They that trust in the LORD
Are as mount Zion, which cannot be moved, but abideth for ever.

— Psalm 125:1

Then he looked at his watch and said, Sergeant Fedorko, there's a train at four-ten, and went back to the orderly room.

That evening Abramowitz, sweeping up, found the captain's Bible left open on the desk at a page near the back, with a pencil line in the margin beside three verses. He read them standing up, with the broom in his other hand. He had been raised on the Hebrew and had not thought much about what it said in English.

Let him sit alone and keep silence,
Because He hath laid it upon him.
Let him put his mouth in the dust,
If so be there may be hope.
Let him give his cheek to him that smiteth him,
Let him be filled full with reproach.

— Lamentations 3:28–30

He closed the book and put it back square on the corner of the desk, and dusted around it.

VI. Holy Supper

Shenandoah, Pennsylvania · the night of January 6

He missed the Saturday connections at Jersey City and slept on a bench in the terminal at Jersey City and came up into the coal country on Sunday afternoon on the slow train, through Tamaqua and Mahanoy, past the black hills of culm with smoke coming out of their sides where they had been burning for years, past the breakers standing up over the patches like churches, with their windows lit.

There was coal here. There was coal everywhere, in heaps along the tracks, in the yards, in the gutters. In New York people were standing in line with baby carriages, and here the children were throwing it at each other.

He got off at Shenandoah after dark and walked out to the patch with his bag. The snow was grey with dust. It was the old Christmas Eve, and the first star was out, and in every company house along the row there was one candle in the window.

His mother had set the table and waited and given him up and set his place anyway. That was the custom. You set a place for the ones who were not there, the dead and the far away and the stranger who might come. There was straw under the tablecloth, from the cow's shed, and a loaf on the straw and a bowl of honey and a head of garlic. There was the mushroom soup and the sauerkraut and the little dough balls with poppy seed and honey, and the bobal'ki had gone cold, and his brother had eaten his share of the pirohy and said so.

His mother looked at him a long time in the doorway. She was small and bent and her hands were black in the creases, from fifty years of the dust, though she scrubbed them every day of her life.

Christos raždajetsja, she said.

Slavite Jeho.

She dipped a clove of garlic in the honey and made the cross on his forehead with it, the way she had done every Christmas Eve when he was a boy coming home from the breaker, and it was sticky and smelled of garlic and he stood still for it, bent down, with his hat in his hands.

You're late, she said. What kept you?

He thought about it.

A boy in the company, he said. I forgot to say goodbye to him.

He took off his overcoat and his muffler and hung them on the nail by the door. The end of the muffler was still stiff with the Long Island frost, and as it warmed in the kitchen it began, very slowly, to drip on the floor.

VII. The Sill

Cherry Grove, Fire Island · 1938

They went to France in April. Of what happened there to the company, to the Top and Keogh and Abramowitz and the Hell's Kitchen brothers and the pastry cook and the captain with the cough, some of it is written down in the regimental history and some of it is not written anywhere. Jonas Kazlauskas came home in the spring of 1919 with a piece of a German shell in his hip that the doctors said to leave where it was. He went back to cabinetmaking. He married a girl from his own church in Williamsburg and they had four children, and the first boy they named Andrew, and when people asked why, he said it was after a man he knew in the Army, and did not say any more.

The war ended, and the Army sold off the camp at Yaphank, the buildings and the stoves and the bunks. Some of the barracks were knocked down and carried across the bay to Fire Island, and the boards went into the first cottages at Cherry Grove, where people from the city came for the summer to swim and be left alone.

In the summer of 1938, a few weeks before the hurricane, Kazlauskas was hired to hang new doors on one of those cottages, and the old sill he took out from under the kitchen door was a length of yellow pine with a number stenciled on the underside in Army paint. He turned it over in his hands. Along the top, a little left of the middle, where a man coming out a door would stand still for a moment and plant himself, the grain was worn down into a smooth hollow, the size and shape of a right shoe.

He did not throw it out. He sanded the rest of it down and oiled it and put it back under the new door, the hollow and all, and the woman who owned the cottage asked him why he had not planed it flat.

People should walk on it, he said.

Background

The verse

Such a one who does his duty is tolerant like the earth, like Indra's bolt; he is like a lake without mud; no new births are in store for him.Dhammapada 95, tr. F. Max Müller (1881)

In the commentary, the Elder Sāriputta, the Buddha's chief disciple, takes leave of the monks at the end of the rains and greets each by his personal and family name, but in the crowd he overlooks one obscure monk, and the hem of his robe brushes against him. The monk tells the Buddha that Sāriputta struck him and went off without asking pardon. Moggallāna and Ānanda, sure their elder brother will "roar a lion's roar," go from cell to cell with the keys and call the whole community together. Sāriputta does not deny the charge. He describes his own mind in nine plain similes (the earth, water, fire and wind that take clean and unclean alike, a dust-rag, an outcaste boy entering a village, a bull with its horns cut, a body felt like a carcass hung round the neck, and a body carried like a leaking pot of fat), and the earth shakes nine times. The accuser confesses; Sāriputta crouches before him with joined hands and asks his pardon in return; and the Buddha says his mind is like the earth, a threshold-post and a still pool. The similes are those of the Sīhanāda Sutta, Anguttara Nikāya 9.11. Read the full commentary story, or about Sāriputta.

Camp Upton, 1917–18

Camp Upton was built in the scrub-oak barrens near Yaphank in the summer of 1917, and the first drafted men arrived in September, when the camp was far from finished; they helped build it, while engineers blasted the stumps from the drill grounds. The 77th Division organized there was made of draftees from New York City, men of some twenty-five nationalities, and it took the Statue of Liberty for its shoulder patch. Experienced Regular Army officers and noncommissioned officers were sent to the new National Army camps to train them. One company's own account, Company B, 307th Infantry (1920), describes two-story barracks with kitchen and mess hall below and bunks above, straw mattresses, and drill, drill, drill. The division paraded down Fifth Avenue on Washington's Birthday, 1918, and sailed for France that April. After the war many of the camp's buildings were taken across the bay to become the first cottages of Cherry Grove on Fire Island.

The winter of 1917–18 brought a coal famine in the eastern cities, and the Fuel Administration answered with price controls, shortened factory weeks and "heatless Mondays." Most of that coal came from the anthracite fields of eastern Pennsylvania, where tens of thousands of Carpatho-Rusyn, Slovak, Polish and Lithuanian immigrants worked, and where boys as young as eight or nine sat over the chutes as breaker boys, picking slate from the coal. St. Michael's in Shenandoah, founded in 1884, was the first Greek Catholic church in America. Its people kept Christmas by the old Julian calendar, on January 7, and began it the evening before with the meatless Holy Supper, straw under the tablecloth and a cross of honey on each forehead. Captain Halpern's Bible is the Jewish Publication Society translation, which first appeared in 1917.
