Walking the American WayCommentary 9.12 · Suppabuddha

The Seventh Floor

Though they dig into the nether-world, thence shall My hand take them; and though they climb up to heaven, thence will I bring them down.
— Amos 9:2

Näin olen kuullut, in Finntown.

I. The Watch

Butte, Montana · 1917

Ferdinand Kahn had come to Butte in 1881 with a peddler's pack and a pair of boots that let in water, and by 1917 he owned one of the tallest buildings in Butte. The Kahn Block stood seven stories high on West Park Street, red brick and terra cotta, with his name cut into the stone over the door and a light court in the middle that went straight up through every floor to a roof of glass, so that on a clear day a woman buying gloves on the main floor could look up through six galleries of iron railing and see the sky. Kahn's sold silk from Lyon and pianos from Chicago and men's suits that came up from the basement tailors in two days. Kahn himself kept his office on the seventh floor, and from his window he could look across the whole of the Hill, the gallows frames and the smokestacks and the ore dumps and the shacks, and he liked to say that he could see every dollar in Butte from there, and that most of them were on their way to him.

He had a son, Julius, and a daughter, Sophie. In 1912 Sophie married a young doctor from Chicago named Nathan Brill, whose father had been a tinsmith in Kovno and whose mother still could not write her name. Kahn had not liked it. He had given them a house on West Granite Street anyway, and an office for Nathan on the fifth floor of the Kahn Block with his name in gold leaf on the frosted glass, and in two years Nathan had the best practice uptown. He set the bones of mine superintendents and delivered the babies of their wives. In 1914 he delivered his own son, Benjamin, in the house on Granite Street, because he would not trust anybody else to do it.

On the eighth of June, 1917, the Granite Mountain shaft caught fire more than two thousand feet underground, and the smoke and the gas went out through the tunnels into the Speculator and the mines beyond it, and Dr. Brill went up the hill with every other doctor in Butte and stood at the collar of the shaft for three days.

He saw the old men first, the ones who had been mining forty years and knew enough to wall themselves into a drift with their own coats and wait. Some of them came up alive. He saw the young ones who had run. He saw the men who were dying already before the fire, with the miners' consumption in them from the rock dust, who came up coughing black and would have been dead in two years anyway, and who knew it, and who thanked him. Then the cages started bringing up the others. He saw them laid out on the ground in rows, more every day. He knew some of their faces from the street.

He went back to the office on the fifth floor in July and sat at the desk and could not do it. The superintendents' bones. Their wives' nerves. The gold leaf on the glass. In October, on a Thursday night, he took off his gold watch and set it on the dresser beside the bed where his wife and son were sleeping, and stood a while and looked at them, and picked up his bag and walked down the hill in the dark to the Cabbage Patch, east of Wyoming Street, where two hundred shacks leaned together between Mercury and Silver with an open sewer running down the middle and three miners to a bed, turn and turn about, one for each shift.

He took a shack on Mercury Street with a stove and a table and a cot, and put a card in the window that said DOCTOR in English and in Finnish and in Serbian, which a Finn named Matti Lahti wrote out for him. He did not charge. He ate wherever he was asked to supper, and when nobody asked he did not eat. Within a year the whole east side called him Doc, and the west side did not call him anything, because on the west side his name was not said.

Ferdinand Kahn never spoke it again. When he had to, he said that man.

II. Julius

Mercury Street · 1918–1919

The son came down after him. That was the part the father could least forgive.

Julius Kahn was twenty-six and had been to Harvard for a year and had come home without saying why. In the spring of 1918 he walked down the hill one Sunday to see what his sister's husband was doing in the Cabbage Patch, and he stayed. He swept the floor. He boiled the instruments. He carried the bag when Matti was too sick to carry it. Through the winter of the influenza he worked beside Doc for nineteen hours a day, and the women of the Patch said he was nearly as good as Doc himself, and Julius heard them say it.

He was quick, and he had ideas. A real dispensary, brick, with beds. A ward for the consumptives. His father's money. His own name over the door, since his father's name was already over enough doors. Doc listened to all of it and said no. He said it gently, the way he said everything. He said the Patch did not need a building with anybody's name on it. It needed somebody to come when it called.

Julius did not take it gently. He went back up the hill in January 1919, and in February, when the company cut the wage by a dollar a day and the men walked out and the soldiers came in with bayonets, a good many of the names on the strike committee turned out to be names from the dispensary's ledger, men who had talked in their fevers, men who had told Doc what they thought of the company with a thermometer in their mouths. Their rustling cards were pulled. Without a rustling card no man could work a shift in any mine on the Hill. Some of them went to Bisbee. Some went to Seattle. Some stayed in the Patch and drank.

Doc never said one word against Julius Kahn. He said nothing at all, which was worse, because it was talked about.

Ferdinand Kahn sat in his office on the seventh floor and added it up. That man had taken his daughter and left her. That man had taken his son and made him into whatever he was now. Every time Kahn went up West Granite Street in his Pierce-Arrow and saw his grandson playing in the yard, the boy had that man's face.

III. The Street

North Main Street · a Friday in July, 1919

The state had gone dry at the end of December. Butte had not noticed. There were stills in kitchens all over the Hill and in half the cellars uptown, and in the Patch a man called the King sold whiskey out of a washtub at fifty cents a pint, and nobody in the city government drank any less than before, only more quietly.

Ferdinand Kahn drank on North Main Street, in the middle of the afternoon, sitting on a kitchen chair in the middle of the road.

He had found out from somebody that Doc was going to supper that Friday with the Trevorrows, a Cornish family on North Main whose youngest he had pulled through the influenza, and he had his driver bring the Pierce-Arrow up the hill and stop it crossways, and he had the driver carry out a chair from the car, and he sat in it with a fruit jar of the King's whiskey on his knee and his hat tipped back and waited. Ore wagons went around him. A streetcar stopped and rang its bell and could not go around him and stopped. Men came out of the saloons that were now called soft-drink parlors and stood in the doors to watch.

Doc came up the hill at half past five with Matti Lahti carrying the bag. Matti saw the chair first.

Mr. Kahn, said Matti, from twenty feet off. It's the doctor coming.

Tell him to go around.

Mr. Kahn. He's going to supper.

Tell him to go around. He's no better than I am. I don't step off the street for him.

Matti said it a third time, and the old man said it a third time, louder, so that the men in the doorways heard it and the motorman on the streetcar heard it, and he drank from the jar and wiped his mouth with the back of his hand and did not look at his son-in-law at all.

Doc looked at him for some while. Then he touched Matti's arm and turned around and went back down the hill the way he had come.

Kahn crooked a finger at a man in a grey suit who had been standing on the corner by the cigar store all afternoon. His name was Dunphy and he was a detective, the kind the company kept, and Kahn had kept him privately since February at a rate that Dunphy did not report to the company. Go after him, said Kahn. Hear what he says.

Dunphy went after him, a block behind, and then half a block, and then near enough.

Partway down North Main, Doc smiled. Matti saw it. In two years he had seen Doc smile perhaps a dozen times, and never at anything that anybody else thought was funny.

Why are you smiling, Doc?

Look at him, Matti.

Matti looked back up the hill, at the motorcar crossways in the road and the old man in the chair with the jar.

I see him.

He has done a hard thing today, said Doc. Seven days from now, on the ground floor of his building, at the foot of the stairs, the ground is going to open and take him.

He said it the way he said that a lung was clear or that a leg would have to come off, without any weight on it at all, and he went on down the hill to the Patch.

IV. The Seventh Floor

The Kahn Block, West Park Street · the same night

Dunphy told it to Kahn word for word in the office on the seventh floor, standing, with his hat in his hands.

Kahn did not laugh. He had known that man for seven years. He had seen what that man's diagnoses were worth uptown. When that man said a superintendent had a year, the superintendent had a year, to the week. When that man said a child would live, the child lived, and when he said it would not, the mother might as well go and see the undertaker. Kahn knew all this, and so he did not laugh. He sat in his leather chair and thought about it with his fingertips together, the way he had once thought about a shipment of Irish linen that was coming by way of Panama.

He didn't say seven days, he said at last. He said seven days, on the ground floor, at the foot of the stairs. Those were his words?

Those were his words.

Then I won't be on the ground floor, said Ferdinand Kahn. And I won't be at the foot of the stairs. And in a week that man is a liar in front of the whole town.

The next morning there was a card in the windows of Kahn's that said CLOSED FOR INVENTORY. By noon the brass bed from his house on Park Street had come up in the freight elevator to the seventh floor, and his Turkish carpet, and his Victrola, and his account books back to 1881, and a crate of bonded whiskey from before the war, and his wife's portrait, and a commode. Then he had the elevator man run the car up to seven and shut off the current and chain the gate. He had the carpenters come in on Saturday afternoon and take out the lowest flight of the grand staircase, the broad one of polished oak that went up from the main floor to the mezzanine, so that it ended in the air ten feet over the marble with nothing under it. He had the doors to the back stairs barred on every floor with iron straps through the handles. And he hired fourteen men, two for every floor, big men, and set them at the doors with chairs and a deck of cards and a jug of water, and paid them five dollars a day in advance, which was more than the company was paying underground.

They were miners, all fourteen. They were men who had lost their rustling cards in February. He did not know that. He had not asked their names.

If I forget myself, he told them, and start down, you put me back. I don't care what I say. You put me back.

Then he sat down in the leather chair by the window on the seventh floor, with the Hill spread out under him, the headframes and the dumps and the smoke going straight up in the still July air, and poured a glass of the bonded whiskey, and waited for the week to be over.

V. The Dispensary

Mercury Street · Sunday night

By Sunday the whole town had it. They had it in Dublin Gulch and in Meaderville and in Finntown, in the boardinghouses and the soft-drink parlors and the cribs on Venus Alley. There were bets in the cigar stores on Main Street, three to one against Kahn, and the odds got shorter every day.

The dispensary was full that night, as it was full every night. Men sat on the bench along the wall waiting to be listened to with the stethoscope, holding their caps, with the cough in them that came up out of the deep levels with the rock dust and never went back down. A Serbian woman sat on the floor with a baby with croup. An Irish widow from Corktown was there with her boy's broken arm. Two of the fourteen men from the Kahn Block were there, off shift, because they had been coming to Doc for their lungs since before February and saw no reason to stop. One of them told it. The iron straps. The stairs cut off ten feet up in the air. The elevator chained at seven.

Somebody laughed. Doc did not. He was listening to the Serbian baby's chest, with his head bent down close, and when he had finished listening he wrapped the baby up again and handed it back and said what to do with the kettle and the towel, and then he sat back on his stool and looked at the bench along the wall.

Let him go up seven floors, he said. Let him go up in an aeroplane. Let him get on a ship at Seattle and go out to sea. Let him go down the Speculator to the bottom level and wall himself in. There's no place a man can go where it can't find him. There isn't any such place.

Nobody said anything. The Serbian baby coughed.

Doc did not talk about religion. Nobody in the Patch had ever heard him say a prayer, though Matti said he lit a candle on Friday nights when he remembered, and stood looking at it for a long time. But he said this next thing in the voice of a man reading, and Matti said afterward that there was a Bible on the shelf over the cot, the new English one the Jewish Publication Society had brought out two years before, which Doc's mother had sent him from Chicago though she could not read it, and that it had been open that week to the herdsman Amos, who had said he was no prophet and no prophet's son.

Though they dig into the nether-world, thence shall My hand take them; and though they climb up to heaven, thence will I bring them down. And though they hide themselves in the top of Carmel, I will search and take them out thence; and though they be hid from My sight in the bottom of the sea, thence will I command the serpent, and he shall bite them.

— Amos 9:2–3

Then he called the next man on the bench.

The people who were in the dispensary that night did not forget it, and some of them were not the same afterward. A Cornish timberman named Pascoe, who had been drinking his pay at the King's washtub since February, went home that night and poured out what he had in the house and never bought from the King again. The Irish widow went to confession for the first time in eleven years. One of the fourteen men went back to the Kahn Block in the morning and sat down at his door on the fourth floor and took out the cards, and did not deal them, and sat the whole day with his hands still in his lap, looking down the light court at the marble floor, as if he were waiting for something that had already happened.

VI. The General

The Kahn Block · the seventh day

The week went by on the seventh floor. Kahn read his account books. He played the Victrola, Caruso and then Sousa and then Caruso again. He drank less of the bonded whiskey than he had meant to, and slept badly in the brass bed, and in the mornings he stood at the iron railing of the light court and looked down six floors at the main floor of his store, at the glass counters under their dust sheets and the oak staircase ending in the air, and counted the days. Sophie came once, on the Wednesday, and stood across the street on the sidewalk with the boy and looked up at the seventh floor windows. He did not go to the window. The guards played pinochle on the landings. Down below in the alley, in the stable behind the store where the last of the delivery horses were kept, his saddle horse stood in its box stall and was groomed twice a day and walked in the alley in the evening.

The horse was a tall dapple grey, seventeen hands, a Kentucky saddler that Kahn had bought in Louisville in 1910 and ridden at the head of every Fourth of July parade since. It had been called Kaiser until April of 1917, when it was no longer wise to call anything in Butte by that name, and then Kahn had renamed it the General, and the newspaper had made a joke of it, and Kahn had liked the joke.

On the afternoon of the seventh day, a Friday, at about four o'clock, a Ford truck backfired in the alley, and the General kicked out the door of his box stall and came through the freight entrance into the back of the store and onto the main floor.

The noise came up the light court like a rockslide in a mine. Glass. Iron. A sound like a gong where the General kicked the brass rail of the hosiery counter, and then the counter itself, and then another one. A guard on the third floor shouted. The guards on the seventh floor got up out of their chairs.

What is that, said Kahn. What is it?

It's your horse, Mr. Kahn. The General's loose on the main floor.

Kahn went to the railing of the light court and looked down. Six floors down, in the slant afternoon light from the glass roof, the big grey horse was turning in circles among the broken counters with its eyes white and its shod hooves sliding on the marble, and the silk scarves from the ladies' counter were blowing up around it in the wind it made like leaves. Then it raised its head and looked straight up the light court, through six floors of railing, at Ferdinand Kahn, and stood still.

It stood perfectly still. Its sides went in and out. It looked at him.

Kahn had raised that horse from a four-year-old. Nobody else had ever been able to catch it when it got loose, nobody in thirty years, only him. He said its name. He got up off the railing and went toward the door to the back stairs to go down and catch it, without thinking, the way you go to a child that has fallen.

The iron straps through the door handles fell out of their own accord and rang on the floor. The door swung open by itself. The two men at the door of the seventh floor, who had been paid five dollars a day in advance to put him back, took him by the collar of his coat, one on each side, and threw him down the stairs.

On the sixth floor the door was standing open when he got there, and the straps were on the floor, and the two men there took him by the neck and threw him down. On the fifth floor, the same. On the fourth floor the man who had sat all day with his hands in his lap stood up and did what the others did. On the third, the second, the mezzanine. And at the mezzanine the lowest flight of the grand staircase, the broad flight of polished oak the carpenters had sawed out and carried off on Saturday afternoon, was back in its place, every step of it, with the brass rods on the carpet, and he came down it all the way.

He landed on the marble at the foot of the stairs, on the ground floor, ten feet from his horse.

The floor opened under him. The witnesses did not agree afterward on much, but they all agreed that it did not crack or cave the way ground caves on the Hill, with a groan first and then dust. It opened, the way a mouth opens, the marble and the joists and the cellar under them and whatever lay under that, and Ferdinand Kahn went down into it alive, without a sound, and it closed.

VII. The Rope

West Park Street · after

It did not close all the way. It left a hole at the foot of the grand staircase about the size of a grave, square-edged, with the cut ends of the marble showing white. The company sent engineers that evening. They lowered a lantern on a rope, a hundred feet and then two hundred, and the lantern went down past rock and old timbering and came to nothing they could see, and they pulled it up again. They said it must be an old working, a drift off some claim from the eighties that was never mapped, and that the whole Hill was hollow under Butte like a honeycomb and everybody knew it, and they wrote that down.

The old men who kept a minyan in a back room on East Galena Street, who had never liked Ferdinand Kahn and had never pretended to, said that the earth had opened its mouth for Korah too, in the wilderness, and that Korah had also been a rich man, and had also had a great deal to say about who should step aside for whom.

The fourteen men collected their five dollars for the seventh day and went back to the Patch. None of them would ever talk about it, except the one from the fourth floor, who said only that the door had opened and he had done what he was there to do.

Doc was in the dispensary on Mercury Street when Matti came to tell him. He was lancing a boil on a Finnish mucker's neck. He did not look up for some time. When he did, he said all right, Matti, and finished the boil, and wrote something in the ledger, and called the next man.

Sophie Brill came down to the store at dusk. The engineers had gone. There was nobody on the main floor but the night watchman and the horse. The General was standing where he had stood when he looked up the light court, at the edge of the hole, in the broken glass and the blown silk, with his head low. His halter rope had come loose from its ring and was hanging down over the cut edge of the marble into the dark, and he was standing very still above it, as a horse will stand when it has been told to wait.

The verse

Not in the sky, not in the midst of the sea, not if we enter into the clefts of the mountains, is there known a spot in the whole world where death could not overcome (the mortal).Dhammapada 128 (numbered 127 in the Project Gutenberg text), tr. F. Max Müller (1881)

In the commentary, the Sākyan noble Suppabuddha holds a grudge against the Buddha, who left Suppabuddha's daughter to go forth and later fell out with Suppabuddha's son, Devadatta. One day Suppabuddha sits drinking in the street to block the Buddha's way to a meal, and will not make way however many times he is asked. The Buddha turns back and smiles, and tells Ānanda that in seven days Suppabuddha will be swallowed by the earth at the foot of the stairway on the ground floor of his palace. Hearing this from a spy, Suppabuddha moves to the top of his seven-storied palace, has the stairway taken away and the doors barred, and sets strong men at every door with orders to turn him back. On the seventh day his state horse breaks loose below; he rises to catch it, the doors open, the stairways return, his own guards throw him down from floor to floor, and at the foot of the stairs the earth opens and takes him. Butte, 1917–1919

By the First World War, Butte sat on top of one of the richest copper deposits in the world, and most of its mines belonged to the Anaconda Company. On June 8, 1917, a fire in the Granite Mountain shaft spread through the connected tunnels of the Speculator Mine and killed 168 men, most of them suffocated; a few survived by sealing themselves behind makeshift bulkheads. To work at all, a miner needed a company-issued rustling card, which could be revoked at any time and served as a blacklist against union men. When copper prices fell after the war, the mine owners cut wages by a dollar a day, the miners struck in February 1919, and federal troops kept the city under occupation until the end of 1920. Montana's statewide prohibition took effect at the end of 1918, and Butte, which had voted against it, went on drinking out of kitchen stills.

Butte's Jewish community dated to 1875. Its merchants built some of the city's leading stores, among them Symons' department store on West Park Street, and its Reform temple, B'nai Israel, was dedicated in 1904 at Washington and Galena Streets. A few blocks east lay the Cabbage Patch, more than two hundred shanties between Mercury and Silver Streets, with no electricity or running water, where miners sometimes shared a bed in turns by shift. Underneath all of it the Hill was cut through with tunnels, and later in the century the ground over the old workings sank by as much as twelve feet across central Butte.
