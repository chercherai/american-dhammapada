I. The Cards

Rochester, New York · spring 2015

Hollis Tate went live at half past four every morning from the floor of her kitchen on Jefferson Avenue. She propped the phone against two old phone books by the baseboard, turned on the little light over the stove and nothing else, sat down on a folded blanket with her back against the refrigerator, and closed her eyes. That was the broadcast. At six she opened them, read a few of the comments that had piled up on the left side of the screen, and went to work folding hospital sheets at a commercial laundry off Lyell Avenue.

She had started the week the app came out, in March, when the whole internet was pointing its phones at things to see who would watch. The title of every broadcast was the same. SIT WITH ME. NOBODY HAS TO TALK. The first morning four people watched for a minute each. By June it was two or three hundred, from Manila and Leeds and Tulsa and a truck stop outside Gallup, and some of them stayed the whole ninety minutes.

There was no sound. Hollis had been Deaf since birth and had gone to the school for the deaf on St. Paul Street from the age of four, and it had not occurred to her to talk into the thing. People wrote to ask whether their phones were broken. After a while they stopped asking, and some of them wrote that it was the only quiet they got all day, and that in the silence of her kitchen they could hear their own rooms: a refrigerator, a dog's tags, a man coughing through a wall, rain. Hollis could feel the refrigerator hum along her spine. That was all she knew about it.

When people liked a thing on that app they tapped the screen, and little colored hearts rose up the right-hand side of the picture and wobbled and faded at the top. During her sits the hearts went up the edge of her kitchen all the time, pink and green and yellow, a slow column of them past the stove light and the shelf with the salt and the small white figure that people kept asking about. With her eyes shut she never saw them.

Sometimes, at six, she answered a comment by writing on an index card with a black marker and holding it up to the lens. She did not answer many. YES. DRINK SOME WATER. I DON’T KNOW. Once, when someone in Ohio wrote that she was lonely, a card that took her a while to letter, copied out of a library copy of Walden she had kept since high school and never returned: I NEVER FOUND THE COMPANION THAT WAS SO COMPANIONABLE AS SOLITUDE. About the white figure on the shelf, after the fortieth time somebody asked: A FRIEND GAVE IT TO ME. SHE HEARS EVERYTHING.

•••

Mateo Arroyo found her at 4:40 one morning in April in the parking lot of a distribution center on the mountain above Pocono Summit, on his last break of a ten-hour night. He was twenty-six. He picked orders with a scanner gun strapped to his wrist that told him how many seconds he had to reach each bin, and he had been doing it for three years, and the gun had never once said he was fast enough. He was thumbing through whoever was live, a girl doing her makeup in Brazil, a man feeding geese, a car crash, when he hit a dark kitchen with a woman sitting in it, and nothing happening, and no sound.

He watched until his break was over. He went back to the floor and picked till half past four and then drove home to the room he rented in East Stroudsburg and lay on the bed with the phone on his chest and watched her until she opened her eyes.

After that he sat with her most mornings, in his car at first, then on the floor of his room with his back against the bed the way she did it. He did not know why. He had been raised on Sixth Street in Allentown by a grandmother with a shelf of santos and a mother who worked doubles at the hospital, and he had stopped going to Mass at fourteen, and nothing in his life had been quiet since. In the evenings there was beer, and girls from the warehouse, and the phone. In the morning there was the kitchen in Rochester, and he found he could stand it for longer each time.

In July he typed into the comments, with his thumbs shaking a little, what should i sit with. He had read somewhere that teachers gave you a thing.

At six she opened her eyes and scrolled, and stopped, and he saw her look straight down the lens for the first time. She took a card and wrote on it slowly, and held it up.

GO SIT WHERE NOBODY WANTS TO BE. SEE WHAT YOU WANT.

He took a picture of the screen. It was still on his phone a year later, when the phone was in a drawer and he was somewhere else.

II. The Bell Pool

Analomink, Monroe County, Pennsylvania · August 2015

Everybody in Stroud Township knew where nobody wanted to be. Penn Hills had been the best honeymoon resort on Route 447 once, a hundred rooms on five hundred acres, with a ski slope and a golf course and an ice rink and a tiki lounge, and heart-shaped bathtubs in every villa. It had run downhill for thirty years, and in the spring of 2009 the last of its founders died at a hundred and two, and within two months the doors were chained and it turned out the place owed the county more than a million dollars in back taxes. The last paychecks never came. Now the county had it, for the taxes, and kids went up there at night to drink and paint their names on the walls, and men came with bolt cutters for the copper.

Mateo had ten vacation days and took them all at once in the slow week before peak season. He parked his Corolla at a pull-off on 447, walked up the old entrance road past a row of tall streetlights with their globes shot out, and carried in two gallons of water, a sleeping pad, a blanket and the phone, which he meant to leave off.

He slept in a villa at the back with the door kicked in. The bed was round. The carpet went up the walls and over the platform where the heart-shaped tub had been until somebody pried it out for the pipes behind it, and left a red heart-shaped hole in the floor that he walked around in the dark. The ceiling had been mirrors. Most of it had come down in the winters and lay in pieces across the carpet, and at first light every piece held a little square of sky, and some of them, when he lay still and looked, held pieces of each other, and the pieces in the pieces held more.

In the day he sat by the pool. It was shaped like a wedding bell, wide at one end and narrowing to a rounded neck at the other, and the water in it was green and still and so thick with algae that a lawn chair and a shopping cart sat in it like things set into Jell-O. Ferns had come up through the cracks in the deck. He sat on the coping at the narrow end with his legs crossed, facing the pines, where he could hear the trucks downshifting on 447 and the creek beyond them, and he closed his eyes the way she did.

On the second day an old woman came up the entrance road with a folding lawn chair and a plastic grocery bag. She set the chair down by the pool and sat in it and took out a Cool Whip tub of halušky, cabbage and noodles fried in butter, and a fork, and put them on the coping beside him, and a thermos of coffee. She did not ask him what he was doing.

My husband went to deer camp forty years, she said. I know what a man sitting looks like.

Her name was Anna Hrabal. She lived in a little house across 447 and had made beds at Penn Hills for thirty-one years, and the last two weeks of her pay had never come either. She came every day at noon after that, and he ate once a day, what she brought, and nothing else. Sometimes she sat in the chair for an hour and told him things without needing him to answer. She had come down from Freeland as a girl, from the coal patches. She had been eighteen the August the creek came up thirty feet in fifteen minutes after the hurricanes and took the camp on Brodhead Creek, the big house with the families from New Jersey up in its attic, mothers and little ones, thirty-seven of them. That was the year Penn Hills got built over new.

Nobody wants to be anyplace too long, she said, folding her chair. That’s the whole Poconos. Honeymoons. You come for a week and you go home.

He saw what he wanted. That was the first thing. He had thought it would take longer. He wanted a cigarette, and then he wanted a beer, and then he wanted the phone so badly his hand went to the pocket by itself, and he let it go there and come back. He wanted to be seen sitting. He wanted Hollis to see him sitting. He wanted the girl from receiving with the gold hoops. He wanted to sleep, and when he slept he wanted to be awake. He wanted Anna Hrabal to come at noon and he watched himself wanting it from about eleven on. He wanted all of it the way the gun on his wrist wanted the next bin, and it was the same wanting every time, under the different names, and some afternoons he could almost watch it come up out of nothing and go back into nothing like the bubbles that rose now and then through the green water of the pool.

The phone died on the fourth day. He let it. On the sixth it rained, and he sat in the villa with the mirrors and watched the rain in every one of them. By the eighth his face had gone thin and quiet, and Anna Hrabal looked at him a long time over her thermos and did not say anything at all.

III. The Shoot

Mount Pocono and Route 447 · the ninth day

Danielle Kostelnik served drinks at the casino in Mount Pocono, the one they built where the old Mount Airy Lodge had been. She wore the black uniform with the corset top and carried a tray of comped vodka sodas up and down the slot rows from eight at night until four in the morning, and she was good at it, and she was tired of it. She was twenty-nine. Her daughter Kayla was seven and lived weekdays with Dani’s mother in Hazleton, in the house on Alter Street, where there was a yard and a school that was not falling down.

On the side, under the name Honey Kosta, Dani sold photo sets of herself online. Some months that money was more than the casino money. Now and then, when the money was good and the man seemed all right, she met one in person, and she had rules about that which she kept.

This one called himself a photographer. He had written to her for three weeks from somewhere in north Jersey, polite, with a portfolio of real pictures of real ruins, grain elevators and churches and a hospital in the Catskills. He wanted what he called abandoned glamour. He wanted her in a red heart-shaped tub at Penn Hills at the golden hour, a girl in a ruin, six hundred dollars for the set, and he had sent three hundred up front to show he was serious. They were to meet at half past six at the old sign by the road.

She did her makeup in the Jeep in the pull-off. She put her hair up in a loose twist so she could let it down for the camera when he wanted it down. She had a vintage slip in a garment bag on the back seat, and the blouse she was wearing, white, with too many small buttons, because the buttons photographed well undone.

Half past six. Seven. She texted him. At twenty past seven she texted again and the message did not go through, and when she went to his profile it was gone. She sat behind the wheel and laughed out loud, by herself, in the car.

She had been stood up before. Kayla’s father had stood her up for most of a year, and then for good. A boy had left her on a bench at Funfest in Hazleton when she was fifteen, with two cotton candies, and she had eaten both. She had the three hundred dollars, and she had the light, which was coming low and gold through the pines now and laying long shadows across the lot, and she did not want to drive back up the mountain yet and put the corset on.

Somewhere in her mother’s house there was a photograph of her parents on their honeymoon in 1984, standing at the narrow end of a swimming pool shaped like a bell. Her mother had feathered hair and her father had his arm around her neck like a headlock. They had divorced when Dani was six.

She got out of the Jeep, and stepped over the chain, and walked in.

IV. The Clapping

Penn Hills · a quarter to eight

The place was worse than she remembered from the pictures people posted. Somebody had written LOVE IS DEAD in silver paint across the front of the lobby and somebody else had crossed out DEAD and written BLIND, and somebody else had crossed out BLIND. Glass crunched under her flats. She walked down past the villas with their doors hanging, looking into each one, the round beds and the shag on the walls, the heart-shaped holes where the tubs had been. There were no tubs. He would have known that, she thought, if he had ever been coming.

Then she came around the end of the last building and there was the pool, the bell, green as a bottle, and a man sitting at the narrow end of it with his eyes closed.

She stopped. He did not move. He was young and thin and needed a shave, in a grey hoodie with a warehouse logo on it, sitting cross-legged on the concrete lip with his hands in his lap like he was waiting for a bus that would come in a hundred years. A lawn chair stood empty beside him. She looked this way and that. There was nobody else anywhere. The light was on the tops of the pines and nowhere else.

Something in her that had been stood up an hour ago came up into her mouth, light and mean and almost happy. Look at this, she thought. Here is a man.

She walked around the wide end of the bell and stood across the narrow water from him, eight feet away, close enough that he would hear her flats on the deck. He did not open his eyes. So she began to undo the buttons of the white blouse, slowly, from the top, the way she did for the camera, all the way down. Then she did them up again. Then she undid them again. She reached up and pulled the pins out of her hair and shook it down over her shoulders, and twisted it back up, and pinned it, and pulled the pins again. Then she clapped her hands, twice, hard, and laughed.

The clap went off the empty villas like a shot. A crow went up out of the pines.

He opened his eyes.

Mateo had been sitting nine days with nothing in him but water and Anna Hrabal’s halušky and the slow watching of his own wanting, and he had come to think that it had gone quiet, and it had not gone anywhere. It came up through him all at once, every bit of it, from the soles of his feet. His whole body went hot and then shook. His hands shook in his lap. Sweat broke at his hairline and ran. His heart went like the scanner gun when you were behind, and the woman across the water was undoing a button and laughing at him, and he could not look away and he could not look at her, and every want of the nine days, the cigarette and the phone and the girl with the gold hoops and the beer and to be seen, rolled together into this one and stood up in him like a wave about to break.

What is this, he thought. What is this. What is this.

V. Senses Still

Rochester and Analomink · 7:52 p.m.

In Rochester, Hollis Tate came home from the laundry and ate supper standing up and washed the plate, and then stood in her kitchen for a while with the plate in her hand.

The young man from Pennsylvania who always wrote in lower case had not been in the comments for nine days. She had noticed on the third. She did not keep track of people, as a rule; they came and went, and that was the arrangement. But she had given him the card, and she had seen his face in his little round picture, and she had thought about him on the bus.

She put the plate away. She propped the phone on the phone books and sat down on the blanket with her back against the refrigerator and went live at 7:40 in the evening, which she had never done, and closed her eyes.

By the pool in Analomink, the phone on Mateo’s folded blanket lit up.

It had been dead five days. He had not charged it; there was nowhere to charge it. It lay face up on the grey wool by his knee and its screen came on white and then showed the notice, the way it always had at half past four in the morning, @hollis_sits is LIVE, and then the kitchen. The stove light. The shelf with the salt and the white figure. Hollis on her blanket with her eyes shut, and up the right side of the picture, already, the hearts coming, more of them than he had ever seen at once, a whole slow river of them, rose and green and yellow, going up past the stove.

Dani saw it too. She would say so for the rest of her life, to anyone who asked and to some who did not. She saw the light come up off the ground beside the man and she thought it was a flashlight, and then she saw it was a phone, and a woman in it, a Black woman sitting on a kitchen floor in a cardigan.

Hollis opened her eyes and looked straight down the lens, and she lifted her hands and signed.

Mateo did not know sign language. He knew three signs, which he had looked up in April out of politeness and had never used: hello, and thank you, and sorry. He understood every word she said. He would never be able to explain how. The nearest he came, years later, was that it was like being reminded of something. The words went into him in the order she made them, with her hands moving close to her body and her eyes on his, and he could have written them down afterward letter for letter, and later, in a library, he looked for them and found them, exactly, in a book she had kept since high school.

Yet I experienced sometimes that the most sweet and tender, the most innocent and encouraging society may be found in any natural object, even for the poor misanthrope and most melancholy man. There can be no very black melancholy to him who lives in the midst of Nature and has his senses still.

— Thoreau, Walden, “Solitude”

Then she put her hands down in her lap and closed her eyes again.

The wave in him did not break. It went down. It went down the way the creek water goes clear in a jar on a windowsill when you stop shaking it, everything that was stirred up in it settling, slowly, all of it, to the bottom, until you can see straight through. His hands stopped. His heart came back down to a walk. The sweat cooled on his face in the evening air. He looked at the woman across the narrow end of the pool and saw her plainly, a tired woman his age with her hair half down and her blouse half buttoned and mascara starting to go in the heat, who had been stood up, he could see that too, and who was frightened now; and he wanted nothing from her at all. He looked at the pines, which were only pines, and they were the most beautiful thing he had ever seen. He looked at the green water, which was beautiful. He looked at the ferns in the cracked deck, and at the empty lawn chair, and at the heart-shaped holes in the floors of the villas behind her, and none of it needed to be anything else, and neither did he.

Dani had stopped with one button half through its hole. She did it up the rest of the way, and the next, to her throat, without knowing she was doing it.

The young man put his palms together and bowed low over the phone, all the way down, until his forehead almost touched the screen. Then he stood up.

And then, Dani said, and she always said this part plainly and never changed it, he went up. He did not jump. He went up off the coping slowly, standing, with his hands still together, the way a man rises on an escalator, past the top of the dead streetlight, past the tops of the pines where the last of the light was, and then north, over the trees, toward New York, and she watched him go until she could not tell him from the dark.

The phone lay on the blanket. The screen was black. She picked it up and pressed the button and nothing happened. It was cold, as if it had been dead for days.

VI. The Doorbell Light

Jefferson Avenue, Rochester · the same night

Three hundred and twelve people were watching when Hollis Tate opened her eyes at 7:52 that evening and signed into her phone, and two hundred of them did not know sign, and all of them, afterward, said they had understood it. That was the first thing people argued about. The second thing was the replay. On that app in that year a broadcast stayed up for twenty-four hours and then it was gone, and this one went the way they all went, the next evening, before anyone thought to save it properly. There were screenshots. There was a grainy recording somebody in Leeds made by pointing a second phone at the first. In it you can see her hands move. You cannot see what they say.

She ended the broadcast a little after eight. She was rinsing her cup when the light by the front door began to flash, the lamp her nephew had wired to the doorbell, three long pulses across the kitchen ceiling.

The young man on her porch was barefoot and thin and had not shaved in a long time. He wore a grey hoodie with a warehouse logo on it and smelled of pine and of a pool that needed cleaning. She knew his face from the little round picture. He did not say anything. He knelt on the porch boards, in front of her, and put his two hands over the toes of her house shoes, and bowed his head down to them and stayed there for a while.

Then he got up and made the one sign he was sure of, fingers from the chin out toward her, thank you, and went down the steps and along Jefferson Avenue under the streetlights, and Mrs. Lacy across the street, who was on her porch with her fan, said afterward that she had watched him all the way to the corner and that he had not turned it. He just was not on the sidewalk anymore.

It is a hundred and ninety miles from Analomink to Rochester as the crow flies. The last time anybody looked, there was still no bus.

•••

Anna Hrabal came up the entrance road at noon the next day with her chair and a Cool Whip tub of pierogi and found his blanket folded square on the coping at the narrow end of the pool, and the pad rolled, and the two water jugs rinsed out and stood upside down to dry, and nobody. She sat in her chair for an hour anyway. Then she ate the pierogi herself.

Dani drove to Hazleton that night instead of to the casino and called in sick from her mother’s kitchen, and went in and lay down next to Kayla in the narrow bed with the glow-stars on the ceiling and did not sleep. She kept the three hundred dollars. She never told the story to a customer. She told it to her mother, who said she had been drinking, and to a priest at St. Gabriel’s, who did not say that, and years later to Kayla, who believed it.

Some mornings after that, when she got off at four and sat in the Jeep in the employee lot on the mountain with the heater going, she opened the app and found the kitchen in Rochester, the stove light, the woman on her blanket with her eyes closed. She never commented. People asked Hollis about the evening broadcast for a long time, and once, after the hundredth time, Hollis held a card up at six o’clock. I WAS SITTING. That was all she ever said about it.

The main building at Penn Hills burned to the ground on the fourth of September, 2017, the third fire in three years. Dani saw it on the news from the casino break room, the lobby going up orange behind the trees on 447. The pool was not on the news. The pool did not burn. It would be there still under the ash and the leaves, green, narrowing to its rounded neck, with a lawn chair standing beside it that somebody had forgotten to take home.

That morning at half past four she found the kitchen in Rochester and watched it until six. Before she closed the app she touched the screen once, with one finger, and one small heart went up the side of the picture, past the stove light and the white figure on the shelf, slowly, wobbling a little, and out at the top.

The verse

Forests are delightful; where the world finds no delight, there the passionless will find delight, for they look not for pleasures.Dhammapada 99, tr. F. Max Müller (1881)

In the commentary, a monk takes a meditation subject from the Buddha and goes to sit in a run-down pleasure garden. A courtesan who has been stood up by a lover wanders in, sees him alone, and decides to unsettle him: she lets down her undergarment and puts it back on, unbinds her hair and binds it again, claps her hands and laughs, and his whole body floods with excitement. The Buddha, seated far away in his chamber at Jetavana, sees what is happening, sends forth a radiant image of himself, and speaks the verse. The monk becomes an arahant where he sits, rises into the air, and goes to give thanks at the Buddha’s feet. The Poconos and Periscope, 2015

After the Second World War the Pocono Mountains of northeastern Pennsylvania sold themselves as a honeymoon capital, and resorts competed with round beds, mirrored walls and heart-shaped tubs. Penn Hills Resort in Analomink, which began as a tavern in 1944, grew in the 1960s to more than a hundred rooms with a ski slope, golf course, ice rink and a swimming pool shaped like a wedding bell. Its co-founder died in March 2009 at 102, and within two months the resort closed, owing Monroe County over a million dollars in back taxes; workers’ final paychecks were never issued. Copper thieves, floods and vandals did the rest, and on September 4, 2017, the main building burned, the third fire in three years. Brodhead Creek runs past Analomink; in the flood of August 1955 it rose thirty feet in fifteen minutes, and thirty-seven people, mostly mothers and children, died at a camp there. The casino at Mount Pocono where Dani works, Mount Airy, opened in 2007 on the site of the old Mount Airy Lodge.

Twitter launched the live-video app Periscope on March 26, 2015, and released it for Android that May. Viewers could comment in real time and tap the screen to send little hearts floating up the side of the picture. In its first year a broadcast’s replay stayed up for twenty-four hours and then vanished; Periscope did not let broadcasts be saved permanently until May 2016. Hollis went to the Rochester School for the Deaf, founded in 1876 on St. Paul Street. Rochester, home also to the National Technical Institute for the Deaf, has been found by NTID researchers to have the largest deaf and hard-of-hearing population per capita among working-age adults of any American city studied.
