The Middle Portion

But having food and covering we shall be therewith content.
— 1 Timothy 6:8

Ετσι τ’ ακουσα, in the cook car below the bluffs at Fallbridge.

I. Food and Covering

Bingham Canyon, Utah · Cheesefare Sunday, 1914

Father Chrysanthos Doukas had a voice they talked about in three states. He had been trained at the Rizarios school in Athens and had served in Chicago and in Lowell, and in each place he had left the community board weeping at his sermons and furious over his accounts, and he had come west that winter with two trunks, a letter from a bishop nobody in Utah had heard of, and no community of his own. The men said he could read the Gospel so that you saw the boat rocking. The men also said, more quietly, that his hands were quick.

On the Sunday before the Great Fast he preached to the Cretans of the Bingham copper camp in the hall over a coffeehouse, at the memorial for two men killed in a cave-in at the Highland Boy. These Cretans were the hard ones of the canyon. They kept the Fast the old way, without oil from Monday to Friday, and they slept six to a shack and sent everything home. Father Chrysanthos took for his text the sixth chapter of Paul to Timothy, and he leaned on it the way a man leans on a gate.

But godliness with contentment is great gain: for we brought nothing into the world, for neither can we carry anything out; but having food and covering we shall be therewith content.

— 1 Timothy 6:6–8

He said that the two dead men had gone down the mountain with nothing in their hands, and that every man in the hall would do the same, and he named the things they would leave behind, the shirts and the watches and the money orders, until the hall was quiet enough to hear the stove tick. Food and covering, he said. A loaf, and a coat on your back, and God. What more did your grandfathers have in the mountains of Sfakia? What more did the Apostle have?

The Cretans wept. Then they did what men do who have been moved and have nothing else to give. They took up a collection that came to a hundred and eleven dollars. One gave him a sheepskin coat with the fleece turned in, new from the company store. One gave him two shirts of good wool. Their headman took down from the shelf at the end of the hall the brass censer and the little silver lamp they had bought by subscription two years before, against the day a priest would come up the canyon to serve them, and asked him to bless the things and use them while he was there.

He blessed them and used them. When he left on the morning train he had the coat on his back and the shirts in his trunk, and the censer and the lamp were in the trunk with the shirts. The Cretans noticed the empty shelf on Tuesday. Nobody said anything for a week, because he was a priest. Then somebody did.

II. Four Towns

The Oregon Short Line · the first week of Lent

In those years there was no bishop over the Greeks in America who could make a priest go anywhere. Every community was its own master. The men of a town got together, elected a board, rented a hall or built a church, and sent for a priest, or hired one who was passing, and paid him what they had agreed, and fired him when they felt like it. And a priest who was passing could ask what a town was offering before he unpacked.

The Greeks of Pocatello met him at the depot with a delegation of four in their Sunday hats. They were shop men and section men of the Short Line, and they had no church and no priest, and they wanted a preacher for the Fast and someone to give them the Resurrection at midnight in the Odd Fellows’ hall. They had heard about the voice. They took him to the coffeehouse and gave him the chair by the stove.

And what does the community give, said Father Chrysanthos, to a priest who keeps the Fast here and stays for Pascha?

Sixty dollars, Father, and your board.

He said he would think about it and asked the way to the hotel, and in the morning he took the westbound train, and left a pair of galoshes behind in the corner of the coffeehouse by the stove.

At La Grande, where the Greeks worked in the Union Pacific shops and kept a room over a fruit store for a chapel, he asked the same question.

Eighty dollars, Father, and the room.

He left his ebony walking stick in the umbrella stand of the fruit store and took the next train west.

At The Dalles they were fewer and poorer, mostly men off the river line, and they offered a hundred because they had been saving since Christmas. He drank their coffee, said it was the best he had tasted since Smyrna, and left behind on the counter of the restaurant on Second Street his brass briki, the long-handled coffee pot he had carried with him from Athens, which his mother had used before him.

In Portland they offered a hundred and fifty dollars for the Fast and Pascha, and the rooms over a confectioner’s on Front Street, and a fee for each sermon besides.

Very good, said Father Chrysanthos. I will stay here.

It rained in Portland from the first day of the Fast to the last. He preached on Wednesdays and Fridays to the shoeshine men and the peddlers and the candy-makers and the mill hands, and on Sundays to everybody, and men came down from the camps on the river on the Saturday night trains to hear him. He preached on the rich man and Lazarus. He preached on the lilies of the field that toil not, neither do they spin. He preached on food and covering. The bootblacks who stood in the back in their aprons said they had never heard anything like it, and the confectioners brought him boxes of loukoumi, and the ladies of the community, who were only a few, sent him a bolt of gold brocade for a new vestment and lengths of black wool for a new cassock, and men pressed coins into his hand at the door.

III. The Cook Car

Fallbridge, Washington · Lent 1914

A hundred miles up the river the extra gang of the Spokane, Portland and Seattle was relaying steel east of Fallbridge, where the new bridge of the Oregon Trunk crossed over to the Deschutes. There were forty-one men in the gang, nearly all of them from the villages around Tripoli in Arcadia, and they lived in six bunk cars on a siding below the bluffs, with a cook car at the end and a water car behind it. They worked ten hours a day, and on Sundays too when the roadmaster said so, and in the evenings the wind came up the Gorge from the west and drove grit into everything, into the bread and the blankets and the creases of their eyelids. On still nights they could hear Celilo, the falls where the river people fished, five miles off, a sound like a crowd that never went home.

The cook was a monk.

His name was Gerasimos and he was past sixty. He had come out from a small house of monks on the Holy Mountain two years before with a letter from his elder, to go among the Greeks of America and beg for a new roof, because the old one let the snow onto the altar. He had gone to Chicago, and to Salt Lake, and up the canyons and along the lines, and he had seen how the men lived, six to a car, sleeping on gunny sacks, eating what the boarding contractors gave them, sending every dollar home for their sisters’ dowries and their fathers’ debts, and he had not had the heart to ask them for anything. So he had written to his elder that he would earn the roof instead, and he had hired on as cook to the first Greek gang that would have him. He sent eight dollars a month to the Holy Mountain and kept nothing. He slept on a shelf in the cook car above the flour barrels, under one army blanket.

He did not preach. In Lent he cooked beans, and lentils, and chickpeas, and bread that he baked in an oven he had dug into the clay bank of the cut and lined with stones, the way they did at home. On Saturdays and Sundays there was oil on the beans and sometimes octopus from a tin. In the evenings, when the plates were washed, he stood between the stove and the flour barrels with his face to a paper icon of the Mother of God pinned to the wall, and said the prayer of Saint Ephraim, and went down three times on his knees with his forehead to the boards, and got up again with one hand on a barrel. He did it every evening of the Fast. The first week, two or three men stayed in the car and did it with him. By the third week there was not room for everybody, and men knelt on the ground outside the open door, in the wind.

O Lord and Master of my life, grant not unto me a spirit of slothfulness, of discouragement, of lust of power, of vain babbling.

But vouchsafe unto thy servant the spirit of continence, of meekness, of patience, and of love.

— Prayer of St. Ephraim the Syrian

Three cousins from the village of Piana had the bunks at the end of the third car. Stavros was the eldest, twenty-four, slow and careful, with four sisters at home and a ledger in his pocket in which he kept, to the penny, what each of their dowries still lacked. Mitsos was nineteen and had a voice like a goat and a temper that came and went like the wind in the cut. Yannis was between them in age and in everything else. He was the one who made peace.

Yannis had two suits, a black one for church and a grey one he had bought in Portland for a photograph to send home, and he had a robe. He had bought it the autumn before from a trader at The Dalles who sold to the river people, a Pendleton robe from the mills at Pendleton, heavy as a sheep, banded in red and black and yellow, and it had cost him more than two weeks’ pay. Stavros had told him he was a fool. Mitsos had told him it was the most beautiful thing in Washington. Yannis said it was the first thing he had ever owned that was not for somebody else, and he slept under it, and the other two slept closer to his bunk than they needed to.

On the Sunday of the Cross, the third Sunday of the Fast, the gang was working in the cut west of the bridge, and the wind had been at the bluff all night, and a slab of basalt the size of a wagon let go above them without a sound until it hit. The others ran. Yannis was tamping under a tie with his back to the hill.

There was no priest nearer than Portland, and the railroad would not hold a body. They buried him the next morning on the flat above the siding, in his black suit, with a cross the monk made out of two pieces of a broken tie. Gerasimos read the Trisagion over him, the prayers for the dead that a monk who is not a priest is allowed to read, and the gang sang the responses into the wind. Afterward the monk boiled wheat in the cook car and mixed it with sugar and a little cinnamon from a tin, and they ate it standing by the grave, a spoonful each, for the soul.

That night Stavros wrote to the famous preacher in Portland. The forty days would fall in May. Would the Father do them the honor, on his way wherever he went after Pascha, of stopping at Fallbridge to sing the memorial over their cousin’s grave? The gang would make a collection.

The Father wrote back on the stationery of the confectioner on Front Street. He would be travelling east by the river line after Pascha. He would stop.

IV. Bright Week

Portland · Pascha, April 1914

On Holy Friday the women covered the bier of Christ with flowers from the market, and the men carried it around the block in the rain behind the candles, and the Father sang the Lamentations so that the confectioners wept into their beards. On Saturday at midnight he came out of the dark sanctuary with one candle and sang Come, receive the light, and the light went from his candle to the first hand and the next and through the whole church and out the doors into the street, where there was not room for everybody, and the bells rang, and the men shouted Christ is risen at each other and cracked red eggs, and the shoeshine boys who had come in from the camps let off pistols at the sky. It was the best Pascha anybody in Portland could remember. They said so for years.

In Bright Week, while the town was still eating lamb, Father Chrysanthos sat in his rooms over the confectioner’s and wrote three letters, each in his beautiful seminary hand.

To Pocatello he wrote that he had left his galoshes with them as a pledge that he was among them in spirit through the holy Fast, and that he prayed for them by name every day of it, and that the community would surely wish to send what was customary for a priest who has kept the Fast for a community, together with the galoshes. To La Grande he wrote the same, about the stick. To The Dalles he wrote the same, about the briki, his mother’s briki, which he could not live without.

Pocatello sent the galoshes and thirty dollars. La Grande sent the stick and forty. The Dalles sent the briki wrapped in a newspaper and the whole hundred they had saved since Christmas, with a note from their president that said they were sorry it could not be more.

He had a third trunk made for the brocade and the black wool. He bought a gold watch on Washington Street. When he went down to the Union Station in the first week of May there were five pieces of baggage on the cart, and the porter had to make two trips.

V. The Division

Fallbridge · May 1914

He got off the train in a black cassock with the sheepskin coat over his arm against the wind and the silver watch-chain of the Cretans across his front, and the whole gang was at the siding to meet him in their good clothes. The roadmaster had given them the afternoon. The monk had made koliva in the big pot, wheat and walnuts and raisins and sugar, and smoothed the top of it with the flat of a knife and laid a cross on it in almonds, and the men carried it up the path to the flat above the siding on a board.

Father Chrysanthos sang the memorial at the grave in that voice, and the wind took it out over the river, and some of the men said afterward that the river people fishing at the falls must have heard it. He sang Memory eternal three times, and the gang sang it after him, and Stavros and Mitsos stood at the foot of the tie-cross with their hats in their hands and did not look at each other. The collection came to thirty-eight dollars. He blessed the koliva and took a spoonful and took the collection.

Then he came down to the third car to see the young man’s things, because Stavros asked him to.

Yannis’s things were on his bunk. There was a razor and a strop, a bundle of letters from his mother, a photograph of himself in the grey suit, and the grey suit, and the robe, folded so the red band showed. The black suit was in the ground.

The cousins had been quarrelling over the robe for forty days. Stavros said the suit and the letters and the razor would go home to the mother, and the robe should be sold in The Dalles for what it would bring, and the money put to the dowries of Yannis’s own sisters, because that was what was right. Mitsos said Yannis had slept under that robe, and it was not a thing to be sold to some stranger, and he would keep it and sleep under it himself and remember him, and Stavros was a bookkeeper with no heart. Stavros said Mitsos only wanted it because it was beautiful. Mitsos said Stavros only wanted the money for the ledger. They had said worse things than that in the forty days. Twice the foreman had pulled them apart.

They stood in the narrow aisle of the bunk car on either side of the priest, with the other men crowding the doorway behind, and Stavros said, Father, you are an educated man and a priest of God. You divide it for us, however you think right, and that will be the end of it.

Will you both abide by what I decide?

We will, said Stavros. Mitsos said it too, after a moment.

Very good, said Father Chrysanthos.

He gave the razor and the strop and the photograph to Stavros, to send home to the mother with the letters. He gave the grey suit to Mitsos, since he was nearly the dead man’s size, so that he would have something of his cousin’s to wear to church. Then he picked up the robe and shook it out, and it fell open from his hands to the floor of the car, red and black and gold, and the men in the doorway made a sound.

As for this, he said, it is not a thing to be fought over by young men, or sold in a shop. It is a priest’s cloth. This belongs on the shoulders of those who preach the word of God.

He folded it once over his arm, and thanked them for their hospitality, and said he would wait for the eastbound at the depot, and went out through the men in the doorway, who stood aside for him because he was a priest.

VI. The Fox

The cook car · that evening

The cousins came to the cook car at dusk. The monk was scouring the koliva pot with sand. They told him all of it, both talking at once, and when they had told it they were not angry at each other anymore, only at the priest, and ashamed, and sick, the way you feel after you have been robbed in front of people.

Gerasimos set the pot down.

It is not the first time, he said. That one has been taking from your plates since before you were born.

Then he sat down on a flour barrel and told them a story, the way he told the boys stories at night in the village on the Holy Mountain when they had been fighting, and the men in the car went quiet to listen.

Once, he said, below the falls, there were two otters. One of them hunted along the bank in the shallows and one of them hunted out in the deep water, and between them, working together, they caught a red salmon so big that the two of them together could hardly drag it out onto the rock. And then they could not agree how to share it. The head is mine, said the one. The tail is yours. No, the head is mine, said the other. And while they were shouting, a fox came down the bank, a fine one, with a fine tail.

Uncle, they said, you divide it for us.

I can’t, said the fox. The eagle has made me a judge, and I sit in court all day long. I only came down here to stretch my legs.

Uncle, don’t say that. Divide it for us, and that will be the end of it.

Will you both abide by what I decide?

We will.

Very good, said the fox. And he bit off the head and laid it to one side, and bit off the tail and laid it to the other side, and said, The one who runs along the bank shall have the tail, and the one who swims in the deep water shall have the head. And this middle portion belongs to the court. And he took the whole middle of the salmon in his mouth and went up the bank with it, and the two otters sat on the rock, one with a head and one with a tail, and watched his fine tail go away into the sagebrush.

Mitsos laughed in spite of himself, and then put his face in his hands. Stavros did not laugh.

That fox has eaten from a great many plates, said the monk. In my village and in yours. In Smyrna and in Chicago. He ate your fathers’ fish, too. He put his hand on the back of Mitsos’s neck, the way you would with a mule. It is gone. Let it go, both of you. You still have each other, and that cost you nothing, and nobody can carry it off over his arm.

•••

The eastbound was late. It was late most nights, and that night it would not reach Fallbridge until after one. The foreman, who was a Greek himself and could not have the priest sitting alone in the depot all evening, brought him down to the cook car for supper, and he came with the robe over his shoulders against the wind, the way you would wear a shawl.

It was still the season of the Resurrection, and there was meat. The monk had made a stew of mutton and potatoes for the gang. The men ate in silence at the long plank tables, and the priest sat at the end by the stove, and talked a little about Portland and about the war in the Balkans that was over now, and nobody answered him.

The monk brought him his plate himself. He set it down, and the bread beside it, and stood a moment with the empty pan in his hands. He did not raise his voice. He said it the way he said everything, the way he might have told a man there was more stew if he wanted it, so that only the near tables heard, and the near tables told the rest.

Thou therefore that teachest another, teachest thou not thyself? thou that preachest a man should not steal, dost thou steal?

— Romans 2:21

Father Chrysanthos looked up at him. He was a man who had an answer for everything, and he had stood up in front of community boards in Chicago and Lowell and talked them out of their own minutes. He opened his mouth to say something. Then he looked at the old man’s hands on the pan, and at the patched cassock, and at the shelf over the flour barrels with the one army blanket folded on it, and he closed his mouth and looked at his plate.

He ate the stew. He thanked the cook. When the eastbound whistled for Fallbridge at a quarter past one he went up the path to the depot in the dark with the robe on his shoulders, and the gang heard the train take water and pull out, and that was all anybody on the north bank saw of him.

VII. The Prayer

Fallbridge · the next morning

The cousins did not sleep much. Toward morning Mitsos said into the dark that he would like to break that priest’s neck, and Stavros said he would hold his coat, and they lay there a while and found they did not mean it, and did not know what to do with what they did mean.

Before the gang went out they came to the cook car. The monk was at the oven in the bank with the long paddle, taking out the bread. They asked him what they should do about it. They meant about the priest. Should they write to the communities, to Portland, to The Dalles, to the newspaper in New York? Should they write to Yannis’s mother and tell her where her son’s robe had gone?

Gerasimos set the loaves on the board to cool. Then he told them the end of the prayer they had been saying with him on their knees every evening for seven weeks, the part they had said so many times that they had stopped hearing it.

Yea, O Lord and King, grant that I may perceive my own transgressions, and judge not my brother. For blessed art thou unto ages of ages. Amen.

— Prayer of St. Ephraim the Syrian

Start there, he said. Then, if you still have something to teach him, you can go and teach it.

They stood in the door of the cook car a long time. Mitsos said, finally, that he had called Stavros a bookkeeper with no heart, and that it was not true. Stavros said that he had wanted the money for the ledger, and that it was true. Then they had to go, because the foreman was shouting.

That night Mitsos packed the grey suit in brown paper with the razor and the strop and the photograph and the bundle of letters, and Stavros wrote to the mother in Piana, and they took it together to the depot to send. Stavros put in a money order for a little more than the robe would have brought in The Dalles. It was in both their names, and Stavros wrote it into the ledger in a column by itself.

They never wrote to Portland. In the autumn they heard from a man who had come up from Salt Lake that the Cretans of Bingham had got their censer back, by express, with no letter. Nobody knew what it meant, and the cousins did not ask.

•••

On the first Sunday after the memorial there was no work, and the two of them walked down the bluff to the river below the cut, where the water came round the rocks green and fast from the falls. They sat on the basalt in the sun with one lunch pail between them and the bread from the monk’s oven. Out on a flat rock in the current two otters had a salmon between them, a red one, and they were eating it from the two ends at once, the one from the head and the one from the tail, working toward the middle.

Background

The verse

Let each man direct himself first to what is proper, then let him teach others; thus a wise man will not suffer.Dhammapada 158, tr. F. Max Müller (1881)

In the commentary, the elder Upananda is a gifted preacher and a greedy monk. After preaching on being content with little, he accepts a heap of robes from monks who keep the strict practices and carries off their utensils too. With the rains retreat coming, he asks four monasteries in turn what allowance they give a monk who spends the rains there, leaves his sandals at the first, his staff at the second and his water pot at the third, and settles at the fourth, which offers four robes. When the rains are over he sends for his belongings and claims the allowance from all four. On the road with his cartload he meets two young monks who cannot agree how to share two robes and a costly blanket. They ask him to divide them; he gives each a robe and walks off with the blanket, saying it belongs to those who preach. The Buddha tells the young monks the tale of two otters and a jackal who settles their quarrel over a fish by taking the middle for himself, and rebukes Upananda: one who admonishes others should first set himself right. Read the full commentary story; its fable is the Jātaka of the jackal Dabbhapuppha (no. 400).

Greeks on the Columbia, 1914

Greek immigration to the United States peaked in the years before the First World War, driven by poor land, debt and the burden of providing dowries for sisters. In the West most of the newcomers went first to the mines, the mills and the railroads: the 1910 census counted 3,448 Greeks in Oregon, more than 2,100 of them railroad workers, many on the Spokane, Portland and Seattle, built along the north bank of the Columbia. Extra gangs lived in bunk cars on sidings, worked ten-hour days and often fed themselves. Fallbridge (renamed Wishram in 1926) was the junction where the Oregon Trunk crossed the river, beside Celilo Falls, a fishing place of the river peoples for thousands of years until The Dalles Dam drowned it in 1957. In Utah some 1,200 Greeks were working the Utah Copper mine at Bingham by 1912. The heavy banded robes of Pendleton Woolen Mills, incorporated in 1909, were made first of all for Native buyers in the Northwest and were costly and prized.

Before 1921 there was no Greek bishop in America. The Church of Greece was given nominal authority over the immigrant parishes in 1908, but in practice each community was governed by its own elected board, which raised the money, built or rented a church and hired a priest, sometimes one who was passing through, and dismissed him when it chose. Portland’s Holy Trinity, the first Greek Orthodox church in the Pacific Northwest, was founded in 1907; its stone church at Seventeenth and Taggart was finished in 1910. The Archdiocese was incorporated in 1921. The Prayer of St. Ephrem, said with prostrations on the weekdays of Great Lent, is quoted here in the translation of Isabel Hapgood’s 1906 Service Book. Father Chrysanthos, the monk Gerasimos, the gang at Fallbridge and every community’s offer are invented.
