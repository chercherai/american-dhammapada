Walking the American WayCommentary 26.37 · Vaṅgīsa

The Fifth Skull

Then will I do the like, O Death, and thou
Wilt ne’er discover which the way I take.
— Psalms of the Brethren 1213

Thus have I heard, on the Hill above Boulder Creek.

I. The Professor’s Desk

Beacon Street, Boston · 1964

Thayer Vance was nine the first time he did it. His grandfather had taught anatomy at the Harvard Medical School for thirty-one years, and on the desk in his study, between the inkstand and a tin of horehound drops, he kept a skull that had come out of the teaching collection when the collection was replaced. It had a brass hinge in the crown so the top lifted off like a lid, and a number inked over the left eye. The boy was not supposed to touch it. He was alone in the study one wet afternoon in March, waiting to be driven to the dentist, and he flicked it on the forehead with the nail of his middle finger, the way his mother tested a melon.

The sound was small and dry. Behind the sound, though not in the room, a light came up. It was dull and bluish, the blue of skim milk in a glass, and it lasted as long as the sound lasted and went out.

He did it again. Blue again.

He told nobody. Over the next nine years he tapped every skull he could get his hands on, which was not many: two in the biology laboratory at Milton, one in a junk shop on Charles Street that wanted thirty dollars for it, and on a family trip to Rome, while his sisters were looking the other way, a whole wall of them in the Capuchin crypt under the Via Veneto, where he went down the row with his fingernail like a boy running a stick along a fence and came up into the street white and shaking. The lights were nearly always one of four. A smoke color, like a coal fire seen through a dirty window. The skim-milk blue. A dull yellow. A dull white. Twice, a muddy red, and once a green.

He did not know what they were until his first winter at Harvard, when the paperback everybody in Cambridge was reading that year came around to him, and he sat on the floor of a room in Weld Hall and read a list he had been carrying in his body since he was nine.

If it be asked, ‘What are they?’ [they are] a dull white light from the devas, a dull green light from the asuras, a dull yellow light from human beings, a dull blue light from the brutes, a dull reddish light from the pretas, and a dull smoke-coloured light from Hell.

— The Tibetan Book of the Dead, Book I

He left Harvard in the spring of 1974 without telling his father, and hitchhiked to Colorado, because it was in all the papers that the poets were going to be teaching at the new Buddhist institute in Boulder that summer, and he had decided he was a poet. He had a duffel bag, a notebook, and sixty-one dollars. Nobody in Boulder could have told by looking at him that he was a Brahmin, except that he said rather.

II. The Crate

University Hill · June 1974

Gil Ostrow and Danny Kehoe had been to Kathmandu twice and come back the second time with a Volkswagen bus that had been green once and a tea crate that had been shipped by sea to Oakland, full of what Gil called Himalayan ritual art. There were brass bells and dorjes, a thanka with water damage, a dozen prayer wheels for the tourists, three thighbone trumpets wrapped in newspaper from Asan Tole, and at the bottom, packed in rice straw, eleven skull cups. The Tibetans called them kapala. Some were mounted in silver with a silver lid. Some were bare bone, the crown of a head sawn off clean at the brow and polished inside by a hundred years of use. Gil sold the bells and the wheels from a card table outside a head shop on Thirteenth Street. He had not sold a single skull.

Thayer met them at a party in a rented house on College Avenue, where somebody had filled one of the bare kapalas with Red Zinger tea and was passing it around as a joke. When it came to Thayer he did not drink. He tapped the rim with his fingernail, and a dull white light came up behind the music and the talk and went out, and he said, before he could stop himself, Oh, this one went up.

Gil Ostrow had been a used-car salesman in Lakewood before Nepal, and he knew when somebody had just said something that was worth money. Say that again.

By two in the morning Thayer had tapped all eleven, sitting on the floor of the bus with a flashlight, and Gil had written on a strip of masking tape on the bottom of each one what the kid said. Four white. Three yellow. Two blue. Two smoke. Danny wanted to know how he did it. Thayer said he didn’t do anything. Danny said that was what everybody said.

Within the week the card table had a sign, lettered by a girl from the poetics school: SKULLS READ · WHERE DID THEY GO? Gil found two monk’s robes at the bottom of the crate, the maroon skirt and the maroon shawl, and put Thayer in both of them, with his hair to his shoulders and his Harvard shoes. The white skulls went for four hundred dollars, with a poem Thayer wrote for each one on an index card and signed with his initials. The yellow went for a hundred and fifty. The blue for sixty. Nobody would buy a smoke skull at any price, and they went back into the rice straw.

The readings themselves cost what people could pay. A student would hand over ten dollars, a dentist from Denver twenty, and once in Aspen a woman with a silver belt paid a hundred, and they would each pick a cup off the table, and Thayer would tap it and close his eyes and say a poem about where its owner had gone, and the poem was good, because he was good. In July they took the bus to Aspen, and over La Veta Pass to Taos, and down to Santa Fe, where a man who owned a gallery on Canyon Road bought two white skulls on the spot. Gil had a line he used everywhere. Ask him. There is nobody in this country like this kid.

Thayer took his share in an envelope and did not count it. He liked the robes. He liked the driving, with his bare feet out the window and Danny working the radio. He did not like the smoke skulls, which he would not pick up without a dish towel, and he did not like that sometimes in the middle of a poem he could hear himself and know he was performing, and he did not like what came into the faces of the people who bought the white ones.

III. Sunday

Thirteenth Street · July 14, 1974

Boulder that summer was full past bursting. More than two thousand people had come for the institute, and they slept in vans along the creek and on the floors of rented houses and in tents up Sunshine Canyon. They filled the auditorium at Boulder High, and on the nights the Rinpoche spoke he was often an hour late and sometimes two, and taught in a suit with a glass beside him that everybody could see was not water, and said things that made the room laugh and then go so quiet you could hear the wiring in the lights. More than a thousand had signed up to hear Ram Dass on the Bhagavad Gita. The two crowds looked at each other across the parking lots. The Rinpoche’s older students had cut their hair and wore jackets, and said sitting, and Ram Dass’s people still said far out, and the town said hippies about both.

On Sunday morning the Hill was quiet. Gil had the card table out early for the church crowd. A few doors up, four people came out of a house carrying things: a tall woman with a box of incense from the head shop, a man in a Vietnam field jacket with a coffee can full of marigolds from somebody’s yard, a heavyset nurse with a bag of oranges, and a skinny girl with nothing.

Where are you all going with that? Gil said.

Mapleton, said the man in the field jacket. To sit with Ama-la.

Who’s Ama-la?

The tall woman told him. A Tibetan who cooked for the house on Mapleton Hill where twelve of the Rinpoche’s students lived. In the mornings, before the house was up, she sat at the kitchen table, and some people sat with her.

What does she teach?

Not a lot, said the nurse.

Then what are you going for? Gil swept a hand over the table, over the white cups and the yellow ones and Thayer in his two robes on a folding chair. Here’s a kid who can rap on any skull you hand him and tell you where that person went. Heaven. Hell. Into a dog. You want to know where your grandmother is, he’s right here. Ten dollars.

What does he know, said the man in the field jacket, not very politely. There’s nobody like Ama-la.

There’s nobody like this kid.

It went back and forth on the sidewalk until the man who ran the head shop came out to see what the noise was. Then the skinny girl, who had not said anything, said, Well, bring him, then. Bring him up and we’ll find out.

IV. Five Skulls

Mapleton Hill

Her name was Dolkar. She was about fifty. She had been born in Nangchen, in Kham, and given at nine to a hermitage of women who practiced the cutting, which meant that they went at night to the places where the dead were laid out for the vultures, and beat the big hand drum and blew the thighbone trumpet, and offered their own bodies, in the mind, piece by piece, to anything that was hungry. In 1959 she had walked out over the passes with two other nuns, one of whom lived. She had broken stones for road crews in Sikkim for seven years and cooked for a Denver geologist and his wife in Kathmandu for five more, and when they came home they brought her with them. She did not go to the Rinpoche’s talks. He is a great lama, she said, when the house asked her. I am a cook. The twelve students paid her and were a little afraid of her.

The kitchen was at the back of the house, with a window that looked west at the first Flatiron. When the four came in with Thayer and the two dealers, there were five clean white dish towels already folded in a row on the oilcloth. The nurse said afterward that Ama-la had folded them at six, before anybody had heard of Thayer Vance.

Dolkar looked at Thayer’s robes for a while. Then she looked at Gil.

You have the crate, she said. Bring.

Danny went down and got it. She did not go through it. She put her hand into the rice straw five times and set one skull on each towel, the bare ones and the silver ones together, and did not look at the masking tape.

You are the boy who knocks?

Yes, ma’am.

She pointed at the first. Thayer tapped it with the nail of his middle finger, and the smoke color came up behind the sound, and he said, He went down. Into the fire. Or the cold, there’s a cold one too.

Good, said Dolkar, and clapped her hands once. Good.

The second was blue. An animal. Something heavy. It’s standing in snow.

Good. She clapped.

The third was yellow. A person again. A girl, I think. Somewhere hot. The fourth was white, and he said so, and each time she clapped her hands once and said Good, the way you would to a child doing his sums right, and Gil, by the stove, began to smile.

The fifth was a bare cup, brown at the rim, without silver. Thayer tapped it.

Nothing came up.

He tapped it again, harder. The sound was the same small dry sound as the others. Behind it there was no smoke, no blue, no yellow, no white, no red, no green. He picked it up, which he had not done with the others, and turned it in his hands and tapped it on the brow and the temple and the back, and held it to his ear like a shell. The sweat came out on his forehead and ran down. The kitchen was silent except for the refrigerator. The man in the field jacket had stopped pretending not to watch.

Let me make sure, Thayer said, and turned it again.

You don’t know, said Dolkar.

No. He put it down on the towel. I don’t know.

I know, said Dolkar.

Thayer looked at her. He was thinking, very fast, the way he had thought on the floor of the bus at two in the morning with the flashlight. Four colors he had carried since he was nine, and here was a fifth thing that was not a color, and an old woman in a cardigan who knew it. If he had that, there would be nobody in America like him. There would be nobody anywhere.

Teach it to me, he said.

Inside, I teach. Outside, no.

Inside what?

Refuge, she said. Then the things before. Then we see.

On the front steps he told Gil to give him a few days. Gil said ten. Danny said he would keep the robes clean. Thayer said, When I’ve got it, you won’t believe what we’ll get for the white ones, and he meant it, and they all three knew he meant it.

V. The Things Before

The back porch · July and August

There was a board on the back porch, sanded smooth, with a folded blanket at one end. It had been there since spring, the nurse said, and nobody in the house had known what it was for. Dolkar taught him the refuge in Tibetan, syllable by syllable, at the kitchen table, until he could say it without looking at her mouth, and then she showed him how to go down on the board, knees and hands and forehead, and slide forward full length with the arms out, and come back up, and say it, and go down again. She gave him a string of a hundred and eight bone beads to count on. When he asked how many, she said a hundred thousand.

He laughed. She did not.

There was one other thing. In the morning, she said, before you bow, knock on your own head. She showed him, on her own gray hair, with a fingernail, two taps, like a person at a door. Then say what is in it.

He knocked on his own head on the porch at five the next morning with the magpies going in the yard, and listened, and no light came up. Nothing at all. He tried again. It frightened him so badly that he went into the kitchen, where she was already lighting the stove, and told her it didn’t work on him.

Say what is in it, she said.

Brains.

More.

So he said it, and over the weeks she made him say it longer: the hair on the head, the hair on the body, the nails, the teeth, the skin, the meat, the strings that hold the meat, the bones, the inside of the bones, the kidneys, the heart, the liver, and on down through the body to the spit and the snot and the oil of the skin and the water in the joints, and back up again, every morning, before the first bow. She said the list was older than Tibet. He believed her.

Gil came by the porch on the fourth day and stood at the bottom of the steps in his sunglasses. You got it yet?

Wait a little, said Thayer, flat on the board. I’m learning it.

Gil came on the ninth day and again on the fifteenth. Danny came more often, with sandwiches from the Sink, and sat on the porch steps and watched Thayer go up and down and up and down with his lips moving, and after a while Danny would close his own eyes. The Naropa people came and went in the house. The twelve students stepped over him on their way to the garbage cans. On the ninth of August the radio on Dolkar’s windowsill said the President had resigned, and the house came down into the kitchen to listen, and Dolkar stood at the sink with her hands in the dishwater and said, So. Now another one, and went on washing.

It happened about the middle of the month. He would not have been able to say which morning. He had done something over six thousand prostrations by then, which was nothing, and his knees were raw under the jeans, and he had stopped writing poems because every line he started sounded like a card in a skull. He knocked on his head before the first bow and listened and it was the same as the fifth cup. He had been hearing that same nothing for a month and fighting it. This time he did not fight it. There was no light to go toward, and nothing in him went toward anything, and nothing was missing. He sat back on his heels on the board. The magpies were in the yard. The sun was on the first Flatiron, and he could see each pine on it. He sat there long enough that Dolkar came to the screen door, and looked at him through the screen, and went back in, and when he came into the kitchen there was a cup of tea at his place with the salt and butter in it the way she drank it, which she had never made for him before.

VI. Impossible

Mapleton Avenue · the end of August

The summer session ended. The vans along the creek pulled out one by one for California. Gil packed the bus for Santa Fe, where the man on Canyon Road wanted more white skulls for the winter trade, and pulled up in front of the house on Mapleton and leaned on the horn.

Thayer came down the walk with the two robes folded over his arm.

Well? said Gil. You get it?

Gil, I can’t go now. It’s impossible for me to go.

You get it or not?

It isn’t a thing you get. He put the robes through the window onto the seat. Sell the white ones for what they’re worth. Sell the smoke ones too. They’re all the same now. They were always the same.

Gil looked at him for a long time through his sunglasses. Then he said the old woman had got her hooks in him, the way they always did, and he put the bus in gear. Danny sat in the passenger seat with the robes on his lap and did not say anything, and looked back once.

In the kitchen that night the man in the field jacket was angry. His name was Walt Garza, and he had been sitting with Dolkar for two years, and he said it in front of everybody.

The kid’s lying to somebody. Either he told those two he’d come back with the goods, and he’s stiffing them, or he’s sitting here letting us think he’s got something. You heard him. They were always the same. Like he knows now. Nobody knows where the dead go, Ama-la. Not him. Not the Rinpoche. Nobody.

The tall woman, Marcy, agreed with him. She had come to Boulder in the spring for the Rinpoche, and stopped going to the talks after a party in June, and would not say more about it than that, and she had a low opinion of young men who claimed things.

Dolkar was drying a pot. No, she said. This boy does not talk like that. He knows now about the dying and the being born. All of it.

She put the pot down and took Marcy’s paperback off the windowsill, the same one Thayer had read on the floor in Weld Hall, and turned the pages without reading them, because she could not read English, until the shape of a page satisfied her. She put her finger on a place and gave it to Marcy, and Marcy read it out loud.

Thine own consciousness, shining, void, and inseparable from the Great Body of Radiance, hath no birth, nor death, and is the Immutable Light—Buddha Amitabha.

Knowing this is sufficient.

— The Tibetan Book of the Dead, Book I

Nobody said anything for a while. Walt Garza went out on the porch and sat on the end of the board. The nurse said later that three of them who were in the kitchen that night stayed with Dolkar until she died, and that Walt was one.

•••

In September, when the town was quiet again, Thayer went up to the university library to look for something in English that would tell him what had happened, and found on a high shelf a brown volume from the Pali Text Society, printed in London in 1913, called Psalms of the Brethren. It was the verses of the first monks, put into English by a woman. He read it standing up. Toward the end, in the prose a commentator had written to introduce one of the poets, he found a brahmin who, it said, won favour as a teacher by tapping on skulls with his finger-nail, and whose fellow brahmins saw in this a means of gain. He had to sit down on the floor between the stacks. The brahmin’s name was Vaṅgīsa, and he had been, afterward, a poet.

Drunk with divining art, of old we roamed
From town and village on to town again.
Then we beheld the All-Enlightened, Him
Who hath transcended all that we can know.

— Psalms of the Brethren 1253

He copied it out on an index card and walked back down to Mapleton in the dark.

Gil had left something on the porch rail the morning the bus pulled out. It was the fifth cup, the bare brown one, with a strip of masking tape on the bottom that said, in Gil’s hand, ??? — can’t sell. Dolkar had peeled the tape off and scrubbed it with salt. After that she set it on the kitchen windowsill every morning before the house was up and poured an inch of clean water into it, as an offering, and poured the water out on the tomatoes in the evening. Thayer, who swept the kitchen now and was the first one up, would bend down to it on his way to the porch. There was never anything in it but the water, and on the water, if he bent low enough, the morning over the Flatirons.

Background

The verses

Him I call indeed a Brahmana who knows the destruction and the return of beings everywhere, who is free from bondage, welfaring (Sugata), and awakened (Buddha).Dhammapada 419, tr. F. Max Müller (1881)
Him I call indeed a Brahmana whose path the gods do not know, nor spirits (Gandharvas), nor men, whose passions are extinct, and who is an Arhat (venerable).Dhammapada 420, tr. F. Max Müller (1881)

In the commentary, the brahmin Vaṅgīsa can tell where any dead man has been reborn by rapping on his skull, and other brahmins dress him in two red robes and tour him from town to town for fees. At Sāvatthi they quarrel with the Buddha’s followers over whose teacher is greater, and everyone goes to the monastery to find out. The Buddha sets out five skulls, of men reborn in hell, as an animal, as a human, among the gods, and of an arahant. Vaṅgīsa reads the first four, and the Buddha applauds each answer, but the fifth defeats him. Wanting the “spell,” he goes forth, is given the meditation on the thirty-two parts of the body, keeps telling his old companions to wait a little, and soon becomes an arahant. When he tells them it is now impossible for him to go, the monks think he is lying, and the Buddha answers that his son now knows all about the passing away and rebirth of beings. Vaṅgīsa became the order’s great improviser of verse. Read the full commentary story, or about Vaṅgīsa.

Boulder, 1974

Chögyam Trungpa, a Tibetan teacher trained in the Kagyu and Nyingma lineages, settled in Boulder in the early 1970s, founded the umbrella organization Vajradhatu in 1973, and in 1974 founded the Naropa Institute, whose first summer session drew more than 2,000 people. That summer Allen Ginsberg, Anne Waldman, Diane di Prima and John Cage were asked to found a poetics program, which Ginsberg and Waldman named the Jack Kerouac School of Disembodied Poetics, and Ram Dass taught a course on the Bhagavad Gita to more than a thousand students. Trungpa was a gifted and influential teacher who also drank heavily, often kept students waiting for hours, and was later the subject of serious accounts of sexual relationships with students and abuses of power, including the 1975 seminary incident involving the poet W. S. Merwin.

The Evans-Wentz edition of the Bardo Thödol, translated by Lama Kazi Dawa-Samdup and published in 1927 as The Tibetan Book of the Dead, was widely read in the American counterculture; it describes the dull lights of the six realms that draw the dead toward rebirth and the Clear Light that frees them. A kapala is a cup made from the crown of a human skull, used in Tibetan Vajrayana ritual, and Chöd, the “cutting” practice associated with the eleventh-century yogini Machig Labdrön, is traditionally done in graveyards and charnel grounds with a hand drum and a thighbone trumpet, the practitioner offering his or her own body in visualization. The verses of Vaṅgīsa and the commentator’s story of his skull-tapping appear in C. A. F. Rhys Davids’s 1913 translation of the Theragatha, Psalms of the Brethren.
