Walking the American WayCommentary 2.9 · Nigamavāsī Tissa

Salt Tea

Long years we have enjoyed the luscious fruit it bare,
And tho' it now has none, it still should claim our care.
— Jātaka 430, tr. H. T. Francis and R. A. Neil (1897)

Thus I heard it, on the sand roads off Route 9.

I. The Round

Freewood Acres, Howell Township, New Jersey · September 1957

Gelung Sanji ate once a day, before noon, and he ate it in Freewood Acres. He had done so for five years, and in that time he had not once eaten his meal anywhere else.

Freewood Acres was a few streets of small houses laid out on sand among scrub pine, on both sides of U.S. 9, which was one lane each way then, with egg farms beyond in every direction. There were Russians there already. The Kalmyks came in the winter of 1951 and 1952, off the transports from the camps around Munich, after the Attorney General in Washington had been persuaded that a Mongol people who had lived three hundred years on the Volga and the Don could be let in as Europeans. Most of the ones in Freewood Acres were Astrakhan people, who had come out of Russia in the war. A few houses at the east end belonged to Don Kalmyks, the old emigration, who had left in 1920 with the White army and spent twenty-four years in Belgrade. Sanji's mother lived in one of those houses. His mother's brother had the next, and a cousin the one after that, and across the road lived a widow named Kermen who had known him since he was baptized, as she put it, because she had lived so long among Serbs that she used their word.

The men built houses for contractors or worked at the rug mill in Freehold. The women sewed. On the lot behind the cousin's house they had put up a cinder-block room with a stovepipe and an altar, and that was the khurul for the east end. Sanji had a cot behind a curtain at the back of it. He was thirty years old. The other monks who had come over were old men, seventy and more, ordained on the steppe under the Tsar. Sanji had been a boy in the children's choir at the temple in Belgrade, the one with the pagoda roof on the street the Serbs named Budistička. He was a novice at fourteen, and seventeen when the community walked out of Belgrade ahead of the Red Army. He had taken his full vows in a barracks in Bavaria from three old lamas who did not expect to live to ordain anyone else.

He owned the robes, a rosary, four books, and a bowl. The bowl was birch root, turned on a lathe in Belgrade by a Kalmyk carter who made them in the winters, and the Baksha had put it into his hands the day he became a novice. It had cracked in the camp, in the cold of 1946, and a Latvian in the next barracks had closed the crack with a brass staple. A monk of his people carries his own bowl in the fold of his robe and holds it out to be filled. In six years in Bavaria it had been filled with barley soup from the kitchens of the refugee administration, and with turnips, and some days it had not been filled. He had learned in those years what he needed, and it had turned out to be less than he would have guessed.

His father had been an officer of Don Cossacks and afterward the head tailor of the colony, and in Belgrade the family had counted for something. Here his mother sewed sleeve linings for a coat factory in Lakewood, piecework, eleven cents the pair.

Every morning after the prayers Sanji folded his outer robe over his arm and went out on the sand road, and at one house or another a door opened. He never chose the house. Whoever saw him first called him in, and the woman of the house set before him the first of whatever was in the pot. That was the custom, the deeji, the first portion, which goes to the altar or to a monk. Most days it was Kalmyk tea, brick tea boiled in the kettle with milk and salt, with a lump of butter melting on top and a little nutmeg if there was nutmeg. With it came bortsak, the fried dough, and if a sheep had been bought from a farmer that week there was budan, the soup, with meat in it. He ate what was set down. He took no second bowl of anything but tea. If there was meat, he ate meat, and on the days the woman of the house apologized for the pot, he ate that with the same attention and at the same speed. When he had eaten he said the blessing for the house, and he went back to the cinder-block room and did not go out again to see whether some other pot had something better in it.

The rest of the day he kept the lamps and said the offices. On Saturdays he taught nine children to read Kalmyk in the old upright script, which their parents could no longer read themselves.

II. The City

North Philadelphia · the same autumn

The larger part of the Don Kalmyks had settled in Philadelphia, fifty-five miles down the road, in the row houses north of Girard Avenue. There was steady work there in the needle trades and the cigar and candy factories. The principal temple was there, on the ground floor of a row house, and the old lamas lived above it. The head of them was the Baksha, Lidji, who was eighty-one, and who was an emchi, a doctor of the Tibetan medicine, which he had learned as a boy on the Don from a man who had learned it in Lhasa.

People came up the stairs to him all day. He took a wrist in three fingers and listened to it with his eyes shut, for as long as another man would take to read a letter, and then he said what was wrong and went to his jars. He had brought the jars from Belgrade through the camps wrapped in his bedding. He had lost the high lama's cushion on the way, and his books of ritual, and he said that he could manage without those. He had been Sanji's teacher from the day the boy was brought to the temple school at seven. He was not a talker. In twenty-three years Sanji had heard him say perhaps four things that were not about medicine or the order of a service, and remembered all four.

The Americans had found the Kalmyks in Philadelphia. The Friends had helped bring them over and did not forget them, and a professor from the University came with a tape recorder. And there was Mrs. Thayer.

Mrs. Thayer was a widow with a house in Chestnut Hill. In 1924 she had gone with her husband to Peking and Kalgan and out onto the Mongolian plateau on a camel, and nothing in her life before or since had come up to it. When she read in the Bulletin that there were Mongol lamas living on North Tenth Street, she drove there the same afternoon. After that the gifts did not stop. She bought every monk new robes of real Chinese brocade from a dealer in New York. She gave the temple an oil furnace and a refrigerator, and a Buick for the lamas to be driven in. At the New Year and at each of the great days there was an envelope for each monk with a hundred-dollar bill in it. And on feast days she sent a caterer's truck to North Tenth Street with whole roasted lambs, and rice, and oranges in crates, and five kinds of cake, and came herself and sat on a cushion among the old women and was entirely happy. The Kalmyks, who had seen charity of several kinds in several countries, agreed that this was a kind they had not seen. They called her the Khatun, the queen, without much mockery in it.

The old monks went to her table in their brocade. They sent word to Freewood Acres each time, and each time Sanji sent back his respects and stayed where he was.

It began to be talked of. Gelung Dordji said it first, and then they all said it. The young one is busy enough up there. He sits in his mother's kitchen like a schoolboy home for dinner. It's his uncle today and his cousin tomorrow. A monk leaves his family, and this one has found a way to leave them and keep them. The Khatun asks for him every time, a monk who can read, a young man, and he can't come fifty miles. What is it he has up there that is better than his own teachers' company?

They said it finally to the Baksha, who was grinding something in a mortar and did not stop.

III. Three Bowls

Freewood Acres · October and November

That autumn the work went. The whole country slid into a recession, and the rug mill in Freehold cut to four days and then to three and let the newest hands go, and the newest hands were Kalmyks. The contractors stopped starting houses. The egg farmers, who had been getting poorer for three years, quit buying anything. In the east end of Freewood Acres four families of the old emigration packed and went to Philadelphia, where a cousin could speak for them at the candy factory, and their houses stood with paper over the windows.

Sanji went out on the sand road every morning as before, and what was in the pots changed.

There had been no budan since September. First the butter went out of the tea, and after that the nutmeg. By November his mother's tea had milk in it only because she gave him the milk and drank her own black. He saw that and said nothing, since there was nothing to say that would not shame her. He ate her bortsak, which was made now with oil saved from the last frying, and told her it was the bortsak of Belgrade.

On a morning in the middle of November it was the widow Kermen who opened her door. She was seventy-four and lived alone on what a son sent from Philadelphia when he had it. He set his bowl on her table. She brought the kettle and poured, and what came out of it was brick tea and salt and water. She had no milk. She set a plate beside the bowl with two bortsak on it from some earlier day. They were hard enough to knock on.

She stood by the stove with her back to him, and he saw that her shoulders were moving.

Emgen, he said, which is grandmother. Sit down. Is there more in the kettle?

There's nothing in the kettle. It's water.

It's tea. Pour me another.

He drank three bowls, slowly, and he soaked the bortsak in the third until it could be eaten and ate both. Then he said the blessing for her house, the long one, all of it. She said afterward that he drank that salt water as if it were the tea of a khan's tent, and that she had looked hard at his face for the politeness in it and had not found any. He had sat there like a man who had what he wanted.

That same week the Buick came up Route 9 with Gelung Dordji in the back seat. There was to be a dinner at Chestnut Hill. Dordji stood in the cinder-block room in his brocade and looked at the cot and the curtain.

There are sixty families in Philadelphia and a furnace in the temple, he said. Here there are twenty houses and half of them can't feed themselves. What are you living on?

I ate this morning.

What?

Tea.

Dordji went back to the city alone. The next word that came from North Tenth Street was from the Baksha, and all it said was to come.

IV. The Question

North Tenth Street · the week before Zul, December 1957

He went down on two buses with his outer robe in a paper bag, because of how people looked at it. The second bus came into the city past miles of row houses with lit windows, and stores, and a factory with its night shift going in at the gate carrying lunch pails. He looked at all of it with interest, as he would have looked at a river in flood, and he felt no wish to stay in it. The monks were all in the upper front room. The Baksha sat by the window on a kitchen chair with a blanket over his knees. He was a very small man. His hands smelled of the roots he ground.

Sanji made the three prostrations and sat on the floor.

They tell me, said the Baksha, that you live up there in the middle of your family. That you are in and out of your mother's house and your uncle's house all day, and that this is why you never come to us, with everything that is given here. Is it so?

No, Baksha.

The old man waited.

I go out once in the morning, Sanji said. Somebody calls me in. Some days it is my mother and some days it is a house where I am no relation. I take from them what I can eat at that sitting. When I have had what will keep me that day, I go back and shut the door. It may be coarse or it may be fine. I have had enough, so why should I go looking for more? I am not with my relations, Baksha. I spend less of the day with my mother than Gelung Dordji spends with Mrs. Thayer.

One of the old monks laughed and turned it into a cough.

And when the houses had nothing? the Baksha said. This autumn.

They never had nothing. There was tea.

The Baksha looked at him for some time. Then he said, Good, and after a moment he said it again. Good.

He turned his head toward the others. You wonder at him. I don't wonder. He had teachers who lived that way, and I was one of them, before I got old and people began bringing me refrigerators. It is the oldest habit in this robe. It is older than the robe.

Gelung Dordji, who was an honest man and already ashamed, asked him to say how it was older than the robe. So the Baksha told it. He told it in Kalmyk, as it is told to children, with his hands lying still on the blanket.

V. The Parrot

A fig grove on the Ganges · long ago

There was a grove of fig trees on the bank of the river Ganga, under the snow mountains, and so many parrots lived in it that when they rose the sky went green. Each had its tree. The king of the parrots had his, and he ate its figs, and drank from the river.

A year came when the figs on his tree were finished. He ate the shoots, and when those were gone he ate the leaves and then the bark. He drank from the river and sat in his tree, and he was content. He was so entirely content that up in his heaven the seat of Khormusta, the king of the gods, grew warm under him. Khormusta looked down to see who on earth had that much merit. He saw a green bird in a bare tree.

Khormusta thought he would see how deep it went, and he dried the tree. It died where it stood. It became a grey stump full of holes, and when the wind blew, the stump moaned and dust came out of the holes. The other birds had long since gone to trees with fruit on them. The parrot ate the dust. He drank from the river and sat on top of the stump in the wind and the sun, and he went nowhere.

Then Khormusta took the shape of a wild goose and came down with his queen and perched on a branch nearby. Bird, he said, there are green trees up and down this river, heavy with figs. I have just flown over them. Why do you sit on a dead stump eating dust?

And the parrot said:

Long years we have enjoyed the luscious fruit it bare,
And tho' it now has none, it still should claim our care.

— Jātaka 430, tr. H. T. Francis and R. A. Neil (1897)

The others left, the goose said. Were they wrong? There is nothing here.

They loved it for its figs, said the parrot. I ate its figs as long as any of them did. Now it has dust, and I find I can live on that. Why would I go?

Then the goose was a goose no longer, and the parrot saw who had been speaking with him. Ask me for something, Khormusta said. Whatever you want most.

The parrot might have asked for any tree on the river, or for a heaven. He said, Let me see this one in leaf again.

Khormusta took up water from the Ganga in his hand and threw it over the stump. The tree stood up green from the root, with shade under it, and its figs were sweet as honey. He went back to his heaven. The parrot stayed in the tree.

•••

That bird was our Teacher, Burkhan Bagshi, the Baksha said, before he was a man, when he was only on the way. So you see where the habit comes from. Anyone will stay for figs. To stay for the tree, he has to be awake to what he is doing every day, and afraid of the day he stops. A monk like that doesn't fall. There isn't far for him to go.

Nobody in the room spoke. Half the men there had been born on the Don and had seen their villages emptied, and the rest had come from the Volga, where in the last week of 1943 the whole Kalmyk nation was put on cattle trains for Siberia in four days and its name was taken off the map. In January of that year the Russian paper from New York had carried four lines saying that the name had been put back. None of them knew what to make of that, and each of them was thinking of it.

VI. Zul

Philadelphia and Freewood Acres · the middle of December

They kept him for Zul, the feast of lamps, when every Kalmyk becomes a year older on the same night. The women had kneaded dough and shaped it into small boats, and in each boat they set wicks of grass stalk wound with cotton, one for each year of the person's age and one over, and filled it with melted butter. When it was dark the lamps were lit and carried up and set before the altar. There were more than a hundred of them. Mrs. Thayer's lambs had come that afternoon. She sat among the old women holding a dough lamp with sixty-eight wicks, which she had counted out herself.

The Baksha was helped down the stairs. He did not go to the high seat. He stopped in front of Sanji, who was at the end of the row of monks, in his cotton robe among the brocade. He said a verse over him in Tibetan, and then in Kalmyk, slowly, so that the people behind could hear it. It is in the book of the Teacher's sayings that the Kalmyks have from the Tibetans, who have kept it a thousand years.

The Bhixu who delights in purity and who looks with dread on impurity is so near nirvana that he cannot fail (to reach it).

— Udānavarga 4:30, tr. W. W. Rockhill (1883)

The word they have put as purity means taking care, the Baksha said. It is how a man carries a full bowl.

Sanji had heard that verse since he was a boy in the choir in Belgrade. He had sung it. Standing in the row with a hundred lamps in front of him, he heard it as a thing said about a particular morning, the morning of the salt tea, when he had looked into the bowl and found nothing in himself that wanted it to be otherwise. He had not known then that there was anything to notice. He saw now that the wanting had gone out of him some time ago without his marking the day. No dinner in Chestnut Hill could bring it back, and no empty kettle could either. He stood very still. The old monks said afterward that his face in the lamplight was like the face of a man who has set down a load at his own door. Gelung Dordji, beside him, took off his brocade outer robe and folded it and laid it on the floor, and stood the rest of the night in the cotton one underneath.

Mrs. Thayer asked to have the young lama presented to her. He came and thanked her for the furnace, which kept old men warm, and he ate at her feast exactly what he would have eaten in his mother's kitchen. He took a piece of lamb, because it was put before him, and bortsak, and tea. In the morning he went north on the two buses.

•••

He lived in the cinder-block room for thirty-one years more. The mill went back to five days in the spring and then, some years later, shut for good, and the east end found other work, as it always had. Nobody else from those houses moved to the city. The old women said you could not leave a place that a monk like that would not leave, and they said it as a complaint.

The morning he came back from Philadelphia it was snowing a little on the sand road. The first door that opened was the widow Kermen's. She had milk that week, her son having sent five dollars, and she had been to the store for butter and stood now with the kettle in her hand, and she watched his face as she poured to see what it would do.

It did what it had done in November. He held out the bowl with the brass staple in it, and she filled it, and he lifted it in both hands and drank.

Background

The verse

A Bhikshu (mendicant) who delights in reflection, who looks with fear on thoughtlessness, cannot fall away (from his perfect state)—he is close upon Nirvana.Dhammapada 32, tr. F. Max Müller (1881)

In the commentary, a young man of good family from a market town near Sāvatthī becomes a monk and is known as Tissa of the Market Town. He goes for alms only in the village where his relatives live and never comes to the city, though the great donors there are giving lavishly. The monks complain that he lives entangled with his kin. Questioned by the Buddha, Tissa says he takes from them only what he needs to eat, coarse or fine, and seeks nothing more. The Buddha applauds him, says that contentment was his own habit too, and tells the birth story of the parrot king who would not leave his fig tree even when the god Sakka withered it to dust, and who asked, when offered any gift, only to see the tree in leaf again. Then he speaks the verse. Read the full commentary story. The parrot's couplet here comes from the 1897 Cambridge translation of the Jātaka; the verse the Baksha recites is the same Dhammapada verse as it stands in the Udānavarga, the collection preserved in the Tibetan canon that Kalmyk and Mongolian Buddhists inherited, in W. W. Rockhill's 1883 translation from the Tibetan.

Freewood Acres, 1957

The Kalmyks are a western Mongol people, Tibetan Buddhists, who have lived on the lower Volga and the Don since the seventeenth century. Some hundreds left Russia with the defeated White armies in 1920 and settled in the Balkans; in Belgrade they built a temple in 1929, one of the first Buddhist temples in Europe, and left it in 1944. Others came west during the war. On December 28–31, 1943, the Soviet government deported the entire Kalmyk population, more than 93,000 people, to Siberia and abolished their republic; a Kalmyk Autonomous Oblast was re-established by decree on January 9, 1957. The Kalmyks stranded in displaced persons camps near Munich were at first barred from the United States as Asians. In 1951 the Attorney General's office ruled them admissible, and most arrived between December 1951 and April 1952, sponsored by the Tolstoy Foundation, Church World Service and the Society of Friends.

They settled mainly in north-central Philadelphia and in Freewood Acres, on U.S. Route 9 in Howell Township, New Jersey, where their temples still stand. Fred Adelman's 1961 article "The American Kalmyks" describes the two colonies, the Philadelphia factory jobs, the New Jersey men's work in housing construction and "until the recent recession, a carpet factory," the butter tea, the fried bortsak and the budan soup, and a senior monk who was a doctor of Tibetan medicine. The recession of 1957–58 is the one in this story. Zul, the lamp festival in early winter, is the Kalmyk new year of age. Sanji, the Baksha, Mrs. Thayer and the temple on North Tenth Street are invented, and the east-end khurul is not any of the real Howell temples.
