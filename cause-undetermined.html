II. Recorded Statement

Shrikant Joglekar, taken at the kitchen table · Tuesday, September 15

Please state your name and occupation for the tape.

Shrikant Vishnu Joglekar. I am a process engineer at Argonne. Not a scientist. I want that on the tape. I make the equipment do what the scientists drew.

Tell me about Saturday, Mr. Joglekar. Take your time.

Saturday was my father’s shraddha. The yearly rite for the dead. He died in Pune last September, on this tithi, this day of the moon, and we could not go. Two tickets were twenty-one hundred dollars and our daughter had just started high school. So this was the first year. My wife had cooked since Thursday. You have to feed Brahmins at a shraddha. The Brahmins eat and the fathers are fed. My mother always had four. My wife wanted four.

Brahmins meaning priests.

Meaning men of that caste who know the rites. In Pune you send a boy down the lane. Here there are perhaps eleven in the whole western suburbs, and in September every one of them is at a wedding in Schaumburg. So Saturday morning she sent me to the mandir. The temple. It is in the old Lutheran church on Seventy-Fifth Street, they bought it in 1984, you have seen it, there is still the cross-shaped window. I went to the office and said to the volunteer, Mr. Pandya, I need four Brahmins for a shraddha today, venerable ones, and he looked in the hall and said, I have four.

And who were they?

Four boys. Seven years old. They had their munja in August, the thread ceremony, all four together on one morning, so now they are Brahmins, technically, with the thread. They were in the hall waiting for the Saturday class. Mr. Pandya said, They are bored, take them, feed them, bring them back by four. I said, Pandya-ji, my wife. He said, They are Brahmins or they are not Brahmins? Which one?

So I took them. They sat in the back of the Oldsmobile with the seat belts across all four, like that. Very quiet boys. One had a Cubs cap. I remember I thought, I will tell her they are the sons of very learned families.

What happened at the house?

You should ask her.

I will. I’m asking you.

She had set the living room. Four good chairs from the dining set, with her shawls over them, the silk ones from our wedding, and four banana leaves we bought frozen on Devon Avenue, and the silver cups. She saw the boys come up the walk and she went into the kitchen and said to me in Marathi, You have brought four little children to feed my father-in-law. Not even old enough to be your grandsons. She took the shawls off the chairs. She put down four of the brown folding stools from the garage, along the wall by the television, and said to them very politely in English, Sit there, please. And they sat. Then she told me, Go back and find me a real Brahmin.

Did you?

I went back and found Dixit-guruji, the head priest, coming out from the noon arati. I said, Guruji, please, my father’s shraddha, come. He came. He walked in and saw four boys on garage stools with nothing in front of them and he asked, Have these Brahmins eaten? My wife said, Not yet, Guruji, please sit. He said, They are seated. It was cooked for four. Give me my bag, and he took his bag off the hook and went home.

My wife said, He did not want to eat. He has a stomach. Go and bring another.

So I went for Joshi-buwa. He is a kirtankar from Nashik, visiting for the season, staying in the basement of the Kulkarnis in Naperville. A very famous voice in Maharashtra. He came because he had known my father’s name. He looked at the boys and said the same thing as Guruji. Exactly. Have these Brahmins eaten. And took his bag.

You must understand it was after one o’clock by then. The boys had come at nine for class. They had eaten nothing because a Brahmin eats nothing before a shraddha. They were sitting on the stools along the wall with their hands in their laps. Nobody had told them anything. The one in the Cubs cap had taken it off.

My wife said, These swamis do not want to eat with children. Go to the other temple, the South Indian one out by the tollway. Bring one old man. One. With a beard, if possible. I will cook more.

And you went.

I went. In front of that temple there is a bench by the parking lot where the old men sit. Fathers who have come from India to stay with sons and have no car and nothing to do. On the end of the bench, the best end, under the tree, was a very old man in a white dhoti and an old brown cardigan. A white beard to here. I said, Grandfather, it is my father’s shraddha, will you come and eat. He said, Yes. He got in the car.

I do not want to say more for the tape.

Mr. Joglekar, the company only needs to know how the openings occurred.

Then the company should ask him.

IV. Statement of the Insured

Vasanti Joglekar, handwritten, eleven pages · Wednesday, September 16

Mrs. Hajek, you asked me to write it because I could not say it into the machine. I will write all of it. My husband is a good man and he left out the part that is mine.

My father-in-law, Appa, I lived with him for nineteen years in Pune before we came here. He taught me to drive a scooter. He ate my food every day for nineteen years and said every day, Salt is a little less, and every day the salt was correct. When he was dying they phoned us at three in the morning and the line was so bad I could hear the sea in it. We did not go. We had the money, really. We did not go because of tuition and because of the cost and because we told each other next year. So this was the first shraddha, and I thought, this one at least I will do correctly. The book says the fathers come and sit near the Brahmins when they are seated. Appa was going to come and sit in my living room. I wanted to give him the best chairs.

Then my husband brought me four children.

I was so angry I could feel it in my teeth. My mother used to say that kind of anger is like salt thrown on the coals, it spits, and I was spitting. I put the children on the garage stools. I did not give them water. A Brahmin must not take food before the rite but water they can have, and I did not give it, because I did not want them to think they were the Brahmins. That is the thing I did. Nobody did it to me.

Then Guruji came and went, and Joshi-buwa came and went, and both of them said the same words, and I did not hear it. I thought they were insulting my cooking.

The old man came at two. When I opened the door I was so happy. A beard, a dhoti, old, old. Like a man from a picture of my grandfather’s village. I took the two good shawls off the chairs and put both on one chair for him, the green and the gold, and I said, Please, Grandfather, sit here.

He walked past the chair. He went to the four boys on the stools and he went down to the carpet in front of them, knees and hands and forehead, the full namaskar, the five limbs on the floor, to children. Seven years old. One by one. The one without the cap started to laugh and then stopped. Then the old man sat down cross-legged on the carpet at the end of the row, lower than the stools, beside the television.

I said to my husband, You have found one old enough to be your father and he is bowing down to boys young enough to be his grandsons. Is he mad? What use is he? Put him out.

My husband took him by the shoulder. Then by the arm. Then around the waist. He could not move him. He is not a big man, Mrs. Hajek, the old man, he was thin, I could see his ribs through the cardigan, and my husband could not move him. So I took one arm and my husband took the other and we pulled him. I hit him on the back. I want to write that down plainly. I hit an old man on the back with my open hand, twice, three times, and we dragged him across my carpet to the front door and out onto the step, and I shut the door.

When I turned around he was sitting in the living room at the end of the row of boys, cross-legged, exactly where he had been. He was moving his hands in the air a little, back and forth, the way you would wave to a child in a car going away.

Our daughter came down the stairs at that moment and saw him and screamed, and I was already screaming, and I telephoned the police.

Then he told us who he was.

My husband will not say the name to you. I will. It was Indra, the king of the gods, whose seat is in heaven. I do not expect the company to write that on the form. When he said it I saw what he was, for as long as it takes to breathe in, and he had eyes. All over him, the arms, the chest, the cardigan, a thousand eyes, like the little mirrors sewn into my wedding shawl when the lamp is near it, and every one of them was looking at the four boys on the garage stools.

I told the police we did not need them.

Then I served. I put the boys on the dining chairs, all of them, with the shawls, and the old man on the carpet where he wanted to be. I served the rice and the varan and the kheer and the vade and the five vegetables, and the boys ate like boys who had had nothing since the night before, and the old man ate a little. Then I went out on the deck with the rice balls for the crows, which I had put on the railing at noon, and which no crow had come near all morning, though the crows are in every tree on Juniper Court. Mrs. Hajek, they came down while I was standing there. Six of them. One walked up and took the first ball before I was even back inside. I sat down on the deck steps and cried until my daughter came out.

When I went in they were leaving.

I will tell you only what I saw. The first boy went up. Up through the ceiling of the stairwell and the attic and out through the little window in the gable, the louver; you have seen it, the slats are pushed out, not broken in. The second went out through the front of the roof and the third through the back. The fourth one, the smallest, with the Cubs cap in his hand, went down. Through the carpet, through the floor, through the basement floor. There is a hole in the concrete by the furnace you could put a bucket through, and no concrete in the basement, none, no dust. And the old man went out by some way of his own. I did not see. The chain was still on the front door.

I want to say two more things.

The boys were not angry. I watched their faces the whole day. I am a mother and I know a child’s anger in the back of the neck. They were hungry, and they were bored, and the small one wanted to laugh, and they were not angry with me for one moment, and that is the thing I cannot sleep for.

The other thing. The book says the fathers come and sit near the Brahmins when they are seated. So Appa came on Saturday. He came all the way. And I put him on a garage stool against the wall by the television, with nothing, not even water, for five hours. And he did not mind. He sat with the boys. Salt is a little less.

Please pay what is correct. I do not need the hole in the basement filled.

VI. Letter

Vasudha Phadke, graduate student, University of Chicago · Monday, September 21

Dear Mrs. Hajek,

Mr. Pandya at the temple gave me your number and said you would like a statement from the boys, since they were present. Their parents have asked me to answer for them, because I teach the Saturday class and they are seven. I am a graduate student, not a priest. I teach the boys their sandhya, their morning and evening prayers, how to say them and roughly what they mean. I can tell you what they told me and what happened at class.

They came back to the hall at ten past four. Their names are Aniket, Mihir, Rohan and Sameer. Sameer had a Cubs cap. They were fed and happy and a little sleepy. They did not seem to think anything unusual had happened, except that the lady had given them the dining chairs after a while, and the kheer was very good, and an old man had bowed to them, which they found funny.

The older boys in the class, who are twelve and thirteen, had heard by then from somebody’s mother about the stools, and the five hours, and the two priests walking out. They asked the little ones, Weren’t you mad at her? And all four said no. And the older boys came to me together and said, Vasudha-tai, they are lying. Anybody would be mad. Five hours on a garage stool, no water. They are only saying it because a Brahmin is not supposed to get angry, and that makes it a lie on top of the anger.

I should tell you that I am the wrong person to have answered them. The book I teach from, which their fathers would like them to respect, would not have let a woman teach them anything, and I have had to make my peace with that sentence by sentence. But I have read it closely for six years and I know where things are in it. So I sat them down, the big ones, and I read them two places.

The first was about age. The old Brahmins in the book grew angry once because a young boy named Kavi had taught them and called them little sons, and they went to the gods to complain, and the gods said the child had spoken correctly. And then a little after that:

A man is not therefore (considered) venerable because his head is gray; him who, though young, has learned the Veda, the gods consider to be venerable.

— Laws of Manu II.156, tr. Georg Bühler (1886)

The second is from the chapter for the man who has left everything and goes about with a bowl. I told them it was the hardest instruction in the book and that I had never once in my life managed it for a whole day.

Let him patiently bear hard words, let him not insult anybody, and let him not become anybody’s enemy for the sake of this (perishable) body.

Against an angry man let him not in return show anger, let him bless when he is cursed …

— Laws of Manu VI.47–48, tr. Georg Bühler (1886)

I said, You think it is a rule they are obeying, so they must be lying about the anger underneath. It is not a rule for them. Look at them. They are not holding anything down. There is nothing there to hold. That is what the verse is describing, a person it is simply true of, and you have never met one, and neither have I, and now four of them are sitting in the back of this hall eating Mrs. Joglekar’s vade out of a foil pan, and you would rather call them liars. That is all right. I would have too, at thirteen.

The one called Rohan asked me afterward why the old man bowed to them. I said I thought he had been waiting a long time for somebody to bow to. He said, In the Cubs cap? I said, Especially in the Cubs cap.

I am sorry I cannot help you with the cause of loss.

Sincerely, Vasudha Phadke

Background

The verse

Him I call indeed a Brahmana who is tolerant with the intolerant, mild with fault-finders, and free from passion among the passionate.Dhammapada 406, tr. F. Max Müller (1881)

In the commentary, a brahmin’s wife cooks for four venerable brahmins and sends her husband to the monastery to fetch them; he is given four seven-year-old novices who are already arahants, Saṁkicca, Paṇḍita, Sopāka and Revata. Furious, she sets them on low seats and sends him for elders. Sāriputta comes, sees the novices unfed, says they ought to be fed first, and leaves with his bowl; Mahā Moggallāna does the same. The husband then brings an old brahmin from another sect, who is Sakka, king of the gods, in disguise. He bows to the novices with the five limbs and sits on the ground beside them; the couple beat him and drag him out, but he is still sitting in the same place. When he makes himself known, they serve the meal, and the four novices leave through the gable, the front and back of the roof and the earth, while Sakka goes another way; the place is called the House with the Five Openings. The monks doubt that the novices felt no anger, and the Buddha answers with the verse. Read the full commentary story, or about Indra, the god Buddhists call Sakka.

A shraddha in the western suburbs, 1987

A shraddha is the Hindu rite for dead ancestors, held on the anniversary of a death by the lunar calendar and in the fortnight of the ancestors each year. In many families invited brahmins are fed as the ancestors’ guests, and rice balls (pinda) are set out for crows; a crow taking the offering is widely read as a sign that the ancestor is satisfied. A boy of a brahmin family becomes eligible to take part in rites after his upanayana, the sacred-thread ceremony, called munj in Marathi; the Laws of Manu set the brahmin boy’s initiation in the eighth year after conception, so a newly initiated boy is often seven. Manu’s chapters on the student and on the shraddha (II and III) supply the scripture of the story, in Georg Bühler’s translation for the Sacred Books of the East (1886), with its well-known lines on caste and on women that the teacher in the story has to read past.

Indian immigration to the United States grew quickly after the Immigration and Nationality Act of 1965 ended national-origin quotas, and many of the engineers, doctors and scientists who came settled in suburbs such as Woodridge in DuPage County, near Argonne National Laboratory and the research corridor along the East-West Tollway. Through the 1970s and 1980s these families built their first temples in houses and in churches bought from shrinking congregations, and the Hindu Temple of Greater Chicago, which had met in homes and rented halls in Downers Grove and Romeoville, opened its temple at Lemont in July 1986. The temple, the Joglekars, the boys and their teacher in the story are fictional.
