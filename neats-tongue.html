Neat’s Tongue

I Sayd, I will look to my wayes,
lest I sin with my tongue.
— Psalm 39:1, Bay Psalm Book (1640)

As I am credibly informed, at Springfield upon Connecticut River.

I. Flesh

Springfield upon Connecticut River · July 1691

Elias Cole killed beasts for fifty-five years. He came up the river at fifteen in the year the town was planted, when it was still called Agawam and there were still Indian cornfields on the meadows, and somebody put a knife in his hand that first winter and he never set it down. He killed for the families who could not bring themselves to do it and for the ones who could not spare the day. He killed the fat oxen and the barren cows in November, and hogs by the hundred, for the salt barrels that went down the river on the flatboats to Hartford, and from Hartford to Boston and the sugar islands, where they fed men he would never see. He was paid in wheat, in wampum when there was wampum, in shillings when there were shillings, and always in the best piece of whatever he had killed, which he carried home himself under his arm.

He ate it with his wife and his son at his own board and sold the rest. He would not eat without flesh. Samp, hasty pudding, rye-and-Indian bread, pease, the sallet greens his wife grew by the door: none of it would go past his teeth unless there was meat on the trencher beside it. The town said Elias Cole would starve to death in a cornfield.

The meetinghouse stood across the lane from his door, and the minister’s house beside it. In fifty-five years Elias Cole never gave the minister so much as a spoonful of anything. He paid the minister’s rate because the town would have taken his cow if he had not, and not a penny over it. In December, when the other men carted the minister his winter wood, Elias sat on his doorstep whetting his knife and watched the sleds go by. He went to meeting because the law said he must, and sat on the back bench with his hat on his knees, and slept.

He had married late, the second time, a girl named Mercy from Windsor who was nearly twenty years younger than he was, and they had one child, a boy, born two springs before the war, and she named him Return. She had one book, her mother’s psalm book, printed at Cambridge in the Bay the year she was born, and she taught the boy his letters out of it. He could read the psalms before he could read anything else. He knew them the way he knew the smell of the yard behind the house, the salt and the blood and the flies.

On a hot afternoon in July, in the year 1691, Elias sold the last of a heifer from the tail of his cart, gave Mercy a piece of the brisket to boil for his supper, and went down the bank to the river to wash the blood off his arms, as he did every evening in summer.

While he was gone Goodman Hale came to the door. He had lived three lots down for thirty years and was the nearest thing Elias had to a friend. His wife’s brother had come down from Hadley without warning, with two boys, and there was nothing in the house but samp.

Let me have a little of the beef, Mercy. I’ll pay him tomorrow.

There’s none to sell. He sold the last of it at the cart and he’s gone down to wash.

Don’t send me off. If there’s a piece in the house, let me have it.

There’s not a piece in the house but what he set by for his own supper. You know he won’t eat without it.

Goodman Hale stood in the doorway a while, thinking. If that was Elias Cole’s supper, then Elias Cole would never give it to him, not for money, not for Hadley, not for anything. So he stepped past her to the board where the brisket lay salted for the kettle and took it up in his two hands and went off down the lane with it.

Elias came up from the river with his shirt over his shoulder. Mercy set before him a dish of samp with purslane and a little vinegar, which she had made as well as she knew how.

Where’s the flesh?

There is none, husband.

Did I not give you a piece of brisket before I went down?

She told him. Hale, and the brother from Hadley, and the two boys, and the brisket going down the lane in Hale’s hands.

Then I’ll not eat. Take it away.

What’s to be done, Elias? Eat the samp.

I will not.

He made her take the dish off the board. Then he took his knife down from the peg and went out the back.

His ox was tethered in the yard behind the house, an old red ox called Broad that had drawn his cart for eleven years. Elias walked up to it and put his hand into its mouth and pulled out its tongue and cut it off at the root. Then he came back into the house with it and laid it on the coals and turned it with the knife until it was done, and had Mercy bring the samp back, and laid the tongue on top of it, and sat down to his supper.

He ate a spoonful of the samp first. Then he cut a piece of the tongue and put it in his mouth.

In that moment his own tongue split in two and fell out of his mouth into the dish.

That is how Goodwife Hale told it, who had come up the lane to say her husband was sorry and saw it through the open door. That is how Mercy told it, until she died. Blood came out of him in a stream onto the board and down his shirt. He stood up and knocked the bench over and went out into the yard and down on his hands and knees in the dirt, and he crawled, and he bellowed. He bellowed like an ox. Out by the fence the ox was bellowing too, with its mouth full of blood, and you could not tell the one sound from the other.

Return was seventeen. He stood in the back door and watched.

His mother took him by the arm. Son, look at him. Look what’s come down on him. That is the hand of God, and it’s on this house, and it will come down on your head too if you stay under this roof. Don’t stay for me. Go. Go now.

She put her mother’s psalm book in his hands, and three pine-tree shillings out of the crock on the shelf, and pushed him out the front door into the lane, and shut it.

He went east on the Bay Path that same night, walking, and did not stop until it was light.

Elias Cole crawled about his yard for three days, bellowing, and the whole town came to look at him over the fence. The minister came across the lane and prayed over him in the dirt. The selectmen talked of presenting him at the county court, for the colony’s old Body of Liberties had said, fifty years before, that No man shall exercise any Tirranny or Crueltie towards any bruite Creature which are usuallie kept for man’s use. But on the third day he died, and there was nobody left to present. The ox died the same night. A minister in Boston who collected such things heard of it the next year and wrote it down among the remarkable judgments of God upon sinners.

II. The Ring

Boston · 1691–1699

It is ninety miles by the Bay Path and Return walked it in five days. He slept in barns and once in a hollow log. He ate what women gave him at back doors, and when they asked whose boy he was he said he was going to his uncle in Boston, and he had no uncle in Boston. He did not open his mouth for anything else.

Boston smelled of tar and fish and the sea, and it had more people in it than he had known were in the world. He went up and down the streets near the Town House for a week, sleeping under a wharf, and on the seventh day he stood in front of a goldsmith’s window watching a man inside work the bellows at a little furnace, and the man came out and asked him what he wanted.

The goldsmith was Isaac Lowde. He needed a boy. He asked if Return could read, and Return took the psalm book out of his shirt and read him the first psalm that came, and Lowde nodded and asked whose boy he was, and Return said nothing at all.

Well, said Lowde, a boy who can’t be made to talk is a boy who won’t tell the customers what’s in the strongbox. And he bound him for seven years.

The next summer they hanged people at Salem for things a great deal less than what Return had seen, and he heard the men in the shop talk about it over the counter, the spectres and the marks on the body, the hand of God, the sins of the fathers, and he kept his head down over the bench and said nothing. He learned to work the bellows and to keep the charcoal at the right red. He learned how the dross rises on the silver in the crucible and how you blow it off and blow it off until the metal lies clear as a mirror and you can see the roof beams in it. He learned to raise a porringer out of a flat disk with the hammer, to draw wire, to chase and to engrave. In the third year Lowde set him to rings.

When a man of any substance died in Boston, his family gave rings to the mourners: plain gold bands with black enamel let into them, and on the outside a little death’s head or a coffin or a skeleton lying down, and round the hoop the name of the dead and the date and the words MEMENTO MORI. A judge in the town was known to have a drawer full of them from the funerals he had walked in. A rich man’s widow might order forty.

One morning in the fourth year Lowde went out to Roxbury on business and told Return, at the door, that a merchant’s widow on the Mill Creek wanted twenty rings for Thursday. Here were the names and the date. A death’s head on each, and the motto. Cut the first one and I’ll look at it tonight.

When Lowde came home after dark there were twenty rings in a row on a square of black cloth by the window, finished, the enamel set, every skull grinning the same small grin, every letter clean. Lowde picked one up and held it to the candle and turned it for a long time, and put it down.

Wherever this boy goes, he said to his wife that night, he’ll eat.

When the seven years were out he gave Return his daughter Hannah.

III. Forty Years

Boston · 1699–1736

Return Cole kept a shop of his own for nearly forty years. He had three sons and two daughters and he named the girls Mercy and Hannah. He made porringers and spoons and tankards and the clasps for Bibles and the buckles for shoes, but mostly he made rings, because he was the best in the town at rings and everyone knew it. He did not keep count. Hannah once tried to reckon it from the books and stopped somewhere above three thousand. Three thousand little skulls. Three thousand times he cut MEMENTO MORI round a gold hoop with a graver no bigger than a darning needle, and never once in all those years did he read the words.

He was civil to everybody and spoke to nobody. He went to meeting because the law still said he must, and sat on the back bench with his hat on his knees. He never owned the covenant. He never gave the minister anything beyond his rate. When Hannah asked him why not, he said the minister had a salary, and went back to his bench. His children learned the catechism at their mother’s knee and recited it to him on Sunday evenings, and he listened with his eyes on the fire. When they asked about their grandfather he said He was a butcher. He died. When they asked how, he said Suddenly.

He ate meat when it was set in front of him and did not want it. He would not have a tongue in the house, of any kind, salt or fresh. Hannah thought it was a fancy. She never knew.

Once, in the hard winter of 1717, when the snow came up over the doors in Boston and men walked to meeting on top of it, a customer told in the shop how a man in the country had cut his beast’s throat in a rage and the beast had lived and walked about bleeding, and everybody laughed at the stupidity of it, and Return put down his graver and went out the back and stood in the snow in his shirtsleeves until Hannah came and brought him in.

The sons grew up and learned trades. Isaac was a cooper. Thomas was a joiner. Joseph, the youngest, went to sea for two voyages and came home and said he would rather have land. There was land to be had up the country, in the river towns, more than there was in Boston, and in the year 1728 the three of them went up the Bay Path together with their wives and took up lots in a new precinct on the Chicopee, a few miles above Springfield, and set up their households there. Return did not go to see them off. He did not tell them their grandfather had lived and died a few miles from where they were going. They did not know it until much later.

In the winter of 1734 and 1735 the awakening came down the river from Northampton. It went from town to town that winter like a fever, people said, but a fever that left them better, and it came to the new precinct on the Chicopee, and Isaac and Thomas and Joseph Cole were among those it found. They wrote to their father that spring, and Isaac wrote again in the summer. Return read the letters at his bench and put them in the drawer with the ring blanks and did not answer.

Hannah died in October of 1736. After the funeral, at which he gave away twenty-two rings of his own making, Return went back to his bench the next morning and sat there, and did not light the furnace.

Up on the Chicopee, his sons talked it over by the fire. Our father is an old man now. He’s alone. He has nobody to get his dinner. So in January Joseph came down the Bay Path with a horse and a sleigh, and loaded the old man’s tools into it in their chest, and the psalm book, which had come apart at the back, and drove him up to the river.

IV. The First Day

The new precinct on the Chicopee · January 1737

He was sixty-two years old. He had walked down that road at seventeen and he was carried up it at sixty-two in a sleigh under a bearskin, and it took four days, and he did not say anything the whole way except about the cold.

His sons had talked something else over by the fire. Their father had never in his life done one thing for God that he was not made to do by law. He was old. Soon he would go where they could not follow him, and he would go with nothing. They could not give him a heart. But they could do what was in their power, which was to keep a feast in his name, as people do for the poor, and let him have whatever good there was in it.

So they asked the minister to dine at Isaac’s house on the Thursday, and the deacons, and every soul in the precinct who could get there through the snow.

The minister was a young man named Jonas Thorne. He was twenty-six, Harvard College, and he had been ordained in the new meetinghouse only in October, when the frame was barely closed in. He was thin, and he had a cough that winter that people worried over. He was not a great preacher. He read his sermons in a light voice with his eyes down. But in the year he had been on the Chicopee he had sat up with every sick person in the precinct, and he could not walk the length of the road without being asked in.

The women of the three houses cooked for two days. There was a goose, and a boiled dish of pork and cabbage and turnips, and pease, and rye-and-Indian bread, and an Indian pudding that had been in the oven all night, and pumpkin, and dried-apple pies, and cider. They put the old man at the head of the board in Isaac’s chair with his back to the fire, and twenty people ate at two tables, and the children ate standing.

When the meat was done and the boards were cleared, Isaac stood up.

Mr. Thorne. This dinner is given in our father’s name, that whatever good is in it may be his. Will you return thanks for him.

The minister did not stand. He turned on the bench so that he was facing the old man at the head of the table, and looked at him for a while, and spoke to him as plainly as if they were alone.

Mr. Cole. You are an old man. You are a leaf in November that hasn’t let go of the branch yet, and it will. They’re waiting for you. You know that better than I do; you’ve made the rings. You’re going on a journey and you have laid up nothing for it. Not a barrel. Not a biscuit. He coughed into his sleeve and waited until it passed. Get yourself a refuge. When the river comes up in the spring, every man on the meadows makes for the high ground and doesn’t stop to finish his supper. Make for the high ground, Mr. Cole. Be wise. Don’t be a fool.

Then he asked for a psalm book, and somebody brought him the old man’s, from the chest, because it was nearest, and he opened it at the ninetieth psalm and gave the line to the deacon, and the deacon lined it out, and the two tables sang it after him, a line at a time, in the slow old way, with the children standing at the wall.

Threescore & ten yeares are the dayes
of our yeares which remaine,
& if through strength they fourscore be,
their strength is grief & paine:
For it’s cut off soone, & wee flye
away: Who is’t doth know
thine angers strength? according as
thy feare, thy wrath is so.
Teach us to count our dayes: our hearts
so wee’l on wisdome set.

— Psalm 90:10–12, Bay Psalm Book (1640)

Return Cole did not sing. He sat with his hands on the board. He had one ring on his own hand, his wife’s mourning ring, which he had made in October with her name round it, and in the middle of the psalm he looked down at it, the way a man looks down at a thing he has been carrying for so long he has stopped knowing it is there.

MEMENTO MORI, it said. He read it.

It was not much. A man in the meadows looking up, once, at the sound of water. But his daughter-in-law, who was watching him, said afterward that she saw his face change at the board like a field when the shadow of a cloud goes off it, and that he sat there after the singing with his eyes open and wet, and did not wipe them, and let the children see.

V. The Second Day

Isaac Cole’s house · the next day

The sons did not ask him what had happened. That night they asked Mr. Thorne to come again the next day, and he came, through new snow, with his cough worse. There was not so much to eat the second day, because they had eaten most of it the first, and it did not matter. This time the brothers said it plainly, before the meal and after it: this is for our father, Mr. Thorne. Not for us. For him only.

So when they had eaten, the minister turned again on the bench to the old man.

Mr. Cole. You are very far along in years. You are come almost to the door. Between here and there, there is no inn on the road, no house to put up at, no place to stop and get your breath. And you have nothing in your bag. He was quiet a while. You know how they take the dross off silver. You know it better than I do. Blow it off. Blow it off again. Quickly, while there’s fire. Get up on the high ground, Mr. Cole, and there’s a place where the water doesn’t come.

Then he reached for the old man’s psalm book, which had been left on the shelf since the day before, and held it shut on his knee. He had sat up with it half the night, his landlady said afterward. He said the words from memory, in the old book’s own wording.

Heare my pray’r, Lord, hark to my cry,
be not still at my tears:
for stranger, & pilgrim with thee,
I ’me, as all my fathers.
O turne aside a while from mee,
that I may strength recall:
before I doe depart from hence,
and be noe more at all.

— Psalm 39:12–13, Bay Psalm Book (1640)

As all my fathers, said Return Cole.

It was the first thing he had said at either table. Everyone turned. And then the old man began to talk, in a low voice, the way water begins to come over a dam that has held for a long time, a little at first, at one low place, and then all along the top. He told them about Springfield. He told them about the house across the lane from the meetinghouse, and the knife on the peg, and the salt barrels going down the river. He told them about Goodman Hale and the brother from Hadley and the piece of brisket. He told them about the ox, whose name was Broad. He told them all of it, at the table, with the children standing at the wall, and his sons heard for the first time what had happened to their grandfather, a few miles down the river, forty-six years before. He told them that he had walked to Boston to get out from under it, and that he had been waiting for it ever since, every day, at the bench, with his tongue held tight against the roof of his mouth so it would not fall. That he had been waiting for it to come down on his head.

It did not come down, said Mr. Thorne.

No.

It was never going to come down on you, Mr. Cole. It was his. All you had to carry up this road was your own.

The minister had given him the whole of it, the way a father puts a whole spoonful into his own mouth to show a child how. The old man took the piece of it that fit his mouth, the way a child does. It was enough. His sons said afterward that it was like watching the ice go out of the river in April, all at once, after a long time of nothing, with a sound like a shot. The people at the second table said they had their own good of it too, and some of them were in the church by spring.

•••

That night Return Cole went out to Isaac’s barn in the dark with a lantern. Nobody saw him go but his granddaughter Hannah, who was nine, and who followed him in her stocking feet across the snow and stood in the barn door.

Isaac kept two oxen, a brindle pair. The old man hung the lantern on a nail and stood in front of the near one for a long time, and the ox looked at him, and breathed on him, two long plumes of white in the cold.

Then he held out his hand, open, palm up, the way you hold out salt.

The ox put out its tongue and took his hand into it, rough and warm and wet, the whole of his palm and his fingers and his wife’s ring, and licked it, slowly, over and over, and he stood there and let it.

The verses

In the commentary, a cow-killer of Sāvatthī will not eat his rice without meat. When a friend carries off his supper, he cuts the tongue out of a living ox, and as he eats it his own tongue splits and falls into the dish; he crawls about his yard bellowing like an ox until he dies. His terrified son flees to Takkasilā, is apprenticed to a goldsmith, marries the master’s daughter and lives out his life without a single good work. His sons, who have become followers of the Buddha, bring him home in his old age and give alms in his name two days running, and each day the Buddha thanks the old man with two verses, telling him he is a withered leaf with no provisions for the journey and must make himself an island. Springfield and Boston, 1636–1737

Springfield was founded in 1636 by the Puritan fur trader William Pynchon as Agawam Plantation, on land bought from the Agawam people. The Pynchons traded furs, grain and salt pork; pork packed in hogsheads went down the Connecticut River and, in time, as far as the West Indies. In 1675, during King Philip’s War, most of the town was burned. The colony’s first law code, the Massachusetts Body of Liberties of 1641, included the clause Elias Cole broke, often called the first animal-protection law in America. The psalms in the story are quoted from the Bay Psalm Book, printed at Cambridge in 1640, the first book printed in British North America, in its own spelling (with the long s modernized); eleven copies of that first edition survive.

In Boston, goldsmiths made the gold mourning rings that families gave out at funerals, decorated with skeletons, coffins and winged death’s heads in the memento mori tradition: remember that you must die. Return Cole arrives the year before the Salem witch trials and lives through the Great Snow of 1717. The awakening that reaches his sons began in Jonathan Edwards’s Northampton congregation in 1734 and spread to some twenty-five towns of western Massachusetts and central Connecticut before it waned in 1737, a forerunner of the First Great Awakening. The new precinct on the Chicopee, its minister and the Cole family are fictional.
